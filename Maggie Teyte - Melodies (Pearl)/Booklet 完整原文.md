**MAGGIE TEYTE – Mélodies**

Debussy himself greatly admired the English soprano Maggie Teyte: "She does not just *sing* Mélisande", he commented, "*she is* Mélisande". Of particular interest are the fourteen songs made with Cortot in 1936 (their only collaboration) and the rare private set made for America in 1940, where her partners were Gerald Moore and the conductor Leslie Heward, who was to die so tragically only three years later.

### Inner Page – Maggie Teyte Biography

**MAGGIE TEYTE**

It seems extraordinary that the role of the heroine in Debussy's one opera *Pelléas et Mélisande* was taken at the première (given, strange though it at first may seem, at the Opéra-Comique) by the British singer Mary Garden, and that her immediate successor should have been another from the same land, Maggie Teyte; both enjoyed the approval of the composer.

Born in Wolverhampton on 17 April 1888, (d. 26 May 1976, Westbury-on-Trent, Derbyshire) Margaret Tate (she later changed her surname to avoid a most unfortunate mispronunciation when it was uttered in French) Maggie; she set on a musical career from the beginning. She studied piano at the Royal College of Music, nudged her way forcefully into a choral tutu rather than had a foot in both London and Paris, and was by 1908 under the tuition of Jean de Reszke. After two years she signed a contract with the Opéra-Comique, and this in turn led to her being sent to Debussy for coaching in the role of Mélisande; her appearance was an immediate success, and she was to continue to be associated with his music for the rest of her life.

In a broadcast of reminiscences made by the BBC in 1958, Teyte talked about working with Debussy, his exacting standards, and how he knew how to achieve them. "I *will* have Mélisande *as I want it*!" he exclaimed. [More detailed comments on the composer's artistic ideals may be found in the great-nephew's excellent biography "The Pursuit of Perfection", Gary O'Connor, Gollancz, 1979]. For the broadcast Cortot sent a recorded message from Lausanne, recalling with pleasure the *arte entente* which he and she had shared during the 1936 Debussy recordings. He mentioned in passing an interesting detail concerning his playing of the left hand accompaniment in *Le Faune*: to enhance the illusion of the location of the flute a drum, a piece of paper was placed between the strings of the bass of the piano; the sonority thus achieved is stunningly appropriate.

Had it not been for the enthusiasm of Teyte's American friend Joe Brogan, whose idea it initially was, this collection would probably never have been recorded. Having no ivy Victor, or with HMV's Walter Legge, the tenacious New Yorker were direct to the latter organisation's Fred Gaisberg, who was delighted with the idea and suggested Cortot – who accepted – as accompanist. It so happened that the pianist was already booked for some concerts in March of that year. Slight rearrangements were made, and that month not only did Cortot accompany Teyte in these songs, but also played the Debussy Preludes Book I.

**Alfred Cortot** (b. Nyon, Switzerland, 26 September 1877 – d. 11 June 1962) was one of the greatest pianists of this century, and was held in high esteem by Debussy. Somewhere Debussy is quoted as having commented on his style of trying to capture the inspiration of a fleeting moment and translating it into notes on a musical stave: from something intangible and imprecise one must make something tangible and definite. Cortot and Teyte, marvellously, invert the procedure in these interpretations of his songs.

### Inner Page – Recording Background

The onset of war precluded the possibility of further collaboration with Cortot, who had accepted the post of Minister of Culture in the Vichy puppet government, but Teyte's recordings of French art-song were continued in London. The additional titles here included were all made at EMI's studios between 1940 and 1942.

In 1940, the indefatigable Joe Brogan, encouraged by the sales in his New York Gramophone Shop of the set of Teyte/Cortot recordings which had recently been being through his endeavors, commissioned a private set of further recordings for his own use exclusively – though he later permitted their release in Britain through the specialist shop Rimington van Wyck, which had already entered this field two years earlier with a set of Oda Slobodskaya in Russian art song. [Pearl GEMM CD 9021]. Now the accompaniment was taken over partly by the London Symphony Orchestra under the distinguished English conductor Leslie Heward (1897-1943) and partly by Gerald Moore (1899-1988), already regarded as Britain's foremost piano accompanist. After this further recordings of French song were to be made by Teyte and Moore, for EMI. The Brogan private set tracks, here making their first complete, are denoted by the issue prefix G.

### The Songs – Detailed Notes

**DEBUSSY**

**1-3. FÊTES GALANTES – Set 1**

Mutual an evocation of an idealised, twilight paradise. *Moonlight*: The beloved's soul is likened to a pleasant landscape, where all manner of hard things are taking place. *Phantoms*: In this surrealistic scene Scarlatti-like harlequins jest and sundry other unrelated characters, go about their various business.

**4-6. FÊTES GALANTES – Set 2**

The innocents are young ladies, inexperienced, somewhat clumsily dressed, espied in the, made of terracotta, and lives in the middle of a bowling-green, he predicts a bad fortune for the two of us. The Sentimental colloquy takes place in the once deserted park, where two ghosts recall the rapture that was once theirs.

**7-9. TROIS CHANSONS DE BILITIS**

Pan's flute is made of reeds and is used, figuratively, to assist the lover in his evening courtship. The tresses of the beloved were in a dream – he tells her – entwined around him so firmly that he might never go free. The tomb of the naiads a girl mourns the frosty wounds, following their bodement: they are naiads as those of a satyr. "No, they belong to a deer" explains her young kinsman. "Satyrs and naiads are dead, and let us say that here is their tomb."

**10-12. LE PROMENOIR DES DEUX AMANTS**

By this calm grotto the singer may relax and breathe pure air. Climène, bear my plaintive loves the lover, and pause awhile in this idyllic place. I tremble when I see you yonder, could it be that you are lost, her wonders.

**13. Ballade des femmes de Paris** is a mercilessly self-congratulatory paean, referring to the beauty of the language as heard spoken in Paris, as opposed to the great cities of other countries of Europe – rather a difficult companion to appreciate.

**14-17. PROSES LYRIQUES**

*Dreaming*: Fancies flit through the mind of the dreamer – in the night, old trees think of the lovely girl who has just passed by, but they cannot speak to her; now she is gone, and they are powerless. Night is as tender as womankind. *Shore*: An evocation of the seashore where twilight descends. White and green iridescent silk... the downpour, then all is still again. *Flowers*: The unhappy lover feels that even the flowers mock him by reminding him of his absent beloved. In his "greenhouse of sorrow" he curses the very sun that gives them life. *Evening*: It is Sunday, and the happy diversions of the populace are contrasted with the lover's sadness, and bitterness in the recollection of the wonderful moments with his sweetheart that will be never repeated. The Blessed Virgin lets fall the balm of slumber – let it come quickly!

**18. CINQ POÈMES DE BAUDELAIRE – No. 3**

The Fountain: An impressionistic piece of poetry, hard to put into a prose reduction. It is set in a courtyard with a fountain, where are found two lovers; the imagery includes flowers, moonlight, the fiery lightning of desire and vast, enchanted skies.

**DUPARC**

**19. Invitation to a journey:** In this evocation of what is apparently some distant Italy, Baudelaire is in fact referring to Holland. "Think of the bliss of going together to a land where all is beauty, ordered measure, luxury, peace and sensuous pleasure."

**20. Phidylé:** It is midday, and the landscape of Arcadian beauty and happiness in the beloved. Awaken when the stars appear, my love, and your smile shall be my reward.

**BERLIOZ – NUITS D'ÉTÉ**

**21. (No. 1) The rose's ghost:** A young girl is visited in her sleep by the ghost of a rose she wore at a ball. "You plucked me, beloved, and now I am a spirit garden, so causing my death, and I am here to haunt you: I can never return. This gentle perfume is my soul: I come from paradise, and my end has been a worthy one."

**22. (No. 4) Absence:** Come back to me, my love! Like a flower without sunlight, my spirit withers without the warmth of your smile. O, say my lot that we are apart – come back!

**FAURÉ**

**23. Après un rêve:** An evocation of masked figures of the Italian Commedia dell'arte in a nocturnal fête champêtre that Watteau might have painted. An elegant pianist provides the accompaniment.
