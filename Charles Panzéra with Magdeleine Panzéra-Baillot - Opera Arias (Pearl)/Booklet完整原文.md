## 第三页 / Inner page 1 (HIPPO_20241217_0003.jpg)

**CHARLES PANZÉRA**

Many great musicians have come out of Switzerland but most have belonged to the German wing. On the other hand, a vital handful have been right at the centre of French musical affairs; and such was the case with Charles Panzéra, born in Geneva on 16 February 1896. He studied with A.L. (Amédée-Louis) Hettich - whose other star pupil at the time was Madeleine Grey - at the Paris Conservatoire but, eschewing traditional Swiss neutrality, interrupted his studies to volunteer for war service in the French army. After being wounded twice, he resumed his place at the Conservatoire and in 1919 carried off the Prix d'Excellence. He was immediately offered a contract by the Opéra Comique and made his debut that very year as Albert in Massenet's *Werther*. During three years at the house he also appeared as Jahel in Lalo's *Le Roi d'Ys* and Lescaut in Massenet's

*Manon*; but his most celebrated role was Pelléas in Debussy's *Pelléas et Mélisande*. This relatively high baritone-Martin part he continued to sing on and off until 1930, also offering it in Amsterdam and from Florence; and when highlights from the opera were issued in 1928, he was Pelléas to Yvonne Brothier's Mélisande. No less a critic than Herman Klein of *The Gramophone* pronounced: 'I have never heard Debussy's realistic musical colloquies sung with greater delicacy, with greater purity of diction, or with a fuller measure of vocal charm. In some respects, indeed, the work of Mlle. Panzéra excels that of the original interpreters.'

At the Conservatoire Panzéra had met the pianist Magdeleine Baillot, almost exactly six years older than himself, and they formed a team to perform the exacting repertoire of mélodies, hitting it off so well that they married almost as soon as he had secured his future. There

has not been a better pairing in the entire history of the lied and the mélodie; indeed the Panzéras started at the top, with the dedication to Charles of Fauré's last song cycle, *L'Horizon Chimérique*. Being a pupil of A.L. Hettich did Panzéra no harm, as Hettich knew all the composers Fauré, Honegger, Ibert, Ravel and Roussel each to contribute a vocalise-étude to a volume he published in 1906. Fauré even lobbied unsuccessfully in 1919 for Hettich to be admitted to the Légion d'Honneur. Written in the autumn of 1921, *L'Horizon Chimérique* was premiered by the Panzéras at a Société Nationale concert on 13 May 1922. Even more significantly, the pair took part in the concert at the Sorbonne in honour of Fauré; and Charles made his orchestral concert debut under the Lamoureux Orchestra under Camille Chevillard. The French arm of HMV, La Voix de son Maître, immediately signed

up the couple and in 1924 one of the great joint recording careers was launched.
The delay in making their first records was due to the huge demand for tours and appearances that literally descended on the Panzéras. Beginning with more than 120 recitals in 1923, they became familiar figures in the concert halls of Europe and North America. In addition Charles had his occasional operatic appearances and his concerts with orchestra to fit in. He became especially identified with the music of Honegger, participating in the premières of *Le Roi David*, *Amphion*, *Cris du monde* and *La Danse des morts; he also took part in the premières of Milhaud's *Les Euménides* and Guy Ropartz's *Les beaux prophètes* and *Le rêve sur le sable*, as well as works by Albert Roussel and Vincent d'Indy. He called his country house La Chanterie. After the Second World War he taught at the Juilliard School in New York before taking up a professorship at the Paris

Conservatoire, where he influenced a generation of students between 1949 and 1966. His publications included *L'art de chanter; L'amour de chanter; L'art vocal (50 leçons de chant); 6 Leçons de chant: Votre voix (directives générales); and Vocalises d'étude. He died in Paris on 6 June 1976.

Charles Panzéra had a voice perfectly suited to concert work and the lighter operatic repertoire. In his younger days he had the high A for singing Pelléas and after about 1930 his tone settled slightly, so that he gained at the bottom of the range what he lost at the top and could encompass the bass-baritone register. His middle register was always warm and relatively rich for the baritone of this type, with a vibrato reminiscent of another great concert and recording baritone, Emilio de Gogorza [GEMM CD 9089]. Much has been made of a 1929 critique by Ernest Newman complaining of the smallness of Panzéra's voice; but Newman was accustomed to

baritones who specialised in the German repertoire - and although Panzéra sang lieder beautifully (his *Dichterliebe* with Cortot at the piano was perhaps the most poetic ever recorded) he came to them from the more delicate French repertoire. No other critic complained of not being able to hear Panzéra and although his records do not suggest the tone of a Maurice Renaud or a Michel Dens, they do not suggest the minuscule volume of a Pierre Bernac either. He recorded in four languages, always enunciating perfectly and always placing his tone precisely; but one of the great pleasures of hearing him sing is listening to what he does with the French language. Suddenly the vowels and consonants which Maurice Chevalier and grotesque de Gaulle find their true medium. Yes, French is indeed a beautiful language when it is sung like this.

The present programme suggests the range of Panzéra's sympathies,

apart from the Fauré, Debussy, Ravel, Duparc and Honegger with which he is most often associated. The baroque items are gravely and beautifully done and Mozartean style - even if this servant sounds aristocratic enough to be Don Giovanni himself. Curiously, in the Don's champagne aria Panzéra sounds too deliberate, perhaps fazed by conductor Piero Coppola's tempo. He is a suave Mephistopheles in Berlioz's *Damnation de Faust* and an ideal Wolfram in Wagner's *Tannhäuser*; and he gives us a rarity in Saint-Saëns' lovely Victor Hugo setting.

The lieder need little comment, although it is worth noting that Panzéra himself wrote the French words for *Der Doppelgänger*. Its original coupling, *Erlkönig*, was one of two celebrated French records of the orchestral version - but whereas the heroic tenor Georges Thill had the assistance of a bass and a boy soprano, Panzéra managed all the voices himself.

TULLY POTTER

## 第五页 / Producer's note and tracklist (HIPPO_20241217_0005.jpg)

**Producer's note**

Few would deny that Charles Panzéra was the most distinguished interpreter of French mélodies during the inter-war years. His honeyed tones and exquisite use of the French language allowed of no rivals. He seems to me to mirror the dichotomy between Gerhard Hüsch and Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau in the German field of song - with Pierre Bernac cast in the Fischer-Dieskau role. Both earlier singers had more sensuous voices; both post-war singers had, perhaps, a deeper understanding of their texts. All of

this is a matter of opinion; none of it detracts from the greatness of them all. Panzéra = Mélodies. This CD seeks to show that he was far more versatile than that. We offer baroque French opera, arie antiche, German lieder - and an exuberant Saint-Saëns song, Schubert with an exciting Berlioz orchestration - and more besides! (It has to be admitted that his Bach *My heart ever faithful* is remarkably plodding).

Once again, thanks to EMI Archives for providing recording dates.
