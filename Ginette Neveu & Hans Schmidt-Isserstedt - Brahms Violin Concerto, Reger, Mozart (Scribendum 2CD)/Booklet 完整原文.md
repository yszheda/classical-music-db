## Brahms Violin Concerto

The recording of Ginette Neveu's exemplary interpretation of the Brahms Violin Concerto in the Hamburg Musikalle is the only surviving one of a live performance by the great violinist. Instead of the sterile atmosphere of a studio here we can enjoy a feeling of communion with an audience – which, though anonymous, conveys something of the excitement of a concert hall, that the listeners on that particular evening experienced a feeling of warmth, fundamental truth, and transcendence can be judged from three press comments from newspapers issued the following day. The print of these post-war papers is almost illegible; for the whole story of a world that was falling apart had to be crammed on to one or two pages. Yet they report with an intensity and spontaneity that leave us in no doubt as to the quality of Ginette Neveu's performance: on Wednesday May 5th, 1948, the praise of Heinz Joachim, critic of DIE WELT knew no bounds: "The performance of the Brahms Violin Concerto bore the hallmark of the great French violinist Ginette Neveu, comprising musicality, virtuosity and brilliance, magnificent passion. She was justifiably acclaimed with overwhelming enthusiasm and acclamation." The same day (Wednesday May 5th, 1948, Issue No. 36, Year 3 of the HAMBURGER FREE PRESSE the critic "E. K." wrote: "The Parisian violinist Ginette Neveu gave a flawless performance of the Brahms Violin Concerto, with adroitness, virility, and extraordinary precision of tone. Here is a musician who occupies a foremost place among the leading violinists of the world. The long ovations that she was accorded came straight from the heart." The write-up in the HAMBURGER ECHO (No. 37, Year 73, Saturday 8th May) was even more effusive: "Ginette Neveu, the young French violinist, indisputably of international calibre, played the Brahms Violin Concerto with a consummate genius, the key to which lies in concentration and absolute control. She performed it with an unsurpassed capacity for graphic declamation combined with rare musical passion and feeling for rhythm."

## Brahms Violin Concerto Op. 77 - Analysis

Brahms began work on the Opus 77 concerto in 1874 and completed it during the summer of 1878 while staying in Portschach. The work was written at the instigation of the famous violinist Joachim, to whom the work was subsequently dedicated and who watched the progress of the work closely, advising particularly in respect of the virtuoso passages. The concerto received its first public performance on January 1st 1879 in Leipzig, with the composer conducting and Joachim as soloist.

The work consists of three movements:

**I. Allegro non troppo.** A grand orchestral tutti in the manner of Beethoven introduces the three main themes of the movement: (i) right at the beginning a melodious theme in stark contrast to (ii), which is rhythmic (quaver, semiquaver and crotchet) and willful in character. Between these, four subsidiary ideas are inserted. At bar 206, in the course of the solo entry, a third main theme appears, (iii), which is immediately taken up by the violins and violas. In the development section tutti passages alternate with virtuoso, technically extremely difficult soli, passages in true classical manner. A traditional recapitulation follows, interrupted by a cadenza. The coda closes the movement in an emphatic D major.

**II. Adagio**, in ternary form. The extraordinarily delicate theme of the A section is stated first by the oboe before it is taken up by the solo violin and freely developed. The faster moving, more fiery B section is principally entrusted to the soloist. Finally the A section returns again with the blissful, indescribably poetic opening mood.

**III. Allegro giocoso ma non troppo vivace.** This vigorous, high-spirited movement has a definite Hungarian flavour about it. It is in free rondo form with the soloist introducing the rondo theme, which is subsequently taken up by the wind section. The three episodes display both virtuosity and expressiveness. After a short cadenza comes the coda, using mainly the rondo theme.

Although the Brahms Violin Concerto was slow to gain recognition, today it heads the repertoire of all great violinists.
