## Notes on Mozart's Overtures and Die Entführung aus dem Serail

Mozart's gem-like overtures perfectly set the stage for the operas that follow them, but they are equally brilliant and satisfying when heard on their own.

*Die Zauberflöte*, a Singspiel, was Mozart's final opera. Its overarching theme is idealism. It conveys that theme in a very human way, without preaching, and also with comedy - and some not inconsiderable sadness. The overture, composed last, is among Mozart's most intricate. It opens with three solemn chords, a significant number, given the opera's association with Masonic rites and philosophies. Also, the key signature is E flat major, a key with three flats. These chords set a bustling fugue into motion, although, in the middle of the overture, Mozart returns to those solemn chords as if to remind us that, at its core, *Zauberflöte* is a serious work.

*Le nozze di Figaro* (The Marriage of Figaro, 1786) is based on a comedy by Pierre Beaumarchais - the sequel, if you will, to *The Barber of Seville*, which later was turned into an opera by Rossini. Count Almaviva, a charming protagonist in Rossini's opera, becomes a darker figure in Mozart's. Tired of Rosina, come back in disguise, and each begins wooing the other's beloved. Initially, the women protest, but their lovers' unconditional trust in them is soon shown to have been misplaced. When the ghost of Donna Anna's father comes to call on the hell-bound nobleman. The rest of the overture has a drive and ruthlessness that suggest Don Giovanni himself. Again, the overture came last; Mozart is said to have composed it the night before the opera's premiere! Mozart's breathless overture to Figaro lasts a little more than four minutes. Again, it prepares the audience for an evening of comedy, but not for the opera's emotional complexity. Despite that, it is as close to perfection as music can come.

Although Count Almavira was not past redemption, the titular nobleman in *Don Giovanni* (1787) never repents his many sins, even as he is dragged down into the fiery underworld. At the start of the opera, he kills the father of Donna Anna, a woman he has just tried to seduce, and his scorecard of atrocities mounts as the opera goes on. Like *Così*, *Don Giovanni* is classified as a dramma giocoso - an essentially serious work with some funny bits. Much of the giocoso element comes from Giovanni's suffering manservant Leporello, who, early in the opera, reads from the massive catalogue of his master's sexual exploits: 640 women in Italy, 231 in Germany, 100 in France, 91 in Turkey, and a whopping 1003 in Spain! The overture opens with powerful chords and foreboding music. These reappear near the end of the overture in the compact Allegro. Even when executing a simple task, Mozart rarely took the easy way out.

*Così fan tutte* can be translated as 'That's How All Women Are'. The plot hinges around a cynical bachelor who puts two young male acquaintances up to testing the faithfulness of their respective girlfriends. The young men pretend to go off to war, but immediately return in disguise, and each begins wooing the other's beloved. Initially, the women protest, but their lovers' unconditional trust in them is soon shown to have been misplaced. Lest one accuse Mozart of misogyny, the next two overtures are taken from operas that put men in an unflattering light - specifically men who abuse their positions of power in search of sexual conquests.

*Der Schauspieldirektor* (The Impresario, 1786) is performed less frequently, partly because it is a one act opera - too short to perform on its own - and lacking the depth of the other operas represented here. Perhaps it would gain in popularity if it were renamed 'Dueling Divas', as it is about two sopranos, Madame Herz and Mademoiselle Silberklang. Each feels that she is more deserving of top billing and a more generous salary than her rival is. Vogelsang, a tenor, tries to mediate, but to no avail, and the opera closes with the three singers and Buff, an actor, all agreeing that 'Every artist strives for glory'.

During his short lifetime, Mozart composed 22 operas. Many of the early ones are infrequently performed, however, and, with *Idomeneo*, *Die Entführung aus dem Serail* (The Abduction from the Seraglio) was the first to gain a firm foothold in opera houses. It also was the first of Mozart's operas to be premiered in Vienna, the city which had recently become his home. The composer himself conducted its first performance on 16 July 1782. The Viennese public heartily embraced the new opera, but while it made Mozart famous, it did not make him rich: he received a flat fee of only 100 ducats for the opera, and no royalties.

The opera was commissioned by Gottlieb Stephanie, director of what was called the 'National Singspiel'. During this period, Europe was crazy for Italian opera, and the Singspiel was intended to demonstrate that homegrown operas were not inferior. Singspiels were presented in the German vernacular, and, to advance the plot, naturalistic spoken dialogue was inserted between the musical numbers. Seen in that light, *Die Entführung*'s success was not surprising. It was an opera that had intentionally been made accessible to a larger audience. Another popular element was Mozart's use of instruments - triangle, cymbals and a bass drum - that conferred a Turkish flavour to the music. One can hear this flavour almost immediately in the overture.

Stephanie was also the opera's librettist. One of the unusual things about *Die Entführung* is how involved Mozart was in the preparation of the libretto - or rather, the libretto's revision, as Stephanie was working from a libretto, originally penned by one Christoph Friedrich Bretzner for a Singspiel that recently had been performed in Berlin. Mozart wrote to his father that Stephanie was preparing the libretto exactly according to Mozart's specifications. Consequently, the characters gained depth and richness. For example, Osmin, the seraglio's comically wicked overseer, plays a more prominent role in Mozart's opera than he does in the work performed in Berlin.

It is entertaining to note that the opera's principal heroine, Konstanze, shares her name with that of the woman whom Mozart would marry later in 1782. And, in a sense, Mozart's marriage to Constanze Weber had the air of an abduction to it, as her family initially did not approve of the idea, and there even was talk of sending the police around to Mozart's lodgings to break up the couple's premarital domesticity.

Another unusual thing about *Die Entführung* is that one of its most important characters never opens his mouth to sing. Pasha Selim, the master of the seraglio, is entirely a spoken role. The reasons for this are unclear; perhaps there was no suitable singer in Stephanie's company for such a role. Nevertheless, Selim makes a deep impression because, as the opera draws to a close, he acts contrary to period stereotypes. Instead of vengefully punishing the four young lovers for trying to escape, he magnanimously allows them to leave - although he has just learned that Belmonte is the son of his enemy, the Commandant of Oran. One wonders if the music that Mozart would have composed for Selim might have foreshadowed the music that he composed for the benevolent, forgiving Sarastro in *Die Zauberflöte*.

## Synopsis

The young Spanish girl Constanze has been abducted by pirates, together with her English maid Blonde and Pedrillo, the latter's lover. They are in Turkey, having been sold there as slaves to Pasha Selim. The young Spanish nobleman Belmonte, who is in love with Constanze, has managed to discover where they are being held and has come to rescue them.

### Act I

Belmonte's initial attempt to gain access to Pasha Selim's palace is thwarted by the surly overseer Osmin, who showers him with abuse before chasing him off. Osmin sounds off about intruders to Pedrillo, who is working for Selim as the palace gardener, and adds that Pedrillo himself should be put to death. Belmonte finds Pedrillo and the servant devises a plan to bring his master into the palace; once this has been done, they will be able to rescue the ladies and return to Spain on Belmonte's ship.

Constanze and the Pasha land from a pleasure boat and are greeted by a chorus of Janissaries. Selim has fallen in love with her, but does not want to force her to become his wife. When he bemoans the fact that she remains cold and distant despite the kindness he has shown to her, she replies that she is in love with somebody else and so will never be able to return his feelings.

Pedrillo introduces Belmonte to the Pasha, passing him off as an architect from abroad: Selim shows an interest in him and invites him to stay in the palace. Osmin tries to bar them from entering the house, but Pedrillo and Belmonte push their way past him.

### Act II

Osmin's attempts to force Blonde to love him prove futile: she remains unmoved by his boorish commands and threats, and soon dismisses him. Constanze is still unhappy and is pining for her beloved Belmonte. Selim once again turns his attentions to her, this time threatening her with torture, but her mind is made up - she declares that she would rather die than give in to the Pasha.

Constanze and Belmonte are left alone together and swear eternal love for each other, even in the face of death. Pedrillo tells Blonde that Belmonte is in the palace, and that they plan to rescue the girls later that night. The maid goes off to tell her mistress the good news, and in the meantime Pedrillo plies Osmin with wine until he staggers off into the house and goes to sleep. Belmonte and Constanze meet again and embrace passionately. The joyful atmosphere is momentarily spoilt when the men begin to doubt that the girls have been faithful to them, but are soon convinced of their mistake; the girls forgive them for being suspicious and the two couples are once again reconciled.

### Act III

Belmonte and Pedrillo appear under the palace windows at midnight. After Pedrillo has signalled to the girls with a serenade, Belmonte climbs up a ladder into Constanze's room and rescues her. But just as Pedrillo is about to fetch Blonde, Osmin wakes up, alerts the watch and has the would-be fugitives apprehended. Selim appears, awoken by all the noise. He recognises Belmonte to be the son of his sworn enemy, who once drove him out of his homeland, and threatens to take revenge on the young man. But when the Pasha returns he unexpectedly grants both couples their freedom: he has decided to repay the cruelty of Belmonte's father with generosity. Osmin rushes off in a rage whilst the others praise Selim's magnanimity.

Synopsis by Gerd Uekermann
Translation by Chris Wood
