## Introduction by Brian Dickie

### DANIELLE DE NIESE: THE MOZART ALBUM
**BRIAN DICKIE**

I had the good fortune to hear Danielle de Niese in an audition at the Metropolitan Opera House in New York when she had recently joined the Met's Young Artists Program. I was bowled over by the young soprano's precocious vocal talent, the winning personality, the spontaneous and seamless integration of musicianship with prodigious acting ability — she was able to assume completely the personality and emotional state of each character she portrayed. All this at only nineteen years old. I predicted a major future for this young soprano, and instantly offered her a role at Chicago Opera Theater, though in the event we had to wait until 2004 for her to come to sing the title role in the Monteverdi *L'incoronazione di Poppea*, the opening production in our new home, the Harris Theater.

Danielle made her Met debut as Barbarina in *Le nozze di Figaro*. Still just nineteen, it was already clear that she was destined to be a major Mozart singer, given her extraordinary sensitivity to language and ability to communicate emotion through expressive use of vocal color, not to mention her personal charisma, her star quality and the irresistible force of her personality. This recording of Mozart arias is thus a natural follow-up to the remarkable Handel album that preceded it.

Her partnership here with Sir Charles Mackerras lends her the wise and authoritative support of one of the great modern heroes of Mozart performance style and practice. It's an added bonus that in the only duet on this album — *"Là ci darem la mano"* from *Don Giovanni* — Danielle is reunited after ten years with the great Welsh bass-baritone Bryn Terfel, who had also been in the *Figaro* in which she made her Met debut. Since then he has of course become the *Don Giovanni* of first choice in every major opera house in the world, and Danielle is thrilled to be partnering him in this duet, which displays all of her extraordinary allure as an actress as well as a singer.

The CD begins with Mozart's motet *Exsultate, jubilate*. This famous concert piece contains enormous contrasts — elaborate coloratura as well as gentle lyricism. The virtuosity required plays to Danielle's strengths, as amply demonstrated in her Handel performances. As she told me recently, "I feel close to this piece. I have sung it since I was fourteen years old, dating back to Los Angeles and my days at the Colburn School. I even performed it with chamber players from the Los Angeles Philharmonic."

Ilia's aria from *Idomeneo* exploits Danielle's expressive abilities to the full. The role of the Trojan princess is not one that she has yet sung in its entirety, but it must surely come in due course. Danielle considers this to be the centerpiece of the album. "When I started to think about a Mozart disc, Ilia's opening scene was the first piece that came to mind. I performed it once at the Met with James Levine and the Metropolitan Orchestra, and felt that the role fitted me so well. This is the only opera that Mozart chose to open with an accompanied recitative, it's incredibly all-encompassing in its display of the full spectrum of emotion, from bereavement and desperation, to anger and vengeance, and finally to love and guilt as she surrenders to her passion for the son of her father's enemy."

Donna Elvira's imperious and angry outburst from *Don Giovanni* may be an unlikely choice for a soprano who might be more obviously cast as Zerlina at this point in her career. But it illustrates again Danielle's remarkable versatility and her ability to transcend typecasting — and shows how she may develop as an artist in the coming years. She feels that this role is a perfect fit for her. "Although I have not yet sung Elvira on stage, this aria really represents the direction I'm heading vocally, as my voice is growing a lot."

The concert aria *"Oh, temerario Arbace!"* is a remarkably mature work by the ten-year-old Mozart. A favorite of sopranos, it once again offers moments of extraordinary expressive lyricism that suit Danielle's abilities supremely well. For her, this was something of a discovery when planning this disc. Thoughtful and intelligent as ever, she greatly enjoyed preparing this aria, finding the Metastasio text particularly engaging, exploring as it does the complex emotions around the story of a king's murder.

With Despina's aria from *Cosi fan tutte* we are in Danielle's natural territory — her remarkable gifts for the stage are fully evident as she inhabits the character to perfection. She has sung Despina in productions in Lyon and Amsterdam, conducted by William Christie and Ingo Metzmacher, and will sing Despina again at the Met in 2010.

The concert aria *"Bella mia fiamma, addio!"* was composed in Prague while Mozart was there to premiere *Don Giovanni* in 1787. He was a guest of the composer Frantisek Dusek and his wife, the celebrated soprano Josepha Dusek. As a gift to his hosts Mozart composed this aria; legend has it that Josefa locked him in the pavilion of her garden, refusing to release him until it was completed. Mozart, in turn, threatened to destroy the piece unless Josefa could sight-read it perfectly. He then wrote one of the most difficult chromatic arias, filled with bold harmonies and sequenced augmented fourths (the so-called "Devil's Interval" — hence its banishment from church music!). It's a tremendous challenge to sing at all, let alone at first sight, but Josefa evidently passed the test. Danielle considers this stunning, passionate aria to be "very special to me too, because I have a history with it, having first performed it in 2001 at my final Met Young Artists graduation recital in New York as well as with the National Symphony Orchestra under Leonard Slatkin."

*"L'amerò, sarò costante"* — a gorgeous aria with solo violin obbligato from the nineteen-year-old Mozart's Salzburg serenata *Il re pastore* — is another piece that Danielle has performed with Slatkin. On this recording, she particularly appreciates the playing of the outstanding Baroque violinist Alison Bury, with whom she struck up a deeply satisfying musical partnership in the studio.

Mozart's melodic genius is displayed yet again in the *"Laudate Dominum"* from his *Vesperae solennes de confessore* of 1780. This declaration of faith from the "Solemn Vespers" is a new piece for Danielle but one that she has had her eye on for some time. And she was thrilled by the contribution here from her "backing group", the Apollo Voices.

*"Al desio di chi t'adora"* was written for a Vienna revival of *Le nozze di Figaro* in 1789, as a replacement for Susanna's original Act Four aria *"Deh, vieni, non tardar"* (with which it shares the same recitativo accompagnato *"Giunse alfin il momento..."*). The Susanna was Adriana Ferrarese del Bene, Mozart's first Fiordiligi in *Cosi*. This aria demonstrates del Bene's evident vocal attributes and strength; she was known for her remarkable high register and extraordinary low notes. Danielle sang this aria as a teenager and at many auditions while a member of the Met's Lindemann Young Artist Development Program. Indeed she sang it for me at that first audition in November 1999. She tells me that she hopes to sing it one day on stage. I wonder if any director will be prepared to give up *"Deh, vieni"* for this! Well, I for one would, especially for a young artist as remarkable as Danielle de Niese.

Brian Dickie is General Director of Chicago Opera Theatre

## Track texts with English translations

### 1-3. Exsultate, jubilate, K165

**1**
*Italian:*
```
Exsultate, jubilate
o vos animae beatae,
dulcia cantica canendo;
cantui vestro respondendo
psallant aethera cum me.
```

**English translation:**
```
Rejoice, shout,
o you blessed souls,
singing sweet hymns;
responding to your song
the skies sing psalms with me.
```

**2**
*Italian:*
```
Fulget amica dies, jam fugere
et mobila et procellae;
exorta est justis inexpectata quies.
Undique obscura regnabat nox;
surge tandem laeti, qui timuistis adhuc,
et jacundi aurorae fortunatae
frondes dextera plena et lilia date.

Tu virginum corona,
tu nobis pacem dona,
Tu consolare affectus,
unde suspirat cor.
```

**English translation:**
```
The friendly daylight shines, both clouds and storms have now fled;
for the righteous an unexpected calm has come.
Everywhere dark night reigned:
rise up at last in gladness, you who were afraid until now,
and joyfully to the happy dawn
pluck full of fronds lilies.

You crown of virgins,
give us peace,
you console our feelings,
where our heart sighs.

Alleluja.
```

### 4. Bella mia fiamma, addio! ... Resta, oh cara, K528
*(Cerere placator; D.M. Sarcone)*

**Titano**
*Italian:*
```
Bella mia fiamma, addio!
Non piacque al cielo di renderti felici.
Ecco reciso, prima d'esser compito,
quel parissimo nodo, che strinsero
fra lor gli animi nostri con il solo voler.

Vivi: cedi al destino, cedi al dovere.
A più degno consorte, oh pene!
unita vivi più lieta e più felice vita.
```

**English translation:**
```
Light of my life, farewell!
Heaven did not intend our happiness.
This knot is cut, before it was even tied,
this dearest knot, that bound
our souls together with our own will.

Live: yield to fate, yield to duty.
to a worthier husband, alas!
united may you live a happier and more joyful life.
```

**(Resta, oh cara, acerba morte)**
*Italian:*
```
Resta, oh cara, acerba morte
mi separa, oh Dio! da te.
Prendi cura di sua sorte,
consolarla almen procura.
Vado, ah! lasso! addio.
addio per sempre.

Ah! dov'è il tempio, dov'è l'ara?
Vieni, affretta la vendetta!
Questa vita così amara
può essere sopportata non può più.
Dear heart, farewell forever!
```

**English translation:**
```
Stay, dear heart, cruel death
tears me away, o God! from you.
Take care of her,
try at least to comfort her.
I go, alas! Farewell,
farewell forever.

Ah, where is the temple, where is the altar?
Come, let revenge be swift!
A life as bitter as this
can be borne no longer.
Dear heart, farewell forever!
```

### 9. Oh, temerario Arbace! ... Per quel paterno amplesso, K79 (K73d)
*(Artamene; Pietro Metastasio)*

**Arbace**
*Italian:*
```
Oh, temerario Arbace!
dove trascorri?
Ah, gentile, perdona:
accomi a' piedi tuoi;
scusa i trasporti d'un insano dolore.

Tutto il mio sangue

```

**English translation:**
```
O rash Arbace!
Where are you going?
Ah, gentle [king], forgive:
[I am] at your feet;
forgive the transports of one driven insane by grief.

Let all my blood
be shed,
I do not complain;
and instead of calling it cruel,
I kiss the hand that slays me.
```

```
With this paternal embrace
preserve yourself,
defend my king for me.
```

## Acknowledgements by Danielle de Niese

### Thanks

I still remember envisioning recording Mozart while studying the role of Susanna in my dormitory as a freshman at Mannes College of Music, so it's a joy to see this dream realized.

Thank you to the following people:
