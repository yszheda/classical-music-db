## [Composer Notes - Galina Ustvolskaya]
by Mark Swed

Galina Ustvolskaya was born in Petrograd in 1919, making her a member of the generation preceding Pärt and Gorecki. But as elusive as those two composers have been, even in their celebrity, she has been even more so in her obscurity. Although we outside of Russia know little about her, obscurity may be the wrong word to describe her career, since she has been a formidable presence in St. Petersburg, where she studied at the Leningrad Conservatory between 1937 and 1947, with some time off for the war, and where she has taught ever since. She apparently made an indelible impression early on. Dmitri Shostakovich, with whom she studied, called her his "music conscience." He patronized her further saying, "It is not you that I am under yours." and once quoted her in his own work.

The Shostakovich tributes are regularly trotted out these days, as Ustvolskaya's music is finally, if still slowly, making its way West, to help her undo her obscurity. But her connection with Shostakovich is probably overrated, her music did not, for long, remain much like his, and Shostakovich was not so conscious of his "musical conscience" as to acknowledge her at all in his posthumously published autobiography, *Testimony*. In fact, Ustvolskaya's mature music, short, laconic works of intense rhetoric, sharply defined sounds, obsessive ostinatos and, for its age, shocking freedom of expression, have little in common with Shostakovich. Where her neurotic mentor turned inward in a music full of private codes, of passive/aggressive responses to a world of artistic repression, hers is a direct, boldly dramatic statement that pulls no punches. It was a dissonant and dissident music which earned her her obscurity.

The *Twelve Preludes* for piano, written in 1953, offer an excellent example of how even early on in her career, Ustvolskaya remained as independent of Shostakovich's influence as she seems to of just about any other current compositional technique. There is a hint, here and there, of her youthful years as a neoclassicist, a bit of Hindemith or Bartok influence here and there, and there is also the stray Shostakovichian angular motivic figure to be found. But mainly the preludes distinguish themselves by being so very different from Shostakovich's own set of piano preludes, and already the thunderous language of her music, which is extraordinary for its ability to speak for itself, is strongly apparent.

That uncompromising language is fully evident in the **Piano Sonata No 6**, written in 1988. Each note and crashing chord seems to be punched out of the piano with Biblical force, as clustered chords rush closer and louder together. The sonata has an unusual form, the last part of it repeating the material in the first part in reverse order, as if a container is needed to enclose such vehement expression. Where Pärt finds in the piano a gentle sonic transcendence, and Gorecki has, from the start, fought hard to channel his political anger into a sensual and embracing humanity, Ustvolskaya remains the conscience not of Shostakovich but that of Russian music, uncompromising in her Old Testament ferocity and veracity.

© 1995, Mark Swed

## [Composer Notes - Arvo Pärt & Henryk Gorecki]

### Introduction

The first notes heard on this recording are tranquil, soothing. The last ones, crashing and without consolation, are anything but that. In between, there is music inspired by archaic chant, by Polish folksong, by motoric Prokofiev and melancholic Shostakovich. The composers, too, come from, and represent, cultures probably more dissimilar than alike. Arvo Pärt is Estonian and now lives in Berlin. Henryk Gorecki is Polish and has settled in his favored remote region of Poland, Katowice. Galina Ustvolskaya is a product of St. Petersburg's musical traditions, and she has seldom ventured far from her home.

These are, in fact, three very different sorts of composers, as unalike in culture and background as career (at one extreme, Gorecki has, out of the blue, become one of the world's most successful serious composers in the last few years; Ustvolskaya, at the other, is still little known outside Russia). Nonetheless, we tend to think of these composers -- along with a handful of other Eastern European composers (most particularly the German-born Russian, Alfred Schnittke, the Kazanian-born Sofia Gubaidulina, and the Georgian Giya Kancheli) -- as having something crucial in common that makes their music distinct from all other Western music. And as diverse as the various pieces on this recording may sound, we are not entirely wrong in so thinking.

All three composers are the grandchildren of the avant-garde, that short-lived optimistic historical moment in the late '20s and early '30s when the Soviet art was allowed to reflect the optimistic mood of the country in an extravagantly futuristic manner. But they are also children of Social Realism and grew up under clamped-down Stalinism and worked in its still repressive aftermath. They are composers who underwent dangerous rebellions in the seeking and finding, through their music, spiritual revelations. They are composers who grew to maturity as musicians having to locate their own voices in a malicious environment of artistic controls. And although that world exists no more, their solutions to artistic crises are of a life-and-death seriousness that informs and transcends every note of their music and always has.

Arvo Pärt was the first of the major Soviet composers to develop a wide listenership in the West (Schnittke became known first to the classical world in general, but Pärt was the first to attract a best-selling crossover audience). Unlike Schnittke, who responded to the complex and confusing life in Russia with a music built upon living with, and even exulting the dissonance of that confusion, Pärt was drawn out of such turbulence altogether. Applying a technique he called tintinnabulation, the sound of ringing bells, he developed a style of music that is becalming in its pure, luminous sonic simplicity.

Pärt's bells are forbidden bells, the ones of the Russian Orthodox church. So as his music became increasingly devout, its aura of spiritual wonderment surely proved even more disturbing to the upholders of Soviet Realism than his early "decadent" abstraction was.

Pärt in fact was -- as was Gorecki as well -- a double rebel. Before tintinnabulating, he had been the first Estonian composer to write twelve-tone music. Born in Paide in 1935 and raised in Tallinn, Pärt initially conformed to the Soviet populist artistic constraints. For ten years, between 1957 and 1967, he served as the sound director for Estonian Radio, although during that period he began experimenting with the immensely elitist dodecaphony.

Pärt's more radical departure, though, was the overthrowing of that dodecaphonic complexity for utter but profound simplicity found in the mystic music of Gregorian chant. "I have discovered," he later said, "that it is enough when a single note is beautifully played." And as Pärt's music became known in the West, he developed the reputation of being a painfully shy, mysterious figure who writes music ancient in technique but with a kind of lucid son's resonance that is peculiarly modern. Indeed, that sound, transfixing and unforgettable in its richness, and the minimalism of the musical materials made Pärt seem to share a certain unknowing kinship with the American Minimalist composers, a perception that surely had a great effect on his nascent popularity in the West.

The two short piano pieces, *Für Alina* and *Variationen Zur Gesundung von Arinuschka*, written in 1976 and 1991 respectively, are sterling examples of Pärt's power over a single note, but they also reveal a charming personal side to the composer rarely displayed in his more somber churchly music. Both are tiny pieces. The bells are heard in the sweet, ethereal *Für Alina* (For Alina), which falls somewhere between Gregorian chant and a lullaby. In the score, Pärt asks that music be "still," "elevated" and that the performer "listen inside himself."

*Variationen Zur Gesundung von Arinuschka* (Variations for Arinuschka's Convalescence), Pärt provides six lucid, if uncomplicated, variations on a childlike tune that beams with warmth, not the least because of the composer's sophisticated sense of piano resonances and their ability to produce the sensation of well-being.

Henryk Gorecki was born in 1933 in Polish Silesia and remained a composer known only to followers of new music until 1992, when the fourth recording of his Third Symphony, which had been written in 1976, suddenly became an international sensation, playing on pop radio stations in Europe and America and becoming the incongruous favorite work of British punk-rockers who used it to chill out to. But if the worldwide fame of Gorecki was an overnight anomaly, his musical development was not. After a traditional education, Gorecki overthrew the acceptable Soviet-imposed Polish style of the '50s, and, during the post-Stalinist cultural thaw, he became enamored of the Parisian avant-garde. But like Pärt, Gorecki, too, rebelled a second time, forsaking the avant-garde for a more accessible style that had its roots in Polish folk and sacred musics, and that could express a devout spirituality. Though Gorecki's style is far more theatrical that Pärt's, the Polish composer has proved his equal in creating an atmosphere of sheer sonic wonderment.

The two early piano works recorded here offer an opportunity to witness just where those clenching sonorities originally came from and how Gorecki made them as distinctive as he has, no matter in what styles he has written. The *Four Preludes*, written in 1955 and Gorecki's first published work, reveal an ambitious 21-year-old young composer who came equally out of the sensual work of Karol Szymanowski, Poland's most important composer after Chopin, and the more urban one of Prokofiev. The outer movements of the short Sonata, written the following year and twice revised (in 1984 and then again in 1990), will also remind the listener of the percussive rhythmic drive in the late Prokofiev piano sonatas. But Gorecki ends his sonata with a pounding resonance that overwhelms any pianistic thunder in Prokofiev and provides a powerful glimpse at the kind of overpowering sonorities Gorecki could achieve (something that he still eagerly produces when he plays the piano). It is, however, in that haunting and inexplicable central slow movement, which sounds like it came out of nothing in Western tradition up to that time, that Gorecki really reveals what a mysterious and compelling voice he was developing.

## [Biography of David Arden]

### David Arden, piano

David Arden has devoted himself for 20 years to the promotion of contemporary music. He has been awarded the Kranichsteiner Prize at the Darmstadt Ferienkurse für Neue Musik, First Prize at the Gaudeamus International Competition for Contemporary Music, and was a Prizewinner in the Carnegie Hall International Competition for American Music. Mr. Arden has been chosen by the United States Information Agency for its Art America Program abroad, touring Asia, the South Pacific and South America. He received his Concert Diploma from the Royal Flemish Music Conservatory, and his teachers have included Aloys Kontarsky, Frederic Gevers and Lucy Brown.

Mr. Arden has worked with Henryk Górecki in Europe, receiving his enthusiastic praise in a taping for Polish Television. In 1993, he gave the US premiere of Gorecki's Sonata No. 1 in a New York recital. In addition, he has worked with, and premiered pieces by, such notable composers as Oliver Messiaen, Henryk Górecki, Luciano Berio, Karlheinz Stockhausen, John Cage, Earle Brown, Jacob Druckman, and Bernard Rands.

Mr. Arden has made radio broadcasts in Belgium, Holland, France, Germany, England, Japan, Hong Kong, Australia, New Zealand, and the United States. And in addition to this recording, he has recorded for the European label Musica Magna, as well as two recent CD's of the complete piano works of Earle Brown and Carson Kievman for New Albion. This compact disc marks Mr. Arden's debut recording for KOCH International Classics.
