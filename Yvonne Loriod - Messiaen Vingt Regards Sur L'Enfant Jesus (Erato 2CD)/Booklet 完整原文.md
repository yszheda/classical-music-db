## vingts regards sur l'enfant jésus
### 1944

They express the various contemplations of the Child Jesus in the crib and the Adorations which are bestowed upon him: from the inexhaustible Adoration of God the Father – to the manifold Adoration of the Church of love, through the personalised Adoration of the Spirit of Joy, the extreme tenderness of the Adoration of the Virgin – then that of the Angels, the Magi, and the pastoral or symbolic creatures (Time, the Heights, Silence, the Star and the Cross).

In addition to those themes which are particular to each of the twenty pieces, four cyclic themes recur throughout the work: a) the Theme of God, b) the Theme of Chords, c) the Theme of the Star and the Cross – d) the Theme of God's "Son" and "The Adoration of the Son".

In close devotion to the three Persons of the Holy Trinity: "The Adoration of the Father", "The Adoration of the Son by the Son" and "The Adoration of the Spirit of Joy". It is also heard in "By Him were all things made" (since the Creative Spirit is attributed to the Word, without whom nothing was made), in "The Kiss of the Child Jesus" and in "The Virgin's First Communion" (each time he is carrying Jesus within her); and it is extended to form the final 'Gloria' in "The Adoration of the Church of love" (the church and all creatures are the body of Christ). The Theme of the Son of Love recurs in "By Him were all things made", "I sleep, but my heart wakes", and "The Adoration of the Cross, the Star and the Cross have the same theme, because the one opens and the other closes the earthly sojourn of Jesus (see "The Adoration of the Star" and "The Adoration of the Cross"). The Theme of Chords occurs throughout: fragmented, concentrated, halved with regard to fragmented, halved, with regard to rhythm and register – transformed and transumed in all kinds of ways. It forms a complex of notes which are later subjected to endless variation – it pre-exists in the abstract, like a series, but is musically concrete and easily recognisable by its colours (a steely grey-blue crossed with red and bright orange, a mauve-ish violet spotted with leather-brown and tinged with purplish black).

The pieces are arranged according to contrasts of tempo, intensity and colours, as well as for certain spiritual reasons. Every fifth piece is one which deals with the Divinity: I – The Adoration of the Father, V – The Adoration of the Son by the Son, X – The Adoration of the Spirit of joy – XV ("The Kiss of the Child Jesus" – the visible manifestation of the invisible God) – XX (The Adoration of the Church of love" (the body of the church being the extension of the Body of Christ)).

"The Adoration of the Cross" bears the number VII (7, the perfect number) because the sufferings of Christ on the Cross have restored to man the 'seventh day' (Genesis 7). Since Angels are beings already confirmed in grace, "The Adoration of the Angels" bears the number XIV (2 times 7). "The Adoration of Time" bears the number IX: within itself, Time has seen the birth of the Eternal One, confining him for the nine months of maternity experienced by all other children. "The Adoration of the Awesome Anointing" bears the number XVIII (God takes 3): the Divinity of God is cast upon the Humanity of Christ in a single being – the Son of God; the astounding wonders of this Anointing, which makes human flesh by the dreadful Majesty of God, presupposes both the Incarnation and the Passion. The two pieces concerning the Creation and the Divine Love cement, or Upholding of all things, as well as the continuity of God (in itself, are VI: By Him were all things made" (6 is the number of the Creation) – XII) "The all-powerful Word" (12 = 2 times 6).

Now follows a concise analysis of each piece:

### I – Regard du Père (The Adoration of the Father)

This piece opens with a complete exposition of the Theme of God. And God says: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased". (The Gospel according to St. Matthew, III, 17.)

### II – Regard de l'Étoile (The Adoration of the Star)

The Theme of the Star and the Cross, containing distinct echoes of Greek metrical rhythms and the neumes of plainsong.

### III – L'Échange (The Exchange)

This piece is one long crescendo, organised according to the procedures of 'asymmetrical augmentation': the same few fragments are repeated, but with the duration of each of them extended by a certain number of time units. With each new phrase, certain notes rise, others fall, others stay the same. The work was based on a line from the antiphon from the Missal: "How wonderful is the dealing here! The Creator of humankind, taking on a body and a soul, had deign to be born of the Virgin, in order that we might share in his divinity".

### IV – Regard de la Vierge (The Adoration of the Virgin)

Absolutely simple and naive. The music here is based on the alteration of two Greek metres: the ionic minor and the ionic Epitrite. In the middle section, several 'modes of limited transposition' are superposed: mode 4¹ on mode 3¹ and on 2¹ 3¹ on 4¹ – to obtain extremely varied colours. In the recapitulation, a new melodic counterpart expresses the tenderness of the maternal gaze.

### V – Regard du Fils sur le Fils (The Adoration of the Son by the Son)

This piece concerns the Creation, accomplished by the Son of God or the Word (and the Child Jesus is the Son of God made man). "All things were made by him, and without him was not anything made that was made." (The Gospel according to St. John, I, 3.)

It expresses the Creation – by All Things: space, time, stars, planets – and the Countenance (or rather, the Thought) of God behind the flames and the seething – impossible even to speak of it, I have no intention to describe it. Instead, I have sheltered behind the form of the Fugue, from J.S. Bach's Art of Fugue and the first Fugue (Beethoven's Sonata op. 106 (the Hammerklavier sonata) has nothing to do with the Hammerklavier fugue. Like this great model, my work has an anti-scholastic fugue.

A fugal Divertissement then superposes two elements. a) The upper part: the Subject is treated in 'non-retrogradable rhythm' (that is to say, the order of durations is exactly the same whether the rhythm is read from left to right, or from right to left – whence the psychical strength of the rhythm).

The Subject is progressively (and symmetrically) eliminated from either end: the non-retrogradable rhythm thus remains constant, since it is amputated from both directions at once – demonstrating our powerlessness to grasp the fullness of non-retrogradable rhythms'. b) At the same time, the bass explores a subject formed of 'asymmetrical augmentation' (in the lowest octave of the piano) permeates the texture, underpinning the entire Divertissement.

The middle section alternates two contrasting phrase-structures: the one extremely brief, the other more protracted. I got this idea from the two great works on astronomy (the category of largeness) and on micro-physics (the category of smallness). The third subject, in quaver demiquavers (heard in octaves, in both the extreme registers), represents the extreme smallness of the Subject (altered in rhythm and register), repeating it in 'asymmetrical augmentation'. This fortissimo theme (in the lowest octave of the piano) permeates the texture, underpinning the entire Divertissement.

### VI – Par Lui tout a été fait (By Him were all things made)

This piece concerns the Creation, accomplished by the Son of God or the Word (and the Child Jesus is the Son of God made man). "All things were made by him, and without him was not anything made that was made." (The Gospel according to St. John, I, 3.)

It expresses the Creation – by All Things: space, time, stars, planets – and the Countenance (or rather, the Thought) of God behind the flames and the seething – impossible even to speak of it, I have no intention to describe it. Instead, I have sheltered behind the form of the Fugue, from J.S. Bach's Art of Fugue and the first Fugue (Beethoven's Sonata op. 106 (the Hammerklavier sonata) has nothing to do with the Hammerklavier fugue. Like this great model, my work has an anti-scholastic fugue.

First entry: normal Subject and Counter-subject. Second entry: in the Counter-subject of the Subject are altered, and it is superposed on itself in 'asymmetrical augmentation'. Third entry: inversion of both the Answer and the Counter-subject – fourth entry: the rhythm and the register of the Answer are altered and superposed in turn on the answer augmenting the Subject. This is immediately followed by a stretto of the Subject, in a triple canon of 'non-retrogradable rhythms'. The Counter-subject is in turn, altered in both rhythm and register, by a quick succession of octaves, seventh-, and sixth-chords forms the Theme of Chords. A further entry of the Subject in a rhythmic triple canon is superposed on staccato chords and tripletted arpeggios in the lower piano. The Subject is then further transformed; it is treated in equal values, in 'normal' and dotted rhythms – and covers the whole range of the keyboard. Each of its individual pitches is followed by percussive grace-note groups, and interwoven with fragments of the Theme of Chords – which is split into groups of one, two, three and four notes (mainly two notes). The same Theme of Chords reappears in the form of grace-notes and multiple trills – reminiscent of the attack and roll of metallic percussion.

A fugal Divertissement then superposes two elements. a) The upper part: the Subject is treated in 'non-retrogradable rhythm' (that is to say, the order of durations is exactly the same whether the rhythm is read from left to right, or from right to left – whence the psychical strength of the rhythm).

The middle section alternates two contrasting phrase-structures: the one extremely brief, the other more protracted. This fortissimo theme (in the lowest octave of the piano) permeates the texture, underpinning the entire Divertissement.

### VII – Regard de la Croix (The Adoration of the Cross)

The Theme of the Star and the Cross is accompanied by a perpetual chromatic motif (tension, accent, dissonance, accent), which is transformed through each phrase of the theme into a sonorous mass of transpositions (mode 4ᵉ (whose general colour consists of vertical bands of yellow, violet and black) and mode 3¹ (made up of reflections of carmine red, violet-ish purple, orange, grey-mauve and grey-pink). This superposition is set against the blackish grey of the recurring chromatic motif – converging at the end of each phrase on to a minor chord with an added major sixth (a chord which has the colour of deep violet).

### VIII – Regard des Hauteurs (The Adoration of the Heights)

It was on the night of the Nativity that the song of the Angels, "Gloria in excelsis" (Glory be to God on high) rang out for the first time; the Angels are his most glorious by birdsong: the song-thrush, the nightingale, the blackbird, the garden warbler, the robin, the blackcap – all kinds of birds. The main soloist, the one with the leading role, is the skylark – the musician of the Heights, bird of the day par excellence – whose song revolves between two poles: a long low note heard during those brief moments of hovering flight, and the extremely highpitched notes that accompany the beating of the wings: testons of melody wind around this dominant theme, leading towards an everlasting, sublime and unpuncturable ceiling.

### IX – Regard du Temps (The Adoration of Time)

The Incarnation of the Word is like an invasion of Time by Eternity; Time encompasses the birth of the Eternal One. We are here facing a mystery which, for certain theologians, is the greatest of all mysteries. The whole piece is thus mysterious – a mystery which is mirrored in the appearances of the musical material. Two musical ideas are developed alternately: a) a theme of differing phrase-lengths – b) a rhythmic canon. This rhythmic canon is in three parts, and the entries of its three themes (built on chords of perfect and augmented fourths) are separated each time by a quarter. The three rhythm canons are of a metrical (long, short, long), although the lengths of the short notes have different values (which are more or less long or short). The linked repetitions of this rhythmic pattern represent the following temporal relationships: the initial durations: are shrunk by 1/5 of their value to P, adding the length to each length I get (or to P); when the original duration is P, adding four times the length I get P; that is to say I can thus reduce them by 1/3, by 1/4 and by 1/5.

### X – Regard de l'Esprit de joie (The Adoration of the Spirit of joy)

In the course of its earthly life, the Soul of Christ enjoyed the lasting privilege of the beatific vision. God is eternally happy, and Christ possesses this same joy; this rapidity, this spiritual intoxication, which we express in the words, 'tu tolus sanctus' (thou alone are the Holy One)'. This joy leads to the permanent dwelling-place of the Holy Spirit, hence the meaning of the title of the piece: Spirit of Joy.

It can be divided into seven sections: 1) An oriental Dance played in the lowest register of the keyboard, and interspersed with the violence of the continuous grace-notes. This section makes use of the neumes of plainsong and of notes which are repeated two or three times, more or less rapidly. 2) The first appearance of the Theme of Joy. 3) A horizontal passage, superposing three kinds of 'asymmetrical augmentations' in which certain notes rise or fall chromatically with each repetition: only the E is fixed. A series of repeated E's (the dominant of the succeeding A major) illustrates an intensified alternation of piano techniques, with the hand held in a flat position, the fingers revolve from right to left around the thumb – like the rotary motion of a pendulum. Further groups of contrary-motion grace-notes lead to the fourth section. 4) The third theme, in a clerical rhythm, sounds like brass horns. It presents three successive variations: the first in A major, the second in D flat, the third in G flat. The first and second appearances of the Theme of Joy is heard as a carillon of high bells. Starting from a pianissimo subject, the music swells through a crescendo on the first four notes of the Theme of Joy, harmonised by a contraction of the chord in mode 3³. 5) Here is the neumatic oriental Dance of the opening, heard here this time in the extreme registers of the piano. 6) The Coda is based on the Theme of a non-retrogradable, and surrounded by pedal crescendos. A passage of trills represents the song of a bird. A last statement of the third theme (heard by the four horns) is followed by the final phrase – which starts with grace-notes and ends with a headlong descent towards the concluding 'bass drum' stroke.

### XI – Première Communion de la Vierge (The Virgin's first communion)

This piece represents the period between the Annunciation and the Nativity: the first and greatest of all communions. Mary worships the Invisible Word in her: God, my God, my Magnificat! my wordless love...

The Theme of God is heard – pianissimo – in B flat and mode 2¹. Rapid melodic festoons evoke the designs of stalactites in excelsior grottos. Other melodic garlands – slower (in quavers), like two doves embracing each other, infinitely tenderless the silent Truth within us. A short reference to "The Virgin and Child" from the first piece of this tombeau; "The Nativity of our Lord") precedes the unprepared-for entry of the Magnificat. The Theme of God recurs – faster (in crotchet and trochaic rhythm. The inclusion of dotted values underlines the breathlessness anacrusis which preaches each accent of this theme of joy and praise. Next, the note D (the note of the anacrusis) is harmonised in a score of different ways: by the 'chord of transposed inversions', and alternating two series of numbers – one of which runs from 1-13, the other from 13-1; the others are at first only implied, are later heard in the bass as regular pulsations representing the heartbeat of the Child. The Coda is based on the Theme of God, which dies away into silence towards the final major third of the inward embrace.

### XII – La Parole toute-puissante (The all-powerful Word)

The Child Jesus is this Son, this Word who upholds 'all things' by his powerful word. (The Epistle of St. Paul to the Hebrews, I, 3.)

The musical language is characterised by both simplicity and terror. It is a fortissimo monody, based on a melodic mode, followed by an augmented fifth and two perfect fifths in scalar structure, as well as the recurring features of a minor third, followed by an augmented fifth and two perfect fifths. Certain notes are ornamented with a descending grace-note figure which always precedes them. The Greek rhythmic pattern here is: Amphimac, Bacchius and the various Epitrites. A percussive effect on the piano (the having the sonority of a tam-tam) substitutes for the missing harmony; this is an ostinato on a 'non-retrogradable rhythm', based on three chords in values 3, 5, 6, 5, 3 – each repetition of this ostinato being separated from the preceding by a silence equivalent to 7 (semiquavers). Several low C's, furnished with a crescendo/de-drum roll, later collide violently with the 'tam-tam' rhythm.

### XIII – Noël (Christmas)

The first part of the piece is a carillon of Christmas bells. There is also a 'xylophone' theme, a concentrated, fragmented form of the Theme of Chords, and the Hindu rhythm 'mica varna' (which means 'mixture of colours' in Sanskrit) – as well as various quivering sounds and alien rustlings. The second part evokes the Holy Family and the Adoration of the Child in the Crib. It is written in the mode of limited transposition, No. 3 (coloured orange, gold and milky-white). Set against that is another polymodal colour: mode 2² (orange, gold and tinged with blue) is superposed on modes 2¹ (gold and brown) and 3¹ (grey and mauve), with a treble pedal-point while alien notes add a touch of red. In the reprise of the first section, the bells reappear through a series of converging chords (a fan-like motion); the cadence bars then refer briefly to the middle section. The whole piece requires the pianist to produce various kinds of attack, in imitation of other instruments: the timbre of bells and the tam-tam, and xylophone and clarinets and flutes – and the concluding 'timpani' rhythm.

### XIV – Regard des Anges (The Adoration of the Angels)

In this example, the 'blaze' of a revolving figure (played with hands crossed, one above the other, while simultaneously) illustrates the following quotation from the Psalm: 'qui maketh, his ministers a flaming fire' (Psalm 104.4). This is followed by the Theme of Chords and a triple rhythmic canon based on Hindu rhythms. It then comes an iconographic evocation of Michelangelo's 'Last Judgement' (in the Sistine Chapel): the apocalyptic fanfare, along with human forms: eyes half-closed with the effort involved, they blow huge brass instruments which have a sonority akin to that of trombones or Tibetan trumpets above the intervallic augmentation emitted by these 'trombones', fragments of the Theme of Chords are heard.

### XV – Le Baiser de l'Enfant-Jésus (The Kiss of the Child Jesus)

This piece uses the three transpositions of the second mode of limited transposition, key of F sharp, 2¹ with the tonic, 2² with the dominant and 2³ with the subdominant. The rhythms – based on prime numbers, trochees and iambs – are both simple and precise; each of the falling phrases is brought to fruition by an elision at the end of the final note (by the addition of a dot). At the beginning, the piano writing is very simple: (stripped of everything like that of Rameau); successive variations evolve towards an ornamentation of its (progressing from a Mozartian accompaniment to the lightness and agility inspired by one of Chopin's Études). But all this is no more than a technical introduction to the material. The climax of this work (at the score as a subtitle indicates for more: sleep, the garden – arms full out towards love – and the shadow of the trees). I am reminded of a picture I am very fond of, which shows the Child Jesus leaving his Mother's arms to embrace the young Saint Theresa. At each communion, too, the Child Jesus throws open the doors upon a marvellous garden, then rushes out into the bright light to embrace us. Perhaps the explanation of this mystical poem is to be found in the fact that the Theme of God is heard continuously, in the form of a berceuse: as if the heart of the sky encompassed our slumbers with its inexhaustible tenderness – as is so lovingly expressed in the Revelation (XII, 4): "God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes."

### XVI – Regard des prophètes, des bergers et des mages (The Adoration of the prophets, the shepherds and the Magi)

This is exotic music – a vast, natal harmony of tam-tams and oboe. In the introduction, the note-values increase progressively according to a chromatic scale of durations – within a decrescendo. In the Coda, the values rallentando progressively – again according to a chromatic scale of durations, but within a crescendo. These two inverse effects create a quantitative umion, as well as a union of the dynamics.

### XVII – Regard du silence (The Adoration of Silence)

Like the one in the key of A – the Adoration of the Son by the Son – the rhythmic canon heard here is formed by the addition of a third to the notes of the lower part, it is, however, based on different material. The top part consists of a single 'melodic ostinato' in white – while the similar ostinato of the lower part is written in mode 4¹. Following this introduction – where the music seems to emerge from silence – the colours emerge from the dark – are two Strophes. In the first Strophe there are several different levels of sound, which intensify several bands of colour – forming a 'harmonic litany' on the notes G and F. This litany is formed of contracted inversions, chords of contracted resonance; a concentrated version of the (four) parts of the theme, (all four sounded simultaneously). Contrasts of colour between mode 3¹ (blue and green), mode 2³ (mauve and pink in the treble, gold and brown in the bass) and mode 4¹ crossed-hand arpeggios, which quiver with a contrary-motion of cobwebs. Towards the end of the Strophe, the Theme of Chords is combined with itself in both its original and its retrograde forms – the retrogradation adding reverberable emotion and resonance to the sound (like two rainbows around one encircling the other). The second Strophe is based on the same material as the first, but with certain changes. The Coda returns to the polymodality of the Introduction, producing the chords formed by the two hands in the top register of the keyboard – this is multi-coloured and with glittering music – a music of confetti and delicate jewels, with colliding reflections.

### XVIII – Regard de l'Onction terrible (The Adoration of the Awesome Anointing)

The Word takes human form; the awful majesty of God chooses the flesh of Jesus... It is this unique, unparalleled and awesome Anointing which is celebrated in Psalm 45. In order to be able to express this fearful mystery, I have had recourse to this tapistry (in the Cathedral at Angers) representing a scene from the Revelation: the Word of God is at war within the figure of Christ on horseback – nothing is visible except for his two hands which hold a sword which he brandishes in the midst of the storm.

The entire piece is a powerful crescendo, quickened by flashes of lightning. In the introduction, there are two chromatic scales of durations: these two successive durations draw nearer together by convergent motion, with a continuous crescendo.

### XIX – Je dors, mais mon cœur veille (I sleep, but my heart wakes)

This piece is based on the Theme of Mystical love. Taken from that most beautiful of mystical love poems, "The Song of Songs", the title of the piece is spoken by the soul who awaits the Beloved. Music can say so much more than words, so I will say nothing more except – except to quote another image of mystical love, taken from the "Fioretti": the Angel drew his bow across the viol, producing so sweet a sound that, if he had continued to play, all who heard him would have died of joy.

### XX – Regard de l'Église d'amour (The Adoration of the Church of love)

The opening spray-like figure in contrary motion is fingered symmetrically in both hands: this is followed each time by a 'non-retrogradable rhythm', amplified by symmetrical additions at each end. The Theme of God is then heard, in B flat major.

A long transitional passage – confused, menacing, and swamped by the pedal – superposes melodic permutations in the treble on an 'asymmetrical augmentation' in the bass, impelled by the flood-like of its crescendo. The Theme of God in D flat is followed by a second flood-like, by the Theme of Mystical love. The spray-like, contrary-motion figure returns, together with the 'non-retrogradable rhythm' (amplified as before). The third flood-like is longer, and also more varied: the coda is composed of broken octaves. A dominant (C sharp) pedal-point is treated as a carillon of bells; during which the Theme of Chords reappears, along with the 'transposed inversions' prepared-for entry of the Magnificat. The Theme of God recurs – faster (in crotchet and trochaic rhythm. The inclusion of dotted values underlines the breathlessness anacrusis which preaches each accent of this theme of joy and praise. Next, the note D (the note of the anacrusis) is harmonised in a score of different ways: by the 'chord of transposed inversions', and alternating two series of numbers – one of which runs from 1-13, the other from 13-1; the others are at first only implied, are later heard in the bass as regular pulsations representing the heartbeat of the Child. The Coda is based on the Theme of God, which dies away into silence towards the final major third of the inward embrace.
