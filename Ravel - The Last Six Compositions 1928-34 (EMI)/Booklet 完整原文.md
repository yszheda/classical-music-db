## Liner Notes by James Harding

Is a great composer the best person to conduct his own work? With notable exceptions like Mahler, Richard Strauss and Britten, the answer seems to be no. Sir Henry Wood reported an embarrassing occasion when Debussy, conducting the English premiere of *Nocturnes*, "suddenly lost his head", and the orchestra, well rehearsed by Wood, continued playing on their own until the end. Yet a composer knows more than anyone else the effects he wants to create and how his work should be performed in order to achieve them. Ravel was not especially brilliant as pianist or conductor. Certain of the more difficult passages in the piano rolls attributed to him were cut by Robert Casadesus, as witnessed by the latter's wife, who said that Ravel's hands were too small for some of the chords. His baton technique, according to the late Sir Lennox Berkelely, was competent but stiff. Nonetheless, and quite rightly, he had firm views on performance, and when Toscanini conducted *Boléro* at what Ravel considered a "ridiculous speed", he did not hesitate to protest – whereupon the "grand virtuoso", as Ravel described him, impertinently retorted that it was the only way to save the work. There were to be no such incidents at the work's first premiere recording in 1930, conducted by Piero Coppola under Ravel's personal supervision and one day before the composer himself recorded the work for another company.

Fearing that Coppola's "Mediterranean temperament" might carry him away, Ravel followed developments closely. All went well until the last section when Coppola speeded up. The composer tugged at the maestro's coat. "Don't go so fast!" They began again and went on until Ravel was satisfied. The major source of the vast royalties that made Ravel's estate one of the richest ever left by a composer, *Boléro*, which he called "seventeen minutes of 'orchestra without music'", was greeted at its first concert performance with shouts of "Rubbish!" from an old lady in the audience. "She got the message!" Ravel drily observed.

*Boléro*, said Coppola, was very easy to conduct, since the crescendo effect is produced by the orchestration alone: all the conductor has to do is be beat time from start to finish like an automaton, and, as Ravel insisted, observe the strict tempo indicated in the score. Having presumably won his spurs with *Boléro*, Coppola later in 1930 recorded *Menuet antique*, the piano piece of 1895 and Ravel's first published work. Interestingly, here is an early venture into the world of *Daphnis et Chloé* in an orchestration, made in 1929, one year after *Boléro*, by a composer in the fullness of his technical mastery.

The Concerto for the left hand was commissioned by the one-armed pianist Paul Wittgenstein, brother of the philosopher, and written between 1929 and 1930. Ravel bitterly disapproved of Wittgenstein's interpretation which took liberties with the score and often sounded like an arrangement. Although they eventually made their peace and Ravel was to direct the first French performance, the composer never forgave Wittgenstein his cavalier attitude. Ironically, there exists a rare sound film (1933) of Ravel conducting with Wittgenstein as soloist. Much more authentic is Alfred Cortot's recording from 1939.

An accomplished conductor as well as a pianist, Cortot had been a fellow student of Ravel at the Paris Conservatoire. He was admirably equipped to meet the formidable demands of a work that calls for supreme virtuosity. It is a sombre piece, moody even, with occasional touches of the jazz measures Ravel had admired on his recent trip to New York. Indeed, the composer's recollection included, along with the symphonic works one would expect, items from *L'Enfant et les sortilèges*, *Valses nobles et sentimentales* and even Debussy's *La Plus que lente*. The G major Piano Concerto, completed in 1931, is, on the other hand, a bright and sparkling divertimento, which is what Ravel at first planned to call it. Composed in the spirit of Saint-Saëns and Mozart, it has an Adagio modeled on the slow movement of the latter's Clarinet Quintet. "I'm composing a concerto for you," Ravel told his friend Marguerite Long. "Do you mind if it ends, pianoissimo with trills?" (When, in the event, it does so that he decided to play the piece himself and spent weary hours practicing Chopin and Liszt. Finally he gave up and entrusted the task to Marguerite Long. She, disappointed at not being able to play the Left hand Concerto because her hands were not big enough, joyfully accepted. A short while after its first concert performance in 1932, Ravel went to the studio to oversee the premiere recording made under the direction of Pedro de Freitas Branco. There, ensconced in the control room, he made sure that the score was performed to his satisfaction. He proved to be quite ferocious: Marguerite Long recalled: "At two or three o'clock in the morning I was exhausted. Finally it was all over ... When Ravel materialised from the control room, ordering 'You must start again!' I could have killed him, but I did it all the same."

*Don Quichotte à Dulcinée* consists of three songs originally intended as part of an incidental music for Pabst's film *Don Quichotte*, starring Chaliapin with George Robey as Sancho Panza. This was the last music Ravel composed and was never used in the film. Each, submitted in a furry of lawsuits, eventually had a score by Jacques Ibert. The premiere recording was made ten days before the first performance at the Paris Opéra, with the composer present and worked as the session went on. Changes in tempo were made, and, before printing, the original *Chanson romanesque* became *Chanson romanesque*.

*Ronsard à son âme*, composed originally with piano accompaniment in 1923-4, was chosen to fill out the fourth side of the records. By this time Ravel was too ill to write, and Singher reports that he dictated the lightly-orchestrated score to Lucien Garban, proof-reader at the publishers Durand. Ravel subsequently, in a handsome gesture, asked Singher which of the two *Don Quichotte* songs he would like to have dedicated to him. The singer opted for *Chanson épique*. The singer opted for 'Of course, you've chosen the right one'.

— JAMES HARDING, 1995

## DON QUIJOTE A DULCINEA

**I. Chanson romanesque**
Said the rogue to the dame:
I love you madly, beautiful one
My heart is on fire
And my soul is sad
I drink to joy!
Joy is my only goal,
Which I attain straightway
When I drink!

**II. Chanson épique**
Holy St. Michael, who grants me leave
To see and hear my love
Holy St. Michael, who hath deigned
To expose me to derision
Holy St. Michael, come down, I beg
With St. George to this shrine
Of thy blue mantled Madonna!
With a leg from Saint Michael, one from
And its roast is purely, its roast in jelly.
As it modestly and chastely.
My holy St. George St. George and St. Michael,
The angel watching over my eye,
And you, St. Michael, protect my eye,
O fair-mounted Madonna!
Amen.

**III. Chanson à boire**
Foin du bâtard, illustre Dame,
Qui pour me perdre à vos doux yeux
Dit que l'amour et le vin vieux
Mettent en deuil mon cœur, mon âme!
Je bois à la joie!
La joie est le seul but
Où je vais droit
Lorsque j'ai bu!

## RONSARD À SON ÂME

Amelète Ronsardelette,
Mignonnellette, doucelette,
Très chère hôtesse de mon corps,
Tu descends là-bas faiblette,
Pâle, maigrelette, seulette,
Dans le froid royaume des morts,
Toutefois simple, sans remords
De meurtre, poison et rancune,
Méprisant faveurs et trésors
Tant enviés par la commune.
Passant, j'ai dit: suis ta fortune,
Ne trouble mon repos: je dors!

**Pierre de Ronsard**

## RONSARD TO HIS SOUL

Dear soul of Ronsard,
Beloved sweet soul,
Most dear denizen of my body,
Now thou dost descend weak,
Pale, lean and lonely,
To the cold kingdom of the dead,
Still simple, free of remorse
For murder, poison and spite,
Scorning favours and treasures
So much envied by the common herd.
Passer-by, I have said: follow your fortune;
Do not disturb my rest: I am asleep!
