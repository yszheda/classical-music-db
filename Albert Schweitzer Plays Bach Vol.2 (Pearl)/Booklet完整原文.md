### Schweitzer plays Bach Volume II

Albert Schweitzer made a voyage to Europe in 1954 from his African hospital and missionary work in Lambaréné, Gabon, for a recital at the request of his record company. His concert career had been in abeyance for 12 years due to recording a good part of Bach's major works for organ, which had made him internationally famous. (The albums of shellac discs under the Columbia Masterworks label.) This event caused considerable talk and led to recitals and lectures on a stay extending from 1954 to 1956. The Volume I-Gei/GM 9959 and in 1957, Volume 2-GM 9960. Albert Schweitzer chose to play close to an exceptional and historic organ which is particularly familiar to him.

At the suggestion of the University of Strasbourg, he suggested the possibility of recording the St. Aurélien Church in Strasbourg, whose organ is ideal in both the acoustics and voicing, as well as its the position of this organ, located in an out-of-the-way part of the city, off the Waisenstrasse, in a medium sized room. The tonal beauties of the organ are kept the Silbermann organ in St. Aurélien is specially fitted for Schweitzer's interpretation of Bach.

The organ had undergone reconstruction by the late Frédéric Haas, whom Schweitzer considered "the most talented of contemporary organ builders of our time," a sign of this being that the Haas family included organ builders and organists for five generations, and from an early age the Doctor taught him how to proceed with the construction.

The organiste titulaire to the sessions, Louis-Eduard Schlotter provides an account of the conditions during the famous recordings:

He aims at more than this, however, for he is sparing of ideas, and so creates a tone-spectrum of his own in order to give to such a speech element the widest possible ramifications. To project his innovative way with a chorale into sound on his preferred instrument, Bach had to work out problems of organ building. His approach to the registration of an organ was the result of his considerations.
