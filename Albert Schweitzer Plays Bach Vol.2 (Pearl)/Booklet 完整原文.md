## Albert Schweitzer plays Bach Volume II

Albert Schweitzer made a rare voyage to Europe, leaving his Africa hospital and missionary work in 1935 to travel throughout the continent. His record company asked the Doctor to devote time to recording a good part of Bach's massy works for organ, which were published in three sets of albums of shellac discs under the auspices of their Bach Society. This event came about in between his reigns and lectures on a stay in London in November 1935, then in the volume I-Gemm CD 9959 and in Alsace in October 1936. The company chose an exceptional and historic organ also familiar to him.

A contemporary account mentions that "he suggested the possibility of transferring the (second set) to the St. Aurelia Church in Strasbourg. The experts from Columbia found conditions ideal in this church, located in an out-of-the-way street behind the Wiesenhäuserstrasse. In a medium room with excellent acoustics the benches could be arranged so that they would not possibly sound well; but they wondered greatly when they observed that the organ sounded as in its best, though the effect was of an unusual kind that could not be heard. They have produced by their own style of registration."

Schweitzer's own registration was based on plenum: "The effect was of an unusual kind that could not be heard. The effect was established by plenum transparency and establishing an architectural hierarchy. When cantus firmus is written in a third remote manual line as in Liebster Jesu, wir sind hier (Track 8), 'it often comes down to your ordinary advantage of an oboe or clarinet colouring. Wonderful effects and blending can be obtained by using a small mixture in the swell and adding couplers for the full organ tone.' This chorale preludes in Schweitzer's style should be played the most delicately of all."

Once having established the sonic context for the individual chorale, Schweitzer then studied the appropriate colouring, and the most important element for an instrumental medium in which dynamics are absent and any variances in legato and detached fingerwork have an immediate effect on the phrasing and articulation of the material.

---

The first volume of his writings on Bach underscores the structural differences between Bach's organ and clavier works and explains the idiomatic reasons behind the phrasing and fugue subjects. The method of planned, sustained voicing in Bach's fugue writing is heard in his phrasing with melodic contour to the nature of the subject, its registral shifts and thematic accompaniment, while the chorale prelude, linked to vocal phrasing, has the accompanying counterpart treated according to the articulation which serves to call attention to his text setting, without venturing onto during harmonic terrain. Schweitzer is guided by the music as a guide to recording details in which Bach's musical rhetoric surfaces, illuminating these diminutive luminaries in Bach's compositional firmament.

## Essay

The Silbermann organ in St. Aurelia is specially fitted for Schweitzer's interpretation of Bach.

The organ had undergone restoration by the organ builder Frédéric Härpfer, whom Schweitzer considered "the most nobly worthy artisan in the guild of builders of our time," a sign of great esteem as Schweitzer's own family included organ builders and from an early age the Doctor understood the art and science of organ construction.

As a visiting musical journalist to the sessions, Louis-Édouard Schaeffer provided an account of Schweitzer playing in the Bach recordings.

"I have surprised him in the midst of his work, in shirt sleeves. He has even taken off his vest here in the warm autumn air so cold as this. I see again the imposing head that resembles one of a Titan, the powerful form with broad shoulders which seems to me the body even heavier and more crammed with energy than ever.

Before the organ over the church pews hangs the microphone; it carries the tones of the organ to the recording apparatus for all the society. Here the wax disc turns; and here the needle scratches the organ tones in the surface. Schweitzer looks like a thick, deep yellow, honey cake. You are surprised that he is at one of the most difficult places where Albert Schweitzer is; as he took me next to the choir loft with him. About half the piece he is about to play is executed; only the pedals. He supports himself on the console with both hands, and plays with assurance and energy the difficult foot pedals at once or twice together. The telephones the sacristy that he is ready. The organ is ready. After reception there puts on a new disc and lowers the needle. Now a muffled bell besides the console gives the signal.... The organ begins.

Another muffled signal comes from the sacristy; the transcription is finished. Schweitzer does not seem completely satisfied, and we go down to listen to it. He notes a couple of minor changes he wants to make, then goes up to the organ for a second rendition, which finally satisfies him.

The registration is typical for Albert Schweitzer begins a Bach Adagio (Chorale Prelude). How smoothly this tone, how gently the sounds flow. When one hears the warm, sweetly bass, the impression seems to be mysteriously drawn from this purest and freed from the bond of matter."

To the "elegant slowness" of the cantabile he heard expressed. Schweitzer lends the last measure of his art to the cantabile; soft wave its full resonance, soft splendor, and resounding sonority. Because of this the transcription is a couple of minutes too long. What I felt as rapturously and without any total impression while I was beside the organ became a certain surprise when sitting down in the sacristy to hear the transcription. I felt the delicate motifs of the Adagio, its magic lustre and restrained fire, flow happily into this work. How beautifully the basses sound! Yes, nods the doctor, 'it turns out well.'

"At a long bench in the sacristy sits a music critic from London. When the music before him he follows the transcription. He exclaims at one place and asks: 'Why do you play a trill just here?' 'Look,' explains Schweitzer earnestly, 'here is a trill, and here is one; therefore there must be one also here. Bach was too indolent to draw it in. You must remember that Bach wrote everything just for himself.'

Coldness creeps into the church, disturbs the organ's functioning. Before the day ended, Schweitzer went up into the loft no more than three discs. Back next morning, he began pursuing at half past nine, covering several Preludes and Fugues, and more Chorale Preludes.

While Schweitzer selected several Preludes and Fugues, it was the Chorale Preludes that his interpretative art attained its height. The synthesis of Bach's chorale melody and Bach's resting there is an equidistant projection of abstract elements from his musical use of 'Word-painting' caught Schweitzer's sensibility to a far greater degree than any other genre in the organ literature. Schweitzer wrote a paragraph on length on the Chorale Preludes in his two-volume work on Bach, and in his preface to the edition of the organ works which he produced in collaboration with Charles-Marie Widor.

While recording primarily as a composer and literary historian providing over the available full masterpiece in St. Sulpice, Paris, Widor carried out a substantial work revolving organ music with Gabriel Fauré. Widor founded the Paris Bach Society, which set up the cause for presenting Bach's Cantatas, an ignored genre. As a conductor, Widor had a rare and influential performance of the Saint Matthew Passion in 1905 with the Concordia Society, again in Paris. He was also a profound musician and active propagator on behalf of Bach's music. Sweeping understanding and sharpened his detailed investigation into Bach's style and significance. With regard to the organ chorale, he writes on the evolution it underwent in Bach's hands.

"In these chorale preludes, Bach appears to have culminated all the forms of Palestrina, Johann Pachelbel, and Reinken. Towards the end of the Weimar period, however, when he became independent of his masters and produces a type of his own - the chorale prelude - the Orgelbüchlein. In this the melody is used as a cantus firmus, in unaltered and uninterrupted, usually in the uppermost voice, sounding in pitch an independently conceived motive, not derived from any of the lines of the fugue, but prompted by the text of the chorale; and embodying the idea that Bach regarded as characteristic for music and expressible in musical terms. Thus, in the chorale preludes of the Orgelbüchlein, not only the text are both represented at the same time; the cantus firmus being poetically illustrated, by means of the characteristic motives. In a footnote, Schweitzer adds:

"Regarded from purely the formal standpoint, it is a variety of the Bach'sche genre of small chorale fantasia."

"Here Bach has realised the ideal of the chorale prelude. The method is the most simple imaginable and at the same time the most perfect. Nowhere is the Dürer-like character of his musical style so evident as in these small chorale preludes. Simply by precision and characteristic quality of the line of the contrapuntal motive he expresses, as may be said, and so makes clear the relation of the music to the text whose title it bears."

"The Orgelbüchlein is thus not only of significance in the history of the development of the chorale prelude, but it is one of the greatest achievements in music. Never before had any one expressed the idea of pure tone painting; nor afterwards did any one undertake to do it so thoroughly. At the same time the essence of Bach's organ comes clearly into view for the first time in this work. He is not satisfied with formal perfection alone but he must also express.
