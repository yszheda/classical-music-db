### The French Piano School — An Introduction by Charles Thorrell

FOR SOME 150 YEARS a distinctively French piano style was nourished at the Paris Conservatoire. During the first half of the nineteenth century Paris was the centre of pianistic activity in Europe: the finest pianomakers, composers and publishers vying for the attention of a musical public. It didn't take too long for the star-system Conservatoire to become infamous, with its faculty made up mostly of distinguished graduates since students would in turn become the next generation of teachers. At first, foreign students were not allowed, and even Liszt was turned away. The repertoire was quite narrow, and all students competing for prizes at the end of the year had to play the same required piece. A premium was placed on fast, clean and brilliant finger work (le jeu perlé) and on a transparent and unforced sound that was never marred by excessive pedal, vanity or vulgarity. This is reproduced ideally by the fingers and wrists rather than the arms or shoulders. Early recordings by Camille Saint-Saëns, François Planté, Louis Diémer, Isidor Philipp and Marguerite Long are classic examples of this style.

But the piano school did not operate in complete isolation. Links with composers and pianists both at home and abroad abounded – Saint-Saëns and Planté performed with Liszt; Philipp studied with Saint-Saëns but with Chopin's student Georges Mathias; Cortot and Magda Tagliaferro were closely associated with Fauré; and Robert Casadesus and Vlado Perlemuter worked with Ravel – these ties are among the most valuable within this tradition.

Diémer and Philipp were the pre-eminent teachers at the beginning of the twentieth century. Diémer's students included such very different pianists as Alfred Cortot, Yves Nat, Robert Casadesus, Lazare-Lévy, Edouard Risler, Marie-François Gaillon and Victor Staub. Philipp's students also showed stylistic variety, with Paul Loyonnet, Jeanne-Marie Darré, Monique de la Bruchollerie and Gabriele Naumes sounding every different. And there is the great Alfred Brendel, the first American to win a first prize at the Conservatoire, who represents an entirely different line, having been a pupil of Charles-Louis Hanon's student Isidor Philipp.

But Harenton later studied in Vienna with Theodor Leschetizky and in Berlin with Karl Heinemann (no relation to the pianist Heinrich Neuhaus), and she may have been following a trend for French-style pianists to do postgraduate study elsewhere. Risler, for example, worked with three of Liszt's students, Breithaupt and with Karl von Stieglitz; Cortot worked briefly with Anton Rubinstein, and Loyonnet studied with Martinius Stavenhagen.

Inevitably the old French school became diluted by the 1950s, due to the dissemination of other performing styles through recordings, radio, television, international competition and increased ease of travel. Today, even foreign musicians are permitted to teach at the Conservatoire.

This series of recordings hopes to give an in-depth picture in sound of the many pianists who represented this school at its peak in the early twentieth century and whose recordings have been neglected in the CD era.

### The Recordings: Volume 2 Chopin, Debussy, Milhaud & Ravel | ROGER NICHOLS

ANY FRENCH PIANIST wishing to make a career always had to play Chopin. Not that said the manner of playing changes greatly over the end of the nineteenth century from being free (otherwise sometimes to be blamed for it) and to the one that observed the authority of the text and aimed to find its purity and completion. Though Marguerite Long tended to belong, partly from the era she hailed to the second group, in her reading of the F minor Fantasy, for example, she has some nice flights of the opening with very little rubato. Thereafter inner parts are occasionally hard to hear, but never to the detriment of sense, while a few added lower octaves in the bass are the only quirks of the ancient manner.

Chopin's 21 Mazurkas do not in general demand a virtuosic technique, but their structure are often difficult to get structure, so perhaps Long was wise to record only one of them! The F sharp minor Op 59/3 allows for playfulness, which Long is happy to go along with: the tone is intimate with no grandstanding, and with enjoyment in the two passages where the notes have to get stuck in the groove. Not everyone will approve her bursts of speed in it, but, for my part, they necessitate a presto rendering of the final poco a poco accelerando, which shows her fingerwork to good effect. In the D flat Waltz Op 70/3 she keeps within strict limits in the limpid touches of rubato.

For the Fantasy Impromptu my note reads 'This is her polite!' One takes her dexterity for granted, but beyond that, she exerts absolute control over the rhetoric, so that the occasional delay at the right hand after the left, another 'moment' mannerism, is exactly forgiven. Also on her path is the Bernaise, where the delivery of a right hand melody is a joy to hear from start to finish. Her perfection eludes her in a surprising way, and the way she takes a pause that falls to materialise, leading to a second or so of silence, is truly magical, if resolute. One explanation might be that she has momentarily overextended the pedal for effect. It should also be said that she may not have elapsed this performance for posterity, as it was made in Japan. Otherwise, the playing is superb. The same can be said for the Scherzo, especially notable for the way she finds shades of colour which, in the case, in the bolder A major section.

The most unlikely performance on this disc is the Barcarolle, where the figure of Debussy makes one feel very uneasy, and as one is related in her lifetime, we find her insistence that 'We must simply listen to Debussy's music' to have an idea of what Chopin must have been for his contemporaries in the audience. Barcarolle all of Debussyism already floats its association of the sea.

Chopin's Barcarolle is one of the most exquisite of his works. It has a delicate, beautiful melody, and a fascinating harmonic movement that takes you through a series of modulations that are both surprising and satisfying. The piece is written in 12/8 time, which gives it a gentle, rocking motion reminiscent of a gondola ride on the canals of Venice. The left hand provides a steady, wave-like accompaniment, while the right hand sings the main theme with expressive phrasing. The middle section features a more passionate and dramatic episode, with richer harmonies and more elaborate figuration. The piece concludes with a brilliant coda that showcases the pianist's technical skill while maintaining the poetic atmosphere throughout.
