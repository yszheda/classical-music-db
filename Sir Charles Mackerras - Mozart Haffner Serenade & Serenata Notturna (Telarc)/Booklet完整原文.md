## Page 2-3

The *Serenata notturna* was composed at Salzburg by Mozart in January, 1776. He wrote the *Haffner* Serenade there in June of the same year. This latter Serenade was written by the twenty-year-old Mozart for the marriage of Marie Elisabeth Haffner, daughter of the deceased Sigmund Haffner, at one time prominent merchant and Salzburg's Mayor, to the forwarding agent Franz Xaver Spaeth. Marie Elisabeth's brother, also Sigmund, ordered the piece and it was first performed in the garden of his summer residence in the Paris-Lodrongasse for the wedding celebrations of July 22, 1776. Mozart later wrote his *Haffner* Symphony for the same brother, probably for the occasion when Sigmund was granted a title (in 1782). The work is one of a number of exhurberant instrumental pieces turned out by the composer during 1775-1780, the years prior to the time Mozart himself left Salzburg service for what the city's angry Archbishop considered to be his inappropriately independent ways ("with a kick on my arse...by order of our worthy Prince Archbishop").

The opening movement is particularly grand and elaborate in keeping with an important civic occasion. The movement's highly dramatic development section explores conventions often associated in music with the depiction of storms (we assume here not marital ones-to-come!).

There is a prominent part for a violinist in 3 (movements 2, 3, 4) of the work's 8 movements. One might think of this Serenade as a hybrid work, part symphony and part violin concerto, but it would not be appropriate to do so. The Serenade is as legitimate a period type as any Concerto or Symphony and simply happens to abound in Concertante elements. Works such as this and the *Posthorn* Serenade are essentially concertos for orchestra, since, in the manner of Bartok's work, there are often solo instrumental passages along the way. (Symphonies were often extracted by Mozart from his Serenades. Mozart made a Symphony-NMA IV:11/vii, 31 — out of this one by eliminating the solo violin movements and adding a new timpani part.)

## Page 4-5

The soloist at the first performance of the Serenade may have been the violinist Franz Xaver Kolb, who also may have played the concerto passages at another celebration described to Mozart by his father in 1777:

He played on the fiddle your concerto (one of five Mozart wrote for him in 1775) and your Nachtmusik (probably one or more movements from the Serenade) and, as the music was so much praised and won extraordinary acclamation and applause, he announced "You have been hearing the compositions of a good friend who is no longer with us." Whereupon they all cried out; "What a pity we have lost him!" The concert took place in Elizenberger's hall. When it was over, they all got drunk and shouldered one another in processions round the room, knocking against the lustres (cut glass candleholders) or rather, against the large chandelier which hangs from the middle of the ceiling, so that they smashed the center bowl and other pieces, which will now have to be sent to Venice to be replaced.

Such is the power of music. Kolb played it again in 1778, according to Leopold Mozart,

*in front of Herr von Meyer's house... We listened to it across the water. It sounded charming.*

It was indeed the usual thing to play such music out of doors. Mattheson wrote earlier (1739), "Nowhere can one better hear... a serenata than on the water during calm weather: for there one can use various instruments with their full strength which would sound too violent and deafening in a room, such as trumpets, drums, horns, etc." During spring and wintertime, though, seasonal demands might place players inside.

Hubert Unverricht tells us in *New Grove* that "A march or movement of march-like character often appears at the beginning or end of (a serenade), or sometimes at both, to serve for the entrance or exit of musicians". In modern times such a tradition is invoked by something like Stravinsky's opening and closing March for his opera *Renard*, played by the musicians already sitting in place (the vocal soloists enter and exit to the March). The time of composition of the *Serenata notturna*, January, is probably already enough to indicate that its first performances were indoors, simply because of the season of the year. But another indication the musicians had to be playing it while already on stage (and probably indoors, during the season's Carnival celebrations) is the opening Marcia's timpani part: it is difficult enough to march playing a cello or double bass, but in the case of timpani, impossible. (In processions, a timpani player could play a pair of drums mounted upon horseback. Such a practice is still been observed occasionally in traditional German military musical setups.) Indoors, the timpani part for Mozart's most unusual Serenade must have rung out splendidly.

The *Serenata notturna* is an antiphonal work. A string quartet (2 violins, viola and double bass) is pitted against the main string body (plus timpani). This arrangement of players, unusual by Mozart's time, is reminiscent of the earlier Baroque concerto grosso. It is not the only time Mozart tried out such fanciful effects during his life in Salzburg. During the same year (1776) he also composed the *Notturno* for four orchestras (KV 286/269a). Contemporary temporary works which survive in the repertory for string quartet and string orchestra are rare: Elgar's *Introduction and Allegro* for string quartet and string orchestra, and Schoenberg's reworking of Handel material in his *String Quartet Concerto* with orchestra, and not much more. Works for string orchestra and timpani are equally rare. Sibelius' *Rakastava* and Waxman's *Sinfonietta* are the only two which readily come to mind.

There are three Minuets in the *Haffner* Serenade and one in the *Serenata notturna*. All four are in turns galant and rustic.

There is a rocking triplet figure underlying the Trio of the *Haffner*'s Menuetto Galante which might strike a familiar note to Prague musicians. The figure is sometimes found in Smetana's music, for example, in *Sarka*, from *Ma Vlast*.

## Page 6-7

Pizzicato passages appear in both Serenades, notably in a short accompaniment pattern toward the close of the *Haffner*'s 6th movement (Andante, m.'s 181/8), and fleetingly in the opening and closing movements of the *Serenata notturna*. Grove says that "any movement with an accompaniment on plucked strings (suggesting the guitar, lute or mandolin) carried serenade connotations." The image is that of the lover-serenader (Don Giovanni, perhaps) singing a courting song (in a "courtyard", usually) to "a beloved or person of rank" (located in an apartment above). Mozart occasionally quotes popular airs in the Serenades and in like works of this period and such quotations might be thought of in the above context.

The refrain of the Andante mentioned above (it first appears in m. 38) is a particularly telling wisp of melody—it haunts the memory long after the Serenade itself has played out its last notes.

During Mozart's time a Serenade would customarily have been performed "on quiet and pleasant nights" about 9 p.m. The *Haffner* Serenade would properly have been played at such an hour. In contrast, a *Notturno* (Grove: "a similar kind of work") would usually have been played about 11 p.m.

—William Malloch
