## TANEYEV AND THE PIANO
by Anastasia Belina

Sergei Ivanovitch Taneyev (1856-1915) was one of the most interesting and least understood of all Russian composers. Rachmaninov described him as "a person of colossal intellect, a musician of the greatest talent, an original personality with individuality and character – a peak of the musical Moscow". A complete pianist, theorist, composer and pedagogue, he is even today considered one of the founders of Russian music theory.

Taneyev became a good friend of his teacher, Pyotr Tchaikovsky, and passed frequently all the duties of the latter to his composition classes at the Moscow Conservatory. The young composer was fascinated by Taneyev's talent and his ability to improvise. Taneyev was one of the few musicians who could play at sight the most difficult piano works. His talent for improvisation was legendary. He could improvise on any theme suggested by the audience.

Taneyev entered the Conservatory at the age of nine. His first composition teacher was Nikolai Rubinstein. Taneyev was an excellent student and graduated with a gold medal. He was the first student in the history of the Conservatory to win the gold medal in both performance and composition.

After graduation, Taneyev continued his studies with Anton Rubinstein. He was a brilliant pianist and could play any piece at sight. His technique was flawless and his tone was beautiful. He was a master of all styles and could play anything from Bach to Brahms.

Taneyev was a close friend of Tchaikovsky and was present at the premiere of his Piano Concerto No. 1. He was the first to play the solo part of Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 1 in Moscow. He also helped Tchaikovsky with the orchestration of his Fourth Symphony.

In 1881, after Rubinstein's death, Taneyev became the director of the Moscow Conservatory. He tried to reform the Conservatory and improve the quality of education. However, his reforms were not well received by the faculty and he resigned in 1885.

After leaving the Conservatory, Taneyev devoted himself to composition and theory. He wrote several important theoretical works, including "Strict Counterpoint" and "Convertibility of Contrapuntal Figures". He also composed many works for piano, including sonatas, variations, and preludes.

Taneyev's piano music is characterized by its complexity and intellectual depth. He was a master of counterpoint and his works often feature intricate polyphonic textures. His piano writing is demanding and requires a high level of technical skill.

Despite his talents, Taneyev's music was not well understood during his lifetime. He was often criticized for being too academic and dry. However, his music has been reappraised in recent years and is now recognized as an important contribution to Russian music.

## Concerto for Piano and Orchestra in E flat Major (1876)

The sixteen-year-old Taneyev began composing his only piano concerto in 1876, a year after his eighteenth birthday. This work was dedicated to his teacher Nikolai Rubinstein and given the Moscow premiere of Tchaikovsky's First Piano Concerto in Moscow and helped correct the score for its publication. He thus knew the piece intimately, and it is not surprising that certain elements of his own concerto are similar. It is nevertheless very much an original composition – the chords in the first cadenza of Taneyev's concerto, inspired by the opening chords of Tchaikovsky's First Piano Concerto, for example.

After completing the first two movements of his concerto, Taneyev showed it to Tchaikovsky, who suggested certain improvements to the orchestration. Taneyev travelled to St Petersburg to visit Tchaikovsky and Anton Rubinstein, César Cui and Rimsky-Korsakov in St Petersburg. Upon receiving adverse criticism from the last three composers, Taneyev left the concerto unfinished.

The concerto was later orchestrated by Shebalin, and published in 1953 in a critical edition by the Soviet music editor Pavel Lamm.

In this early work, the hallmarks of Taneyev's mature style are inherent in its monothematic development, and the complete absence of virtuosity for its own sake. Its passionately romantic Allegro opens with an unusually constructed theme (Ex. 1).

**Ex. 1**
*Allegro*

After the orchestral introduction, the piano enters with a brilliant cascade of semi-quavers, spanning four octaves. The soaring lyricism of the second subject, clearly inspired by Tchaikovsky's melodic language, contrasts with the sombre main theme.

The second movement, *Andante finafle*, lasts only about six minutes, in contrast to the long 25-minute Allegro. Its main theme (Ex. 2) appears in the piano, pianissimo.

**Ex. 2**
*Andante funèbre*

In spite of the criticism of those of Taneyev's contemporaries who thought that the Allegro was too long and monotonous, the concerto speaks with a passion, honesty, and expression that announces an important stage in Taneyev's creative output: the young composer was beginning to find his own style.

## Prelude in F major (1894-95)

This prelude is the early survivor of three preludes written for the Russian virtuoso Alexander Siloti, who performed the last two in a concert in Antwerp in 1894. Whereas the Piano Concerto is a work of a recent graduate, the composer of this piece was an experienced and respected author of a monumental cantata, three symphonies, six string quartets and an epic opera. The Prelude is bright and lively; it is separated into a number of distinct sections, which are identified by changes in tempo (Allegro animato, Vivo, Più vivo, Poco meno vivo), dynamics, and texture. The rhythmic complexity of some passages does not impinge on the textural clarity, which at times shows affinities with Brahms' piano writing. Taneyev treats the piano as an orchestral instrument, as can be heard particularly in the Vivo, where every part has its own distinctive voice and function. The quality of this prelude makes the loss of the other two even more regrettable.

## Lullaby (1881)

This short piece in B flat major takes its inspiration from folk-song: indeed, the melody seems to have been written for the voice, and could be easily provided with lyrics. The simple rhythm and pacifying rocking accompaniment directly evokes an image of a mother singing her child to sleep. In the opening part the melody appears in the left hand, accompanied by soft, chiming chords in the right. The middle section, in C minor, introduces the melody in the right hand with a few simple changes. In the final part, the original slowly dies away in the major key that oscillates between the augmented and major triad: the child is now peacefully asleep.

## Theme with Variations (1874)

Written as a composition exercise, this work was undoubtedly inspired by Tchaikovsky's *Three original etudes*, composed a year earlier. The opening folk-like theme is played in the piano class of Rubinstein. This early work paints a picture of an eighteen-year-old composer who was just beginning to test himself in the art of composition but who, even at this early stage showing unusual talent. Although already being a skilled performer, Taneyev shied away from flashy keyboard pyrotechnics: this *Allegro*, like much of his piano music, has many brilliant and difficult technical passages, but they are all necessary and natural expressions of the musical ideas – none of Taneyev's piano music was written in order to display technical prowess for its own sake.

## Andantino Semplice (date unknown)

Although the precise date of this composition is unknown, the *Andantino Semplice* in B minor could have been written around 1876-78, since it shares a number of traits with the Piano Concerto, particularly imitative and fugal fragments. These techniques, particularly used closely with Romantic music, allude to the works of Anton Rubinstein, particularly to his Piano Concerto in E minor, which Taneyev learned in 1874-75. The opening motive in the right hand is imitated in the left, followed by the imitation of the second phrase in the next section. He did not escape Brahms here either, particularly in the harmonic language in the allusions to the two keys separated by thirds on either side – D and G major – of the tonic B minor.

## Repose (Elégie) (1880)

This beautiful, Chopinesque elegy, in ABA form with a short coda, was written when Taneyev, already a teacher at the Conservatoire, was 24. The A parts are composed in E major, and the appearance of A flat at the beginning of section B is quite unexpected. This is an unusual key change, bringing a sense of repose and relaxation to the middle section which, with its use of the Dorian scale, sounds almost archaic. After the second A part a brief coda brings the work to a close with a simple soaring arpeggio, dissipating into nothingness.

## Marche (1879)

The March is built on a rhythmic figure shown in Ex. 7. ## Allegro in E flat Major (1873-74)

One of Taneyev's earliest works for solo piano, this single movement was written as a composition exercise in sonata-form. The main theme opens the work, appearing as a soaring twelve-bar phrase (Ex. 5).

**Ex. 5**

The lyrical second subject (Ex. 6) is close to the melodic language of Tchaikovsky's music.

**Ex. 6**

Taneyev only completed one movement – a well-crafted Allegro spanning 263 bars. Its music is dreamy and romantic, with clear, lucid textures and without any tension or conflict. In many places the Allegro shows the influence of Schumann, whose music Taneyev often introduced the new works of his contemporaries and former students in harmony and analysis classes. Taneyev continued to teach composition even after resigning from his position as Director. His final resignation came in 1905, as a protest against the unfair treatment meted out by his successor, Vassili Safonov, to the students who sympathised with the failed revolution of the same year.

Having been Tchaikovsky's student and closest friend, Glieré and Rachmaninov, as well as the critic Leonid Sabaneyev, the pianist Alexander Goldenweiser – a founder of the Central Music School in Moscow – and many other brilliant musicians. Although never wealthy, Taneyev continued to take paying piano students. He made a difference in their lives, giving them a well-to-do or a poor family. And, as he did not rely on performances for income (he received a monthly salary from the Conservatoire), Taneyev was able to hand out lessons for free to the most gifted students. But not all felt comfortable with the arrangement and, as Sabaneyev recalled, for his student Taneyev's flat was 'a sort of free canteen': "He offered help with various day-to-day errands and always invited Taneyev to tea after the lessons, feeding him cakes and sweets, which he adored."

Despite the immense workload at the Conservatoire, Taneyev wrote a number of both original and Russian-language translations of foreign classics. He composed not only piano pieces and romances but at that time mainly worked on his composition and harmony exercises. The first work that brought him recognition was a cantata, *Ioann Damaskin* [John of Damascus], written in 1883-84. It was also the first composition to which Taneyev gave an opus number. He continued to compose at a feverish pace, writing pieces for piano, chamber ensembles, a large number of pieces for choir, romances, a concert fantasy for piano and orchestra and a Wagnerian opera, *Oresteia*. Having tried himself in almost every genre, Taneyev gained a modicum of fame and respect, being awarded several masterpieces, among them his cantata *After the Reading of a Psalm* and the C minor symphony (No. 4, although it was originally published as his Symphony No. 1).

Taneyev's influence on Russian composers was far-reaching. He recognised musical talent and took young Prokofiev and recommended that his parents hire Glieré (then Taneyev's student) as a teacher of theory and composition. Some of his students, such as Nikolai Zhilyayev, Alexander Goldenweiser, became respected teachers at the Moscow Conservatory. He taught such figures as Shebalin, Kabalevsky, Khachaturian, Lazar Berman, Ilya Ginsburg and Nikolai Kapustin. Taneyev's influences can be heard in the harmonic language of Scriabin's early piano pieces, in the well-crafted piano concertos of Scriabin's and Medtner's, and in the well-crafted chamber music of Stravinsky.

## The Composer's Birthday (1892) for narrator and piano, four hands

This musical greeting was written by Taneyev for Tchaikovsky's 52nd birthday, on 25 April 1892. As a ballet-parody, it was crafted from a number of themes from Tchaikovsky's compositions: the operas *Mazeppa*, *The Queen of Spades*, *The Maid of Orleans*, *Iolanta*, *Eugeni Onegin*, the ballets *The Nutcracker*, *Swan Lake*, *The Sleeping Beauty* and *Orchestral Suite No. 4*, the poem *Francesca da Rimini*, and the *Second Symphony*. The themes, superimposed, form two-, then three-, four-, and five-part counterpoint; the work is written on two, three and four staves. A text, explaining the action of the ballet, is written above the score:

First scene. The composer, in silver wreath, accepts congratulations from the close friends. Maria enters (first theme will be that of her homecoming [O] may, my, by badesh near family rushes (O), my does not will be the start of your homecoming!). Then enters a prince, Taninha based [theme from Gemina's aria from *Eugeni Onegin*, 'Lyubov voi vozvrai pokony' (Love conquers all age)]. The two waltzes, maining just that he will interpret a separate ballet [theme from *Mazeppa*, theme from *Francesca da Rimini*, Op. 56]. Maria starts a conversation with him. *Francesca da Rimini* flies in on a cloud (Francois's theme from *Francesca da Rimini*). She vanishes after noting: Herman (aria 'Moya li dush' prishel'! (Can I survive a day?)) from *The Queen of Spades*. A little shepherd enters. All those who are present notice a crane of a very large size [theme of the folk-song 'about a Crane' from the *Second Symphony*]. Herman thinks of Lisa, the prince of Tatiana, and a knight of Iolanta [theme from Herman's aria 'Ia inenyu shto ne znayu' (I do not know her name') from *The Queen of Spades* and from Voldemont's aria 'Chudny pervenets tvorenya' (Wondrous first-born Creation') from *Iolanta*]. The second prince, Lisa's fiancé, enters with the first prince, and remains with him until the end of the evening [theme from the *Maid of Orleans*]. The ballet ends with congratulations to Tatiana from Evgeni Onegin]. Unexpectedly, a group of children storms in. Children's gallop [theme from *The Nutcracker*].

Second and last scene: A group of cranes flies in and begins to circle around with the children. Curtain.

This delightful miniature, a mere 76 bars long, is proof that Taneyev did not only produce 'academic' music; it allows his musical laughter to reach modern ears after a century's confinement in Russian archives.

Since it is not clear whether Taneyev intended the text to be read aloud or silently, this CD presents the piece in two versions, one with narration and the other without. This edition has been realised from Taneyev's manuscript (reproduced on p. 14) by Joseph Banowetz.

## Quatre Improvisations (with Arensky, Glazunov and Rachmaninov) (1896)

In the autumn of 1896 Taneyev went to St Petersburg to oversee the preparations for the première of his new opera *Oresteia*. During this visit his friend Arensky invited him, Glazunov and Rachmaninov to dinner, after which the four friends decided to write something just for fun. They each took a page of manuscript paper, wrote a line of music and passed it onto another person until the page was completed, thus completing four improvisations on four sheets of paper. These pieces show the extent of mutual understanding and mastery of the four composers – the sections are carefully crafted in order to smoothly flow from one to another.

The first three improvisations were started by Arensky, Glazunov and Rachmaninov respectively. The dreamy, Chopinesque first variation is built on a simple dotted-dhythm figure; the second is a galloping polka, and the third is a brilliant *Allegro scherzando*. The last improvisation is the most varied, musically and rhythmically. Taneyev began it *Largo*, with dramatic *forte* octaves, followed by the *piano* melody high in the right hand. Arensky continued with a waltz, Rachmaninov and Glazunov with a march, and Taneyev rounded everything off by returning to the original tempo and writing a conclusion reminiscent of Bach's fugal aludes.

## Performer Biographies

**Joseph Banowetz** has been described by *Fanfare* (USA) as "a giant among keyboard artists of our time", by *Russia's News* (Moscow) as "a magnificent virtuoso who amazed the public by his deep understanding of the composer's spirit", and by *Bach Magazine* (Warsaw) as "a virtuoso of the noblest sense of the word". Banowetz is a graduate with first prize of the Akademie für Musik und Darstellende Kunst in Vienna; his instructors included Carl Friedberg (a pupil of Clara Schumann) and György Sándor (a pupil of Béla Bartók).

Banowetz has been heard as recitalist and orchestral soloist in over 35 countries, with performances with the St Petersburg (formerly Leningrad) Philharmonic, the Budapest Symphony, the Prague and Bratislava Radio Orchestras, the National Symphony, the Concert Society Orchestra of Barcelona, the New Zealand Symphony (on a twelve-concert national tour), the Beijing Central Philharmonic, the Shanghai Symphony, and the Hong-Kong Philharmonic. In 1992 he was awarded the Liszt Medal by the Hungarian Liszt Society in Budapest, in recognition of his outstanding performances of Liszt and the Romantic literature.

Joseph Banowetz has made thirty compact discs for the labels Naxos, Marco Polo, Warner and Altanta labels, the works including the Tchaikovsky First Concerto, both Liszt Concertos and Totentanz, both d'Albert Concertos, the Huang Concerto No. 1, world-premiere recordings of all eight d'Albert Piano Ballades, and solo or piano and orchestra works by Bach, Balakirev, Busoni, Chopin, Debussy, Godowsky, Liszt, Mendelssohn, Rachmaninov, Rubinstein, Schubert, Schumann and Stevenson. One of the Rubinstein piano-and-orchestra series was named by *Fanfare* as an outstanding international release for 1993, and a similar distinction was given by the German Music Critics' Association for Banowetz's world-premiere recording of works by Balakirev.

After graduating from the Music School of the Leningrad Conservatory and the Music Academy in East Berlin, **Thomas Sanderling** started his career at the age of 24 as the Music Director of the Halle Opera. He appeared frequently with most of the East German institutions including the Dresden Staatskapelle, Leipzig Gewandhaus and Komische Oper Berlin, and won the German Critics' Prize.

He attracted the attention of Shostakovich, who asked him to give the East German premieres of his Thirteenth and Fourteenth symphonies and to make the first recording of the *Mikolajko Suite*, the composer's last orchestral work. As a direct result he became the assistant of Leonard Bernstein and Herbert von Karajan.

**Anastasia Belina** studied piano performance in Russia at the Shebalin School of Music in Omsk, and musicology in New Zealand at the University of Auckland. Her primary research interests are nineteenth-century Russian music and Wagner. She is currently writing a dissertation at the University of Leeds examining Wagnerian influences on Taneyev's opera *Oresteia*.

**Adam Wlodnicki** has performed and taught on five continents. He has recorded extensively for various labels, Centaur and Altante among them, and has made radio and TV recordings in a range of different countries. His concerts recordings with the Polish National Radio-Symphony Orchestra include music by contemporary Polish composers Kazimierz Serocki, Tadeusz Baird, and Jan Krenz. The CD release of *Piano Trios* by Robert Muszynski (with Robert Davidovici, violin, and Carter Enyart, cello) was chosen by a *Fanfare* critic as one of the top five recordings on the 'Want List 2004'.

In recent seasons Wlodnicki has appeared at prestigious international music festivals among them the Arundel Festival (England), Les Rencontres Internationales Frédéric Chopin in Nohant (France), the Chopin Festival in Mariánské Lázně (Czech Republic), and others. He has also performed and taught in Japan, the Czech Republic, China, Hong Kong, Korea, Israel, South Africa, Colombia, Italy, Austria, Bulgaria, Spain and Portugal – the critics describing him as a "stunning virtuoso" and a "musical star".

Since 1991 he has returned annually to his native Poland for highly successful tours. Wlodnicki has served on juries of international competitions and is performance editor for the edition of Paderewski's Complete Works: A Steinway Artist, Adam Wlodnicki is Professor of Piano at the University of North Texas, Denton.
