## Liner Notes by Egon E. Kenton

"Frenchmen have always worshipped the spoken word, its accent, meter, and timbre, and have never permitted it to yield either to music or to pantomime. As a consequence, the melodic invention of their musical dramas has had a reserve almost unknown to balance."

In these remarks, Curt Sachs summed up the gist, the essence of the French outlook in balance. But in order to understand the Herculean task fully knowingly took upon himself when he decided to write a French opera; in order to understand why Gluck (the only one who successfully did so) was tremendously successful, to be sure; nevertheless a large part of his French public listened to his operas with cold hostility, to understand why the Italian madrigal became popular in England but not in France; why the symphony (which in Germany and France was late and slow in seeping into French concert repertory; and why the survival of the diatonic tonal system was not an issue in twentieth-century France — for all these answers, we must look beyond what closer analysis of contrapuntal-harmonic considerations reveal.

Since in the foregoing we seem to have emphasized the role of national or racial differences, we must touch upon a curious and unexplained fact about this otherness of the French. The French are descendants of a mixed stock of Franks (conquering Germans but arriving 500 years earlier), the aboriginal Celts, and a sprinkling of Romans. There is nothing there to justify an outlook totally different from that of the English, Germans, Italians, Scandinavians. And yet, the outlook of the French in matters musical can be compared only to that of the ancient Greeks, with whom the French have no racial connections.

The opening words quoted above from Sachs, actually apply equally to the Greeks; considered from a sociological viewpoint as recitation — epic or dramatic; music embellished the word, but the word was the all-important element.

The medieval and early history of French music is essentially the history of French music. Polyphonic music was a French invention. The origin of musical drama goes back to medieval France: the mystery plays. Our first examples of secular vocal and instrumental music come from France. In France we must not forget the trouvères and the troubadours. The most important role in all Europe for more than 700 years was the motet, which owed its existence to a new motif (ditty) applied to the contrapuntal part or parts; to that the troubadour and trouvère sang the text denoted the music; and that the instrumental music was dance music, and an embellishment of rhythmic body movements.

For the French, a "deep" or "inner" sentiment, a passionate outburst or fluttering, throbbing expression (pathos) in public is insupportable and in bad taste. It is this reticence, this keeping from boring one's innermost emotions that explains the fact that Monteverdi's *Lamento d'Arianna* or *Lagrime dell'Amante sulla tomba dell'amata* found no echo in France, as was the case among Beethoven and his contemporaries in Germany. It explains also why one can search the depths of the soul that Beethoven set to music (without words), to the passionate accents and expression of Brahms; and to the unbridled frankness of Schubert and Schumann, without Italian coloratura vocalise, which the French consider unspiritual. A *Cavalleria rusticana* or an *Elektra* suits people who can come from France.

This reticence may also be seen in the trial of Baudelaire before a court of justice. And it can safely be said that Rimbaud remains unpopular in France to this day.

True, in the latter part of the nineteenth century French composers could not remain contemplative, not aloof: they included in their music, in their experiments with the harmonic innovations of Wagner, with different degrees of boldness.

Not all of them recognized that Wagner — in *Tristan* — had destroyed the traditional tonal system. But when questions arose, they did not try to find new answers for them. And not all contemporary composers limited themselves to his example. Debussy, who in his youth journeyed from Rome to Bayreuth to get the full impact, considered it only as one of the many ways to go on composing. He invented and used several other ones — pentatonic and whole-tone scale, modal harmony, etc. At any rate, there were Chopin and Liszt before Wagner, who extended and sometimes negated the traditional conventions of tonal music. Debussy fought against the spirit of Baudelaire's time, as well as his time.

William Austin quotes from a letter Busoni wrote in 1905 to his pupil Egon Petri: "A man who has walked, jumped or dived into the abyss is no longer in awe of the depths — he suits people who say to him on the brink and dare not go down." Busoni did jump into the abyss.

Gabriel Fauré was older than all the above-mentioned composers; he was born in 1845, and he lived to almost the end of 1924, at an age that have been reached by several of his contemporaries who jumped into the abyss. He was too much of a Frenchman: he certainly flirted with the abyss, but remained in safe distance from the brink. His music is characterized by a reserve in all melodic matters; it never loses the feeling of balance and good manners, but always kept firmly in control, a masterful counterpoint that never becomes scholastic, a form independent of classical tradition but always justifiable musically, and a clearheaded, unsentimental expressiveness, perfectly polished and serene.

Fauré was a pupil and admirer of Saint-Saëns. Although less versatile than his master, Fauré equally young has at the organ console, and as a true Frenchman he was attracted by the word, especially the vocal poem, and as one of the finest masters of French lyrical song. In the second half of the nineteenth century it was no longer possible in France to compose arias simple story operas and expect them to succeed; the language made singing possible for Fauré to write in the style of Bellini or Verdi. Hence, he composed only two operas, and not without much soul-searching and misgivings. It was also difficult to ignore chamber music. But chamber music can be considered chamber music only in the French sense, counterpoint of austere artistry, and a judicious use of surprising harmonic innovations go well with chamber music. The rapid evolution of music since the beginning of the twentieth century — a period of ten years — saw many changes. Fauré, the first to be influenced by German music. Harmonic innovations are no longer surprising today, and his first body of chamber music is almost totally neglected. But as the great innovators of the nineteenth century turned their trait into the new idiom of the twentieth century, the impressionists and sacrificed not only musical tradition but musical beauty as well, so the public will surely also feel surfeit with surprise, shock, and the dissonant novae, and turn back to the masters of restraint and classical masters: Brahms's *Requiem*, Bach's *St. Matthew Passion*, and finally to the ruler of the ballet. "Antonin me!" But Stravinsky spent his last two years orchestrating Bach's Preludes and Fugues, and we feel sure Bach still astonished him. Fauré, as were all composers of genius, was equally astonished by the masters of the past, and he also felt that art cannot stand still, and extended the frontiers of harmonic realm. But his very French aesthetics compelled him to keep his extension within bounds, to apply his innovations in soberly measured doses. The result is a style that astonishes us by its astute mixture of the new with the old; the old remains the point of departure and the ultimate goal, but with new colors so that they enliven by contrast the basic and always present diatonic tonal system, and thus remains always true to the ancient Greek ideal of measure and balance essential to beauty.

Fauré wrote in all musical forms except the symphony. After a few early attempts at chamber music throughout his life. He wrote his first piano quartet in 1879, and his string quartet in 1924, shortly before his death.

The Piano Quartet in C Minor, Op. 15, is undoubtedly his best-known work of chamber music, still popular today and frequently performed. It is in four movements, quite classical in form and procedure, but it is characteristic of the composer's mastery of counterpoint and romantically passionate thematic material. Its naturally the classic mode as does Haydn's, so that the limitations and counterpoint never become scholastic. They are carried out with the greatest of ease. A chain of two similar masters of ease and naturalness in counterpoint: Mendelssohn and Donizetti.

The movement starts right with the main theme — Fauré never resorted to an introductory measure — played by the strings over a widely arpeggiated accompaniment. Fauré accepted the basic difference in sound and character between the piano and stringed instruments, and never tried to make the piano sing long, sustained melodies. It was not necessary to suppose for him that the sound of strings cannot be imitated on the keyboard of a stringed instrument or a choir of these and giving them and the piano equal roles in a rich contrapuntal texture offered more than enough opportunity to achieve variety. It is fascinating to see how completely the roles of the two groups are interchanged. The piano is the protagonist, but incorporates all the luxuriant musical vegetation of the late romantic era. There is as much tumult and passion in the music as one finds in Brahms; and the question arises, whether Fauré knew this work by Mozart, Beethoven, or Brahms. Through a surprising series of modulations — enharmonic and other — we reach the relative major key of E-flat, and the lyrical second theme. After a coda in which the first theme reappears in the same key.

There follows an ingenious and interesting development section leading to the recapitulation, in which the themes appear in their classical predetermined keys, and the movement ends with a short coda, the conclusion enhanced — a la piano — in which only the pulse of the syncopated chords of the outset remains alive.

The second movement is a Scherzo, Allegro vivo in 6/8. After the pace has been set by pizzicato chords of the strings, the piano brings a twisting, twirling, dancy melody, somewhat like a folk tune, in which young people, possessed and impelled by the driving force of their amorous bodies, twist and turn, oblivious to everything around them, completely absorbed in their own self-breathing. The movement is in E-flat and the six-measure pizzicato preparation brings a quasi*recitativo* di IV/VI* modified subsequently in fascinating sequences of chords. The motor changes back and forth between 6/8 and 3/8 and 3/4 time, after a pause on the dominant seventh chord of the rhythm. This movement is not classically concise any longer, although very precisely placed in form: like Beethoven's scherzos, it approaches a rondo in its expansion. It has a Trio with a cantilena and sweeter strain. After a partial repetition of the Scherzo, this time, now in B-flat, through another quasi*ostinato* of IV/V-I. After the enchanting conclusion of the Scherzo, the recapitulation.

The *Adagio* returns to C minor. It is a three-part song in which the strings are never treated not as a choir but as individuals, entering in imitation of a prayer-like short melody over the pizzicato chords of the piano, cadenza-like prelude for the Adagio. From there on, the solo violin leads into a *cantabile* passage that nothing can stop. This movement is really in sonata form, but no longer classical. The first subject is merely a motif, and a second rhythmic one appears somewhat later, achieving what the first subject did not achieve: a sense of renewal. The wonderfully singing second theme is yearning and full of lyrical passion. Most astonishing is the development section that takes these themes through all possible keys, mostly without modulations, a strong and most unusual, daring, dramatic, emotional statement.

The Piano Quartet in G Minor, Op. 45, was composed in 1886. It is also in four movements and as in the previous work, the Scherzo appears after the first movement. But, while Fauré's manner of writing is immediately recognizable, one way he handles the piano and the strings; there are notable differences between the two works. The second quartet leaves the classical mold behind, both in form and harmony. There is much bolder, the classical outlines blurred, and the first movement changes in the course of the recapitulation and ends in a major, a procedure we shall see repeated in subsequent works. The movement again has three distinct themes, but they have greater character and originality. The first especially has an imperious character that shows the development of the composer's individuality. The Allegro molto moderato begins, as does the first quartet, in the minor, and the first theme is announced by the strings. An idiosyncrasy probably retained from song composition. Then the powerful rhetoric of the string body asserts itself, as does the first theme as well, hardly permitting the following subject to breathe, twice more to appear in the course of the movement. The theme changes often meter — so far — as indicated above — as to change the mode, thereby attempting to change the entire character of the energetic first theme. It is as fascinating to watch this manipulation as it is to watch his harmonic changes from major to minor, and back, and to major again. The meter here changes from 5/8 to 2/4 to 3/4, without batting an eyelash — that is, without having less than six eighths to a measure. Thus we have sometimes a half note, sometimes a dotted half note as a measure. Three eighths, or two, or three, or two or three. We are in the realm of *le jeu de l'amour et du hasard*. This is a real Scherzo, a play with notes, in which a widely arched melody — rhythmic transformation of the main theme of the first movement — is also fitted to the adventurous nature of the six eighths.

If the G major ending of the first movement surprised us, we are no less surprised by the Scherzo that follows in G minor, and the idiosyncratic use of triplets which persists throughout, compared to the transcendental beauty of its mood and its deeply moving melody. Throughout the first movement there is in the piano part something like the pealing of bells, and above it a fervent, almost religious prayer-like melody. If the two opening movements, as is usual in a program, are never far below the surface, we have no compunction in assuming that the composer had just that in mind. Intoned at first by the viola, and accompanied by the "bells," the melody is taken over by the other strings, but it is the piano that comments upon this chant in the middle section. This movement is one of Fauré's most moving compositions.

The last movement, *Allegro molto*, returns to G minor, but it again turns to G major, in the home key E minor. It is passionate music, and Fauré is again taking liberties with its 3/4 meter: alternating runs in triplets with duplet runs; two eighths against three, and cross-accents. Similarly in the harmony, where we find an endless variety of changing keys and unexpected spicy harmonies.

In 1920, Fauré, now seventy-five, resigned his position at the Conservatoire where he had been director since 1905, and retired to private life. Liberated from his cares and worries, he did not have to express or push into the background the age to compose. It burst forth with a vigor beyond his age. The result was the Piano Quintet in C Minor, Op. 115, finished in 1921. If he had won a new lease on life, Fauré's fantasy roamed free again, and he produced a work full of youthful energy and excitement: a real *Scherzo*, not a *Scherzino*.

The initial Allegro moderato shows the well-known Fauré technique of piano *arpeggios* over which a sweeping melody with wide intervals is introduced by the viola, taken over by the violin, then by the piano. In the second subject the roles are reversed: the piano has the melody. There is, as in the Quartet, Op. 15, a curiously fragmented second subject given by the strings, which the piano begins interrupting. The whole is built up to a climax, and is followed by one of Fauré's inimitable development sections.

There follows the Scherzo, *Allegro vivo*, in E-flat, 3/4 time. The rushing *arpeggios* and runs in chromatic sequences give this movement a Mendelssohnian flavor. The Piano has *moto macabre*. The Trio in B-flat brings a contrast of smoothly flowing and interwoven arabesques of melodies, brought to a climax by a piano melody extending to 20 measures under one legato note. It is again a breathless rushing of music, and there are fascinating melodic references to the first movement.

The *Andante moderato* in G major (but frequently straying into other keys) is a melody or response — *Je t'aime, tu m'aimes — d'amour — un amour!* — The theme is genuine emotion here, one that found its perfect expression naturally and sincerely.

The final *Allegro molto* is also rich in invention: melodic, harmonic, and rhythmic. The movement is in 3/4, but the pace varies considerably, from measure to measure. It also begins its melodic arch in four 3/4 measures. The melody is taken up by the upper instruments, the tenor voice. The development section shows Fauré's inexhaustible fantasy in transforming his themes and investing them with a different character at each appearance. This is one of Fauré's strongest compositions.

For the first decade of the twentieth century, Fauré was already known, at least in Paris, but not first appreciated — or for the wrong reasons. His chamber music was largely ignored; Debussy, who reviewed some of his music, wrote rather flippantly about it, but then Debussy is known to have been terribly unfair in his criticism, particularly of Franck and Fauré, not hesitating to have a following among young composers, a "cultivated and civilized minority."

Few composers have produced a similar rotter of talented pupils: Maurice Ravel, Louis Aubert, Charles Koechlin, Florent Schmitt, Roger-Ducasse, Rhené-Baton, Jean and Louis Huré, Gabriel Grovlez, Georges Enesco, Emile Vuillermoz, Pierre Lanteuil, Nadia Boulanger. The young Aaron Copland became his admirer and wrote in the last year of Fauré's life to New York's *Musical America*: "It is difficult to realize that this fragile, delicate-looking man is the master of so many of our most gifted musicians." Schirmer the Elder bought and published Fauré's Piano Quintet in D Minor, Op. 89, composed in 1906. In the previous year Fauré was appointed director of the famous Conservatoire in Paris, the position overhauled with the most illustrious names in French musical history. It was somewhat hastily written, and it does not have the fresh spontaneity of the quartets.

The first movement begins *molto moderato*, as if it had been composed "in the manner of Fauré." We find most of his known devices here: the piano *arpeggios*; the melody is introduced throughout, while the strings, unisono, singly, or with countermelodies, sing. The movement also begins in major, but does not remain there. The *Adagio* in the home key of G major does not bring too much contrast, and it is rhetorical rather than deeply felt. It is as fully scored as the first movement. A final *Allegretto moderato* does provide variety with its amiable melody and its light texture. But however light and concise, it still had to be thought out enough, and when it is and the *grandioso* is opened, it sounds somewhat hollow.

Fauré was seventy-nine in 1924 when he composed his *Trio for Piano, Violin and Violoncello in D Minor, Op. 120*. He belonged to the same generation as Tchaikovsky and Saint-Saëns and Goya, retained to old age their creative faculty and were able to produce works with undiminished fresh invention. Thus, while Haydn's late works were among the most grandiose of his creations, Fauré in this work turned to simplicity. Apart from the inevitable learned style, the simplicity of structure and limpidity of texture of earlier times — one might say that of Rameau. There are only three movements, and the finale sounds as though it had been converted.

Originally as a Scherzo. The entire work is characterized by grace and elegance, and a youthful lightness. He dispensed with the virtuoso trappings and sometimes empty ornamentation of the first quintet, and the work gains by its economy and directness.

The theme of the *Allegro ma non troppo* is announced by the cello and repeated by the violin. The second theme is given by the piano. The elaborate development gives the composer an opportunity to display his ingenuity in juggling with far-fetched keys, and a very much shortened recapitulation brings the movement to a close. The *Andantino* in F major assigns the first theme to the strings, to which the piano opposes a *cantando* subject that reminds us, finely chiseled as it is, of Mendelssohn's *Songs Without Words*. The final *Allegro vivo* in 3/8 brings two curiously contrasting phrases of six measures, *alternately sounded* by the strings and the piano. The phrases are later reduced to three measures, and this macro-meter a *la* *hat* is retained through the movement and the D major coda that ends this amiable work.

In the same year Fauré composed his only chamber music work for strings alone: his String Quartet in E Minor, Op. 121. It was his last work. One wonders what was in the mind of the aged composer when he set out to write a string quartet, a task all composers of note approached with trepidation, and for which they prepared themselves by writing and discarding several — sometimes many — apprentice specimens. He attained in the course of a long and busy life all the honors he might have wished for. He was a commander of the Legion of Honor, he was surrounded by a host of respectful pupils and friends. He was tired, and had a presentiment of the end. He wrote hastily, and in fact could not revise his manuscript. He died in November 1924. All this is seen in the score of the work, mastery as it is in craftsmanship.

The three movements, *Allegro moderato*, *Andante*, and *Allegro*, lack sufficient contrast in the character of the themes, in rhythmic vigor, in dynamic fire, but the combination of the four instruments, of their melodic expressiveness, of their timbre, the combination of the harmonies, of the modes, of contrasting keys, is still there. This is a touching testimony of an old man who was a great master, and to whom Nicholas Slonimsky paid the following tribute:

Fauré developed a musical idiom all his own; by subtle application of old modes he evoked the aura of eternally fresh art; by using unresolved mild discords and special coloristic effects in his instrumental music he anticipated the procedures of impressionism; in his piano works he shunned virtuosity in favor of the classical lucidity of the French masters of the clavier; the precisely articulated melodic line of his songs is in the finest tradition of French vocal music.

Fauré's stature as a composer is undiminished by the passage of time.

**EGON E. KENTON**
