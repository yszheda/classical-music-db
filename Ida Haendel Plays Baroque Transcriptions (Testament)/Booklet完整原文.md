## Liner Notes by Tully Potter, 2002

Ida Haendel reckons that if you were to let a hat fall and ask her to play a concert tomorrow night – or perhaps even tonight – she could offer you a choice of 30. And she is adamant that gender is no bar to interpreting the big concertos. "The control, the inward power, it's already a force inside of you, and that's what you need to play a Brahms or a Beethoven. When you start to play the Elgar, it's like a whole life. Some of the young players, they don't realize the strength of it. It's my femininity that helps me. The aggressive is not the main part of violin playing. If men don't manage to do it, it's because they don't have that inner intensity – you either have it or you don't. And it depends on the composer. In Brahms you can do all the passion you want but you can't do it in Beethoven." She picks up her 1696 Strad and plays the main theme from the first movement of the Beethoven Concerto with a seamless legato and a subtle portato. Then she plays the opening of the Kreutzer Sonata, which she feels most people play too fast nowadays. "They can't sustain it because they don't have the control." Nor does she agree with throwing herself about the stage like at least one of the younger generation. "I never had a lot of movement. People think if you don't move, you don't have temperament. If you move about, you are playing for the public, which I don't do. My first duty is … I'm trying to please one person only, the composer. The real genius is the composer." Miss Haendel spends much of her time in Miami these days but she does have her misgivings about the US. "America is all about communication, marketing. Classical artists compete with entertainers. They spend a lot on publicity. I never did that, not a penny."

How much does she practise? "I don't believe in it, I never have. Sorry! I have never felt that you can reach a certain limit if you do a certain number of hours. I never needed a lot of practice. To prove it she plays a passage from Paganini's 24th Caprice that some players fudge by executing a glissando, whereas the composer wrote separate notes. Her fourth finger jerks along the string in perfect tune. "See! I haven't practised that for ages. I was lucky that I had a very concerned father who didn't want me to play too much. Even if I was sick, I liked playing, and my father used to come and take the violin away from me." It was Natan Haendel who oversaw the career which started in Poland when the three-year-old Ida took her sister Sara's fiddle and played a song her mother had been singing. "I strongly believe in reincarnation because when I picked up the violin at three and a half I could play, as if something that had happened before was carrying on." The family moved from Chelm to the capital Warsaw so that Ida could study with Mieczysław Miwałowicz at the Conservatory. At nine she won a gold medal and at ten the first Huberman Prize; and in the 1935 Wieniawski Competition – controversially won by Ginette Neveu – she was given a special prize for a Polish contestant. She was encouraged by her compatriot Bronisław Huberman. He heard him play only when she was too tiny to appreciate it. "He was a tremendous personality. I am sure he played out of tune, and scratched, but the great personality came through. He predicted the Holocaust years before it happened. He should have been in politics." She was able to playing in his centenary celebrations in Israel in 1982. Her main teacher was Carl Flesch, the super-controlled antithesis of the all-or-nothing Huberman.

What has she done in her life, rather than practising for hours! "I did everything else. I went shopping, because I love clothes! If I have to give up the violin, I shall just buy more clothes instead." She has had a reputation for frilly gowns ever since she made her London debut at Queen's Hall in 1936. She settled in England in 1939, taking citizenship in 1940 and helping to boost morale with many wartime concerts. She was a Proms favourite for more than 30 years until that venerable series succumbed to the fashionable demand for younger and younger players – most of whom, children of the CD age, make such a poor sound that they can hardly be heard in the Royal Albert Hall. The last time Miss Haendel appeared at a Prom she performed the Britten Concerto, a work she feels evangelistic about. "Walton, Britten and Elgar are the most wonderful three composers. The Britten is neglected, which is unfair. There's such turmoil in it. He composed it in Canada; he was about 25 and didn't know himself. There's a lot of agony in it. The end is most resigned."

In 2000, astonishingly, Ida Haendel made her debut at London's premier chamber music venue, the Wigmore Hall, with a recital including the Third Sonata by another of her teachers, George Enescu. Her 1996 recording of the sonata with Ashkenazy caused a stir, not least because a bonus disc contained most of her 1940s 78rpm records, considered prodigious in their day. It was clear, however, that the 1996 Haendel played better. Now she would like to have another go at a work which she recorded as long ago as 1953 with the maverick Romanian conductor Sergiu Celibidache, one of her early mentors. "The time has come, she played it faster or slower today? "Tempo is not an issue, it's what I understand of Brahms now. I learn every day. Every time I pick up the violin I discover new things, I find I missed this accent or that accent. Many violinists do things with the violin because they can't do it any other way. What I did then [in 1953] was instinct. Now I know more. Amazingly, she has never even played the Bach Double Concerto. "I've never been asked to do it, or the Beethoven Triple Concerto – it's been my dream to do that." And she has not got round to recording the Brahms Double Concerto, although she has played it.

Miss Haendel has had her Strad for almost 40 years. "Before that I had a German imitation of a Guarnerius and no one knew the difference." Her favourite among her four bows is a Hill Fleur de Lys. The violin has a superb one-piece back with the figure running slightly diagonally. "When I was going on a BBC Symphony Orchestra tour with John Pritchard I saw a little crack in the front of my violin. I took it to Etienne Vatelot in Paris but he said he would have to keep it a month. He couldn't let me have another because he needed the work. Strad gave me the Gaberieli. He took the other Strad but it gave me a G string like the original. She agrees that most of the violin tone is created by the player.

"Of course a good instrument helps. When I do masterclasses I never demonstrate on my Strad, in case they think: oh, but she's got a Strad, so it's easy for her. I always use the student's violin. But you can change the sound. It's up to the performer. If you are going to play Falla or Brahms or Stravinsky, you have to adapt the sound to the style. You can't treat it as if you're playing Sibelius or as if you're playing Debussy. You have to change the style according to the style of the composer." She takes her violin and caricatures a famous violinist heard playing the Sibelius Concerto in an ersatz gypsy style on the radio. "It makes me angry when someone plays like that."

This recital is one of two Miss Haendel recorded with the British-based Australian pianist Geoffrey Parsons (1929-1995), best known for his work as accompanist to the world's great singers. "In those days I played a lot of concerts with him," she recalls. "He was a very talented pianist and accompanist. I took him to other countries as well. We had a very good rapport. It is a safe bet, however, that they never played this programme in concert, as the pieces are so similar in style that one would be sufficient for any recital. "I have played all of them since I was about seven, except for the Vitali, which I studied after I heard Jania Heifetz play it," Miss Haendel says. "I nearly died when I heard him play it like him – the drama he put into this piece! Nobody played it like him." At her second Wigmore Hall recital in 2001, Miss Haendel performed the Chaconne in Heifetz's memory. It is popular with violinists because it is well written for the instrument and gives the player scope for interpretation, within its strict baroque framework yet its origins are shrouded in mystery. It is attributed to Tomaso Antonio Vitali but is not like his other works; and it has come down to us only in an 1867 edition by Mendelssohn's violinist friend Ferdinand David. Similarly the other works on this CD are reflected through the arranging skills of two early 20th-century violinists. La Folia was the most popular tune for variations in the baroque period and Corelli's version, published as the 12th and last of his only set of violin sonatas, Op. 5, was perhaps the best known. David made a famous transcription of it for violin and piano but the version Miss Haendel plays was prepared by Fritz Kreisler, who introduced a few difficulties of his own. He also made the arrangement of Tartini's most celebrated sonata. Tartini claimed to have dreamed of a visitation from the Devil who, on being handed his fiddle, played him an extraordinarily beautiful piece. When he awoke, he wrote down the G minor Sonata, incorporating a truly devilish trill in the finale, but admitted it was not as good as the music he had heard in his dream. Perhaps that was why Kreisler decided to beef up the final trill to such an extent! The result has always been a favourite with fiddlers – even the purist Adolf Busch preferred Kreisler's version to all others.

As for Nardini, he was Tartini's best and dearest pupil; and when he was himself famous, hearing in 1669 that Tartini was ailing, he hurried to Padua to care for his dying teacher. In the 19th century an E minor 'concerto' by Nardini was much played, although it was in fact arranged from movements of three of his sonatas. The work Miss Haendel plays here is based on a genuine sonata in D major, the second in a set of seven which the composer announced as having 'embellished adagios'. It was arranged for violin and piano by Haendel's teacher Carl Flesch – who imported the Larghetto from another Nardini sonata for the sake of contrast.
