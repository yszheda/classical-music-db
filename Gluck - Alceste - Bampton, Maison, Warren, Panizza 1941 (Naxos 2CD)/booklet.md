Christoph Willibald GLUCK (1714 - 1787) Alceste (complete) 1941 This broadcast of Alceste brought us Rose Bampton replacing Marjorie Lawrence who, though scheduled, was unable to sing. This was, on one hand, a great loss, as Marjorie Lawrence was an extraordinary soprano, admired for her singing of the title role. On the other hand, this preservation offers Bampton in what may be her characterization and in the classical stature of her singing. greatest singing on record; surpassing, perhaps, Lawrence in the nobility of her In view of the rich repertoire which was offered in the 1940-41 season, it was entirely admirable of the Met to add another early opera from the baroque period to the Orfeo which was mounted with Thorborg and Novotna. It was an extraordinary range of operatic masterpieces the Met presented, which traversed the line from Gluck through Mozart, Donizetti and Puccini to Wagner, Richard Strauss and Montemezzi, many of which productions were memorable. The French repertoire included Lakmé, Carmen, Samson et Dalila, Louise, Pelléas and this Alceste. 8.110006-7
Gluck wrote the French version of his opera, previously given in Vienna in 1767 for the Paris Opera in 1776. The 1941 production of the Met was its American premiere. Alceste represented Gluck's avowed desire to create music drama, something which, in its classical simplicity and elegaic melodies, nevertheless managed to remain true to the drama of a love which triumphed over death. It is something of an astonishment that a work stemming from an antique era, when dramatic works were static, should remain so faithful to its period and yet be intelligible and enjoyed by a modern audience ready to hear Wagner, Strauss and Montemezzi, nearly 175 years later. Bampton brings the music to life. Warren is the other asset, though he lacks the requisite style and his voice is rather thick for communicating the French language in so immense an opera house, nevertheless, he is in magnificent voice, singing music not usually associated with him at the beginning of his famed Met career. Maison is more than acceptable as King Admetus, believable, stylish, but since his was never a very distinguished voice, nothing he sings is memorable. The music critics praised the production, Herbert Graf's staging, the massive sets, though some complained that the static passages in this otherwise beautiful score were emphasized by too often adopting classic poses, detracting from the simplicity at which Gluck aimed. Nevertheless, for what the New York Sun termed a "loftily conceived and often deeply moving score", the entire endeavour was commendable, the reviewer concluding that the generation which could hear both Orpheus and Alceste in the course of a season or two could count itself fortunate. We join that fortunate radio audience. There is broadcast commentary and curtain calls. With good sound for the era much better than EJS excerpts, or dubs in circulation.
