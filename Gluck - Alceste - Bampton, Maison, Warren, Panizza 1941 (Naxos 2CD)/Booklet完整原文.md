## Introduction by Richard Caniell

### Christoph Willibald GLUCK (1714 - 1787)
### Alceste (complete) 1941

This broadcast of *Alceste* brought us Rose Bampton replacing Marjorie Lawrence who, though scheduled, was unable to sing. This was, on one hand, a great loss, as Marjorie Lawrence was an extraordinary soprano, admired for her singing of the title rôle. On the other hand, this preservation offers Bampton in what may be her greatest singing on record; surpassing, perhaps, Lawrence in the nobility of her characterization and in the classical stature of her singing.

In view of the rich repertoire which was offered in the 1940-41 season, it was entirely admirable of the Met to add another early opera from the baroque period to the *Orfeo* which was mounted with Thorborg and Novotna. It was an extraordinary range of operatic masterpieces the Met presented, which traversed the line from Gluck through Mozart, Donizetti and Puccini to Wagner, Richard Strauss and Montemezzi, many of which productions were memorable. The French repertoire included *Lakmé, Carmen, Samson et Dalila, Louise, Pelléas* and this *Alceste*.

Gluck wrote the French version of his opera, previously given in Vienna in 1767, for the Paris Opera in 1776. The 1941 production of the Met was its American première. *Alceste* represented Gluck's avowed desire to create music-drama, something which, in its classical simplicity and elegaic melodies, nevertheless managed to remain true to the drama of a love which triumphed over death. It is something of an astonishment that a work stemming from an antique era, when dramatic works were static, should remain so faithful to its period and yet be intelligible and enjoyed by a modern audience ready to hear Wagner, Strauss and Montemezzi, nearly 175 years later.

Bampton brings the music to life. Warren is the other asset, though he lacks the requisite style and his voice is rather thick for communicating the French language in so immense an opera-house, nevertheless, he is in magnificent voice, singing music not usually associated with him at the beginning of his famed Met career. Maison is more than acceptable as King Admetus, believable, stylish, but since his was never a very distinguished voice, nothing he sings is memorable.

The music critics praised the production, Herbert Graf's staging, the massive sets, though some complained that the static passages in this otherwise beautiful score were emphasized by too often adopting classic poses, detracting from the simplicity at which Gluck aimed. Nevertheless, for what the New York Sun termed a "loftily conceived and often deeply moving score", the entire endeavour was commendable, the reviewer concluding that the generation which could hear both *Orpheus* and *Alceste* in the course of a season or two could count itself fortunate. We join that fortunate radio audience.

There is broadcast commentary and curtain calls. With good sound for the era; much better than EJS excerpts, or dubs in circulation.

This derives from the first master-tape made of the NBC transcription discs between 1948 and 1949 and acquired under written authority from dated May 1983. There were 16" transcriptions which we seamlessly edited together and pitch-corrected. Dr Irwin Elkins was a central figure in obtaining the source material on which we worked. This is the first occasion on which these originals have been transferred to CD.

## Technical information

The broadcast of this performance derives from NBC transcription discs (1935-1943) made available to IPRMS by authorisation from NBC in 1983, or, from private recordings made for singers involved in the performance or, in lesser instances, from preservations made by private collectors of the period. In all instances, the transfer to CD was made from the first strata of recording directly from the original discs via a Prism Sound 20bit Analogue to Digital Converter to digital tape and then Noise Shaped to 16bits using the Prism Sound SNS 2 process.

Considerable effort has been expended to remedy a host of defects which afflicted these preservations. There were chiefly lapses in pitch, truncation of orchestral chords, choral passages or a word or more lost from an individual singer owing to the "instantaneous break" recording technique then in use. By exhaustive work we have resolved all these problems by seamlessly replacing missing portions, with the exception of one or two clumsy joins or groove defects stemming from the original transfer of the discs to tape in the 1940s.

These performances (taken from the original first generation recording source) are of extraordinary historical interest but - good as they sound - they do not offer the kind of silent reproduction to which we have become accustomed since the LP/stereo era. Indeed, we have allowed the grit and ticks to remain unfiltered, editing between available preservations where possible, but there is no filtering, which has ruined most of the pirated third and fourth generation offerings of these performances in the international market-place. We have thus preserved the bloom of the voice and the original recording including the broadcast commentaries of the period as it has never been heard before. Given that these CD editions deliver access to the original preservation, you will hear performances in their original splendour and we can with confidence tell you that does not get any better.

## Restoration Notes

The sound on these CDs has been restored using two CEDAR processes. Derived from research funded by the British Library National Sound Archive and carried out at Cambridge University, these eliminate unwanted blemishes such as clicks and crackle from the audio signal. Once the problems are correctly identified and removed, the original sound can then be recreated using powerful and sophisticated digital interpolation processes. The result is a higher quality audio signal almost completely free from the scratches and surface noise normally associated with recordings of this vintage.
