## Preface (English, page 5)

The Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, 16 February 1983. Jon Vickers, with Geoffrey Parsons at the piano, was performing *Winterreise* by Schubert – a composer far removed from the artistic world of the great Canadian tenor, who was better known for his barnstorming operatic appearances than for his work in the intimate medium of chamber music. I was curious to know what he would make of this genre, which hardly seemed suited to his personality, in which a heightened theatrical instinct was pre-dominant. The performance came as a shock: Vickers drew on elements from some of his greatest roles – the humanity of Florestan, the despair of Tristan, the tears of Peter Grimes and the torments of Otello – in his approach to the work, and all these were suddenly brought together in a deeply moving interpretation that both soared to the heights of ecstasy and plumbed the depths of despair. He took us on a never-to-be-forgotten journey that was a million miles away from the typical song recital. I was determined to capture this smouldering musical phenomenon for posterity, and was able to do this five months later, at the Salle Wagram – our main recording venue at the time. Sometimes, this profession brings us really unmissable opportunities, and transports us to a world far apart from everyday reality...

**Alain Lanceron**
President of Warner Classics International

## Jon Vickers: A glance at his Warner Classics Legacy (English, pages 6-7)

"Great artists have made warnings to us as individuals and to us as a society. If we don't recognise it..."

Jon Vickers did not finish the sentence, but he did not need to – he had made his point to the captivated audience that packed the Barbican Cinema for his return to London after an eleven-year absence, on the 25th of October 1998. He had retired from singing a decade earlier, but his impact on audiences and his peer's role in society and the performer's duty therein burned as blazingly in that historic public interview as they had in the 36 years, from 1952 to 1988, when his operatic incarnations held his audiences spellbound, first in his home country of Canada and then in the world's theatres.

A recording of that special event is issued in full for the first time on this CD set, and it reveals the words and ideas behind the voice that moulded Jon Vickers' entire approach to life – his profound Christian faith and philosophical conclusions that informed every word he sang and every gesture he made, and how he drew from a particular favourite opera role, and yet, in the tradition of history's greatest stage interpreters, equally he separated out his personal conflict from the dramatic and psychological essence of the characters he stepped into as he embodied each fictional character and breathed truth that was intensely and sometimes frighteningly realistic. That intensity, personified in a voice of truly colossal dynamic and expressive range, was however still achieved with little effort or emotional trauma. "A weeping Tristan is a weeping Tristan, but a weeping Jon Vickers is a fool" he said during the Barbican Cinema interview. The thundering fortissimo outbursts that shook the theatre seats, the whispering mezza-voce threats that pierced through the auditorium, and the vivid emotional conflicts that flooded his face and body as he conjured up through iron vocal control, rigorous intellectual discipline and a meticulous pursuit of the composer's intentions. Even with some highly distinguished colleagues, he was never afraid to take risks – like in Britten's *Peter Grimes*, where the composer strongly disliked, he openly stated his belief that he was pursuing what for him was a dimension of truth that Britten himself did not believe he had conceived. His argument has been heralded as contentious, but it has now pondered for post-generously spoken. For Jon Vickers, as mercilessly demanding and severely critical of himself as he was of other singers, conductors and stage directors, the objective aspiration for knowledge, veracity and understanding through the broadest possible investigation was the artistic ideal.

Jon Vickers relates in the Barbican Cinema interview how he progressed from being what he called "a stonfishd student at the Royal Conservatory of Music" in Toronto to an internationally exalted discovery when he was engaged to sing at the Royal Opera House Covent Garden in 1957. With what was then the Covent Garden Opera Company, he made his most striking early impressions in 1960: that year, Ricardo (for King Gostow as the character originally was) in Verdi's *Un Ballo in Maschera*, and later as Canio in Leoncavallo's *Pagliacci* both sung in English in the following year – the title role in Verdi's *Don Carlo* in Italian in a groundbreaking production by Luchino Visconti, conducted by Carlo Maria Giulini making his Covent Garden debut; and three years later, in 1964, Florestan in Beethoven's *Fidelio*, which he sang in German with Otto Klemperer conducting his first opera at Covent Garden. Not long after that legendary event he showed his interpretation with Klemperer in this studio, and the essence of what they achieved together in the theatre is vividly preserved in their EMI recording of *Fidelio*, a performance that has been reissued in a 4-CD set by Warner Classics in 2015, and also on DVD.

Vickers emerged during a period in the mid-twentieth century when there were still some singers who approached their roles with a sense of dramatic truth and psychological realism that is all too rare today. His ability to inhabit a character completely, to live the role rather than merely perform it, set him apart from many of his contemporaries. He brought to every role a depth of understanding and a commitment to truth that was both intellectually rigorous and emotionally devastating.

## Liner Notes (English, pages 8-13)

Vickers especially felt a personal affinity with Florestan's faith in the throes of his desperation, and he identified very personally with the ultimate faith sustained in the face of catastrophe by another role he recorded for EMI: Samson in Saint-Saëns' *Samson et Dalila*. As with Florestan's first appearance, but in an even more physical situation, Jon Vickers was clothed in a simple white sheet, drenched and on stage as Samson is first seen and heard in the opera, as the tenor Jon Robson recalls:

"He was standing amongst a crowd of Hebrews, all wavering the same kind of brown cloak costume. I wouldn't notice him – he was hidden amongst them. And then suddenly he stepped out, calling them to fight, and suddenly the whole crowd parted before him! He had something exceptional about him that communicated passion, strength, vigour and life."

And those elements generated spontaneous creative interactions with other artists appearing with him. Sometimes the intense realism in his performances could frighten them, as mezzo-soprano Grace Bumbry recounts of his Don José against her Carmen in Bizet's *Carmen* with him:

"I remember him so vividly in the final scene of the opera when Don José kills Carmen. You feared for your life because it was so real. And he would always time the actual killing slightly differently. Sometimes he would let me get past him and he would then grab me, swing me around and jab me – or he would jab me before I managed to get past him. He did it all for real, because you just never knew – but it was always perfectly timed with the music, whichever way he did it. No matter what he did, he was always what I term 'in tune'."

Audiences too were scared when Jon Vickers reached the final scene of *Carmen*, as the musicologist and author David Cairns remembers:

"It was absolutely terrifying. You felt dreadful things were going to happen on the stage in real life, not just being acted."

The terror in Vickers' voice in that scene is powerfully projected in EMI' recording of *Carmen* with him and Grace Bumbry, but that is only one aspect of the remarkable panoply of human colours he conjured up in and invited all the operas he performed. In Act One when Micaëla brings Don José, who has just experienced Carmen for the first time, news of his mother and tells him she longs to see her son and forgives him for running away from home, Jon Vickers is touchingly gentle and poignantly tender in his exchanges. Here he has the dual timbre and phrasing of the light lyrical tenor that René Jequier of Don José in the first act, making the surprise of the audience even greater when that contrast disappears and all the most striking and in due course shocking. Vickers' mastery of the almost impossible requirements of a tenor, makes his Don José one of the great voices of the twentieth century. His voice (which is not dramatic) reminds us of the unprecedented demands the composer made on his singers in this revolutionary work. When José pours out his impassioned love to Carmen in the Flower Song, Vickers is one of the few tenors who has managed both to focus the intensity required in accordance with the instructions as: very near the end of the aria, Don José sings 'et j'étais une chose à toi' – 'I belonged entirely to you'. At this phrase rises up to a high B flat, the marking is for a diminuendo to pianissimo with a rallentando, and although Jon Vickers does reach a pianissimo, he hushed, dolent expression, seemingly woven in impeccable intonation, is breathtakingly faithful to the composer's instructions. Indeed, Jon Vickers himself sees not only that this most exquisite aria is overwhelmed by the despair that he can only whisper: "And Jon Dobson reflects 'it's so beautiful and so touching. The man is laying his heart in front of Carmen – with a B flat!'."

All the more frightening it then is when he unleashes the full massive force of his dramatic closet voice in Act Three as, demonised now as the smugglers' pathetic, demented, hopelessly trying to win back Carmen's love, and almost demented with anger at her new passion for the toreador Escamillo, his violent threats her that he will never let her go – these are the desperate sounds of real life.

One of the great challenges of an operatic career is the need to transform the character and hue of a role over successive performances. As one of the most striking elements of Jon Vickers' art was the evident and range of his voice, the dramatic intensity, the power of his acting, the ability to create a character from within. His portrayal of Don José was a tour de force. He brought to the role a unique combination of vocal power, dramatic intensity, and psychological insight. He made Don José a real person, not just a character in an opera. He made us believe in him, feel for him, and ultimately fear him.

Sadly, from 2004 until his death on the 10th of July 2015, Jon Vickers was in a nursing home suffering from acute Alzheimer's disease. He is sorely missed by his legion admirers and many friends. Although his searching preoccupation with the human and spiritual mission of his art could give him a fearsome presence; he was a deeply generous person and could also be disarmingly amusing and earthbound, as can be heard in the Barbican Centre interview. His former stage colleague at the Royal Opera House Covent Garden, the tenor John Dobson, remembers him with great affection:

"He was most approachable. Yes, he could be difficult, we all know that, but he was a very human man and he was a great artist. I did a lot of performances with him and I admired him immensely."

And he goes on to say:

"You could say that Vickers had a big voice, and I guess he did. But, to special strength wasn't so much about its size. It was powerful because it was so round and solid and all-embracing. It just came out and flooded the theatre."

Dobson, who notably appeared with him in the famous Klemperer performances of *Fidelio* and also the illustrious Elijah Moshinsky production of *Peter Grimes*, has the final words here as, recalling Jon Vickers' Siegmund in Wagner's *Die Walküre* at the Royal Opera House Covent Garden in 1961, they quintessentially exemplify this titan singer's immense artistic presence:

"In the Act 2 Todesverkündigung when Brünnhilde comes to call Siegmund, she appeared up behind him as he was sitting on a ledge of rock at the front with Sieglinde lying on the ground in front of him. As Brünnhilde began to sing, calling him to Valhalla, he just raised his head and looked out into the theatre – and he moved her – from the rock into the theatre in front of him, I could hardly breathe, because it was electric. The man's personality and thought process did it – and I thought to myself 'this man is very, very special'."

**Jon Tolansky**
