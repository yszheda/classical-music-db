**GLENN GOULD**
**(1932-1982)**

Just about everything concerning Glenn Gould was unconventional. As a musician, he was practically self-taught; he gave public live concerts for a brief time, yet his records and tapes are heard constantly, wherever classical piano music is broadcast. His life style was anomalous. He defied tradition in his views toward his instrument, toward classical music, and indeed to the whole aura that surrounds the usual concept of the world of classical music. Called eccentric by some, he nevertheless produced a musical sound appreciated and admired by dilettante and professional alike.

Born in Toronto, Canada, on September 25, 1932, Glenn Gould began his piano studies at age three with his mother, and continued with her until he was ten. From then on until he was nineteen, he studied piano with Alberto Guerrero at the Royal Conservatory in Toronto. Part of this time was devoted to the study of the organ and music theory. Because he was spending so much time with his music, Gould had little left for academic subjects, so he left school without ever receiving a diploma. At age nineteen he also left the conservatory, and from that time on he became his own teacher.

During his teen years, Gould gave numerous recitals and was frequently heard in broadcast concerts for the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation. Gould's international career, however, was launched in 1955 when critics gave him rave reviews following his debut recital in New York. Within twenty-four hours, the twenty-two-year-old Gould had signed an exclusive contract with Columbia Records. His first recording for Columbia was the Bach Goldberg Variations, and it was the success of this recording which brought Gould worldwide attention and numerous concert engagements throughout the United States, Western Europe, Israel, and Russia.

For the next nine years, Gould concertized extensively, but came to dislike public concerts because he found them "distasteful and somewhat degrading." Beyond that, he did not think that the concert stage was the ideal way of presenting music. "The very nature of the stage," he had once said, "is that the show must go on. 'Take two.' I don't think I like what I have done up to now. You know you can't do it over, and yet, in my concerts, I always wanted to do exactly that. I had this incredible urge to stop, turn around, and say, 'I don't think I'll try that again.'"

Although the concert environment did not afford Gould the opportunity to try it again, the studio environment did. In fact, it opened a whole new world for Gould because he saw all the possibilities of what he could do with music using the electronic resources at hand. "I'd bet you that before I began touring in 1956, I had not my whole life played more than maybe thirty or forty concerts, and there's really nothing new, you know, a lot of learning goes on at that time and I found even in my early teens that, for me, the most comfortable situation was the studio environment and not the concert environment." This statement goes a long way toward explaining some of Gould's eccentricities.

He began experimenting with microphones, placing them in unorthodox positions to extract the kind of sounds he wanted the listener to hear; frequencies were altered to improve tone and quality of the sound and to bring out more musical ideas, and like a film maker, Gould edited and spliced together material from the best of his "takes." In effect, what Gould saw was new art form being created, and his brand of pianism into a new kind of art form. This undertaking fit in perfectly with a philosophy often enunciated by Gould himself: the artist's obligation is to a performance.

Using the recording studio as his medium, Gould produced a prodigious amount of recording. Although he was best known for his recordings of the keyboard music of Bach and Schoenberg, he also recorded the music of many other composers such as all the Mozart sonatas, most of the Beethoven sonatas, all the Beethoven concertos, as well as works by Hindemith, Prokofieff, Grieg, Bizet, Scriabin, Sibelius, with his own transcriptions of Wagner's music.

The music of Hindemith, Krenek, and Barbaro also attracted Gould because of their contrapuntal lines. Homophonic music with its emphasis on melody and accompaniment was boring to him, which was probably the reason why he disliked romantic music. In fact, after having recorded all of the Mozart sonatas, Gould wrote a seven-thousand word essay on why he disliked Mozart. He had never chosen to play a work by Schubert, Chopin, or Schumann; nor did he find Stravinsky's music appealing. "His music certainly is basically oriented to a visual, choreographic sense, and only minimally interesting from the horizontal perspective."

Besides making recordings, Gould was also involved in numerous other projects during his lifetime, the most important of which was for the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation. For this venture, Gould had done interviews with several people and then had spliced the tapes so that several conversations could be going on at the same time. He also wrote articles for newspapers and magazines, his subjects ranging from politics to popular singers. He particularly was a fan of Barbara Streisand. Once he retired from the concert circuit, his dream was always to be Walter Cronkite for a day, reading the news on television.

Gould's private life was as solitary as his artistic life in the studio. He spent most of his waking hours, usually between midnight and dawn, in his recording studio, located in a Toronto hotel. He had always said that it was just impossible to develop his truest extent a kind of Howard Hughesian secrecy. He spent his nights working, went to bed at five or six o'clock in the morning, and got up three in the afternoon.

Eccentricities? He had a few; he had been seen wearing an overcoat and scarf indoors as well as out; his gloves had no fingers in them; his rickety piano chair was quite low (fourteen inches high), and his piano was elevated as a result; on his infrequent tours he took this dilapidated chair with most of the wooden slats missing with him; and while he played he hummed or buzzed, sometimes adding a counterpoint to the music. When asked about these peculiarities, Gould replied, "I don't think that my life style is like that of most other people, and I'm rather glad there isn't. I think it's in some way integrated with the kind of work I want to do."

## 内页 2

**The Concerto in B-flat Major, No. 2, Op. 19** was written when Beethoven was twenty-four years old. In three movements, the concerto begins with a charming Allegro con brio, 4/4, as is the first movement. The orchestra is heard first as it states and develops the principal theme. The piano then enters with a variant of the same theme. The second theme is heard first in F major by the orchestra. After the double exposition, a development and recapitulation follow. The movement comes to a close with a cadenza followed by a brief coda.

The slow movement, beginning at bar 3/4, begins with the orchestra announcing the principal theme, which is then fully stated by the piano. The movement ends with an expressive dialogue between the piano and orchestra.

The finale is a Rondo in B-flat major, Molto allegro, 6/8, which is humorous and jovial in spirit. The principal theme with its syncopated rhythms is first stated by the piano and recurs throughout interspersed with various episodes. The movement concludes with an ebullient coda.

**The Concerto in C Minor, No. 3, Op. 37** was completed by Beethoven in 1802. In three movements, the concerto begins with a highly dramatic Allegro con brio, 4/4 as its first movement. The orchestra is heard first in a lengthy tutti as it states and develops two strongly contrasted themes. The first theme, bold and masculine in character, is directly opposed to the quiet, flowing and lyrical nature of the second theme. The piano then enters with restatements of the themes. The development features a musical dialogue between the piano and orchestra, followed by a pause before the piano is heard in a vigorous coda bringing the movement to its conclusion.

The slow movement is a Largo in E major, 8/8 and written in the remote key of E major, begins with the piano stating the principal theme which then broadens and unfolds in its rich and subtle use of ornamentation. The movement ends in the same tranquil mood in which it began.

The finale is a Rondo in C minor, Allegro, 2/4, which is humorous and witty in spirit. The principal theme is first stated by the piano and recurs throughout in excursions to distant and surprising tonalities, interspersed with various episodes. After a brief ornamental passage, the piano introduces a new and contrasting secondary theme which is stated in a new tempo and meter, Presto, 6/8. Unlike the dramatic opening of the concerto, the movement concludes in a brilliant and ebullient mood.

**Beethoven completed his 32 Variations in c-minor** on an original theme in 1806, after the onset of his deafness. The theme is brief and simple, encompassing only 8 bars, yet in its simplicity this "minimalism" is a high concentration of melodic and harmonic energy. The work constitutes an important contribution to the genre: it demonstrates how one can write a complete set of theme song as the metric scheme remains the same. All variations, except for the concluding one, are in B-beat form like the theme; they follow one another without interruption.

— Marie Dupont
