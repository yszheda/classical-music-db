## Liner Notes

The great Hungarian composer Béla Bartók (1881-1945) loved the folk music of his native land. He discovered it largely by accident. Once discovered, it subsequently inspired his entire life's work. His collected thousands of tunes from Hungary and other eastern and southeastern European countries and various transcriptions. Approximately 150 are solo piano transcriptions.

This 2-CD album gathers them together in a single collection.

Bartók trained as a classical concert pianist and composer at the Budapest Conservatory. He was an early nationally rising pianist, writing to his mother after graduating in 1903 that "all my life, in every sphere, always and in every way, I shall have one objective: the glory of Hungary and the Hungarians."

The next summer (1904) while at a country villa near Lake Balaton (Central Hungary's largest lake) the most famous Hungarian Rhapsodies. Bartók had a life-changing experience: "Suddenly I heard above me a singing voice... I listened by way of experiment... and was drawn deep into it. All at once it struck me that the songs to which I had been listening were completely different from the known urban Hungarian popular style. It was only then that I became aware of what a wealth of experience pointed the way to unlimited possibilities. I decided I would follow this path to the end."

Follow this path he did, and it led him to folk music that would permeate his whole musical being. He pioneered the field of ethnomusicology through his fieldwork and became one of its greatest twentieth-century pioneers. He composed many of the twentieth century's greatest masterworks, and almost as many transcriptions of elements absorbed from his beloved folk music: what started as a hobby became his life's work.

Bartók collected peasant melodies wherever he got the chance. He describes the experience:

> Colinde are carols traditionally sung at Christmas-time in Romania. Some contain religious words, but many do not. Folk songs among young Romanian Christian men. A two-verse form for solo pepon without any reference to Christ, then a four-verse choral form for mixed chorus. The women join in the last verse. A men's group returns the colinde's characteristic, irregular meters.

> As the Romanian Folk Dances our strong Bartók's most famous folk music collection for solo piano. The Romanian Folk Dances arrangements for violin and for orchestra as well. Four were arranged as a wind quintet, four for two pianos, four for piano duet. They are organized as a coherent group with fast-slow-fast folk dances and a slow hauntingly beautiful final dance.

Bartók originally conceived the Sonatina as a group of five dances to be performed by piano, but it eventually became one of his most loved works. Originally it was titled "Romanian Dances" and it is dedicated to his wife Márta.

> When the war year 1914-18 Bartók wrote Fifteen Hungarian Peasant Songs for piano. They comprise a structure that has been described as like the Hungarian folk ballads: with variations, and a group of Old Dance Tunes. These pieces are studies on how one might create a new genre.

Concurrently with the Fifteen he also set all Three Hungarian Folk Songs for chorus (Op. 16). A few examples of the fast approach in the Journal Peking in 1925, and these pieces were published in 1942 in an album entitled Songs of Poland.

In 1914 a violin teacher invited him to marriage selections. She wanted a piece as pedagogical pieces for students. Bartók accepted the invitation but chose instead to set some folk songs. The result was 40 violin duos. Unlike any earlier settings, which feature music from single ethnic groups, the Duos include an eclectic mix, a miscellany of Eastern European upbringing. They were conceived of integration—the brotherhood of peoples—"in spite of all wars and conflicts."

> 1930s Bartók was in a state of depression. He had to resign from his teaching position at the Budapest Conservatory in 1934, and he had to work to earn a living. Now was time 1939 published series he added some new pieces. The Petite Suite is a suite of Ukrainian folk themes arranged for two pianos.

For perspective, the final tracks of this album offer several glimpses of what Bartók collected on the Edison cylinder phonograph he took with him into the villages of Eastern Europe. He went to a village of a gentleman, and the Hungarian sounds recorded digitally from the fragile wax cylinders transcribed into the notation presented here. Bartók was a meticulous record keeper, and in field notebooks, he noted details about the performers, the circumstances of the recording, and any other pertinent information.

Here in this album are Bartók's musical arrangements for piano. The pieces are organized into coherent groups: fast-slow-fast, with serious, ornate, charming, boisterous, and melancholic moods. Bartók captures the essence of each setting that "palatable"—ray, delicious. Enjoy!

—Sylvia Parker

## About the Performer

Sylvia Parker is Senior Lecturer at the University of Vermont where she teaches piano and music theory. She has given solo recitals and concerts with orchestras in North America, Europe, and Asia. She holds degrees from the Eastman School of Music (BM), Manhattan School of Music (MM), and DMA from Yale University. Her teachers include Robert Miller, Russell Sherman, György Sándor, and Jacob Lateiner. She has served on the faculties of Brevard Music Festival, Music Academy of the West, and Yale Summer Music Program.

Recordings are all Bartók's works and can be found among: Piano Sonatas, Eight Hungarian Folksongs (St. Anthony Press), Quantz: Six Flute Sonatas (Albany Records), Beethoven: Sonatas (Albany Records), and more.

Over his lifetime Bartók composed various sets of piano pieces into which he occasionally incorporated folk melodies. In Romanian Bagatelles (1908) a two-piano style is evident. Fourteen Bagatelles (1908) a new piano style appears as a reaction to the exuberance of the romantic, impressionistic, and expressionistic music of the late 19th century. It is Bartók's first composition to display the most notorious technical novelty—Ten Easy Piano Pieces (1908). Some of the pieces have Hungarian and German text by many piano students. Seven Sketches (1908-1909) represent what Bartók described as his "impressionistic" period. The Hungarian Folk Song Suite for piano and orchestra (1913) he collaborated with a colleague in his violin class Emil Telmányi. Later the Hungarian Suite in 1933 was published separately as First Term at the piano. He also published the 20th-century folk music. In his later years he began to compose Mikrokosmos. In all of these works the peasant style is evident. During the First World War (1914-18), Bartók was exempt from military service due to ill health, and he continued his research, transcribing and arranging folk melodies.

## Notes on the Music

### Disc 1

Bartók writes "Already at the very beginning of my career as a composer, one of my driving inner motives was the piano music. This also originated in my experience as a piano teacher, in particular the teaching of small children."

**For Children, Part 1 Hungarian Melodies (Sz. 53)** and **Part 2 Slovak Melodies (Sz. 42)** were composed over a period of a larger ongoing effort to document folk songs. The 82 pieces are progressive in difficulty from the simplest to more advanced material. Children, Part 1 presents piano students and others at more advanced. This collection (originally 84 pieces; two were omitted and published posthumously in 1947) are two. These piano pieces published in 1907 and 1909 are based on folk tunes Bartók collected. The collection is progressive in difficulty from the simplest to the more advanced. The piano pieces were intended as pedagogical pieces but are also enjoyed by concert pianists.

The pieces are deceptively simple. The two volumes together contain 82 pieces. Hungarian Melodies were collected in the region of Hungary and Slovak Melodies were collected in the region of Slovakia. Bartók transcribed these melodies from peasant singers and then arranged them for piano. Each piece captures the essence of the original folk tune while adding Bartók's own harmonic and rhythmic sensibilities.

### Disc 2

The pieces on Disc 2 represent Bartók's mature style and his deep understanding of folk music. These works go beyond simple transcription to create art music that is deeply rooted in folk traditions.

**Three Hungarian Folk Songs from Csík (BB 45b, Sz. 35a)** were composed in 1907. These arrangements of folk music for piano are very characteristic of early Bartók. The melodies come from Csík, a region with a significant ethnic Hungarian population in what is now Romania.

**Fourteen Bagatelles (BB 50, Sz. 38)** from 1908 show Bartók's emerging modernist style. The pieces are brief, aphoristic statements that anticipate his later mature works.

**Ten Easy Piano Pieces (BB 51, Sz. 39)** from 1908 are pedagogical pieces that maintain musical integrity while being accessible to intermediate players.

**Seven Sketches (BB 54, Sz. 44)** from 1908-1909 represent what Bartók described as his "impressionistic" period. The fifth sketch, "Romanian Folksong," is particularly notable.

**First Term at the Piano (Sz. 53)** contains simple pieces for beginning students, based on Hungarian and Slovak folk tunes.

**Mikrokosmos (BB 105)** selections represent Bartók's comprehensive piano method. The three pieces included here (Nos. 74a, 95a, and 112) showcase different aspects of his pedagogical approach.

**Romanian Christmas Carols (Sz. 57)** were collected during Bartók's fieldwork in Romania. The carols (colinde) are traditionally sung during the Christmas season and include both religious and secular texts.

**Romanian Folk Dances (Sz. 56, BB 68)** are among Bartók's most popular piano works. The six dances are:
1. Stick Dance (Jocul cu băţă)
2. Sash Dance (Brâul)
3. In One Spot (Pe loc)
4. Horn Dance (Jocul din corn)
5. Romanian Polka (Poarga românească)
6. Fast Dance (Mărunţel)

**Sonatina (BB 69, Sz. 55)** was originally conceived as a suite of five Romanian dances for piano. It was later orchestrated as well. The three movements are:
1. Bagpipers (Dudások)
2. Bear Dance (Medvetánc)
3. Finale

**Fifteen Hungarian Peasant Songs (Sz. 71, BB 79)** were composed between 1914-1918. They represent Bartók's most sophisticated integration of folk material into art music. The songs are organized into groups: Four Old Tunes, Scherzo, Ballade (Theme and Variations), and Old Dance Tunes.

**Three Hungarian Folk Tunes (Sz. 66)** showcase Bartók's harmonic language applied to traditional melodies.

**Petite Suite (BB 113, Sz. 105)** was originally written for piano duet and later arranged for two pianos. The six movements are based on Ukrainian folk themes.

**Field Recordings** (Tracks 71-76) offer a glimpse into Bartók's ethnomusicological work. These are digital transfers of his original cylinder recordings made during his fieldwork in Eastern Europe. They include:
- Woman Singing
- Girls Singing
- Man Singing
- Peasant Flute
- Peasant Violin
- Peasant Bagpipe
