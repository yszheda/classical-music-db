## [Page 3 - Liner Notes Part 1]

A noble Romantic of the twentieth century, Nikolai Medtner (1880-1951) wrote exclusively for piano, leaving a large body of piano works that include fourteen sonatas, three concertos and various pieces in smaller form (the most famous being *Russian Round Dance* and *Knight Errant*). Nonetheless, unlike the music of his devoted friend Sergei Rachmaninov, the music of Medtner never became widely popular among performers and audiences. His only work for two pianos, *Russian Round Dance* and *Knight Errant, Op. 58*, which are not frequently performed, had not been researched in depth until 2003.

### A Historical Background of Opus 58

The conception of the pieces for two pianos, Medtner's only composition in this genre, was likely inspired by the arrangements of piano pieces by Rachmaninov. Conductor and editor Eduard Illy, who had been studying and performing Rachmaninov's *Symphonic Dances* with an orchestra, should be taken into consideration. This correspondence led to Medtner composing a useful source of inspiration regarding Medtner's approach to writing for two pianos. The composition history of the *Knight Errant* seems to be mysterious - it was dedicated to the popular star in the 40s piano duo Vera Vronska and Victor Babin who, oddly enough, had never performed this breathtaking masterpiece. Neither did Medtner himself: it was performed in Paris by a piano duo several years later.

### Performance Notes on the Opus 58 in the Form of a Dialogue

After exploring and performing a number of works for two pianos, I was surprised by such a multitude of tempo, dynamics, and articulation markings as it is in Opus 58. Medtner usually marks his piano scores in an incredibly detailed way. This requires constant collaborative work between the pianists if they are to achieve harmonizing of their rendering of the expressive content of these pieces. I am strongly hoping that by practicing our dialogue on a continuous basis, we would achieve a remarkably flawless and synchronized performance allowing us to express the same musical idea, tempo and pulse in a way that can usually only be accomplished by a solo pianist. *Russian Round Dance* opens with a vivacious and colorful presto; it is a dance that is actually moving, the same motive which resounds several times in the piece. Several contrasting themes, some purely dancing and others expansive and singing like chorales, remind me of Russian folk melodies. However, unlike other Russian composers, Medtner did not borrow these themes from the folklore, but composed them from scratch.

*Knight Errant*, a brighter and more complex piece of the two, also has a battalion of themes which interact with each other, bringing the piece to the climax in the form of a challenging fugue. It vividly paints a brave yet doubtful wandering knight seeking solution to his ever-lasting dilemma.

## [Page 4 - Liner Notes Part 2]

Russian Rhapsody was written in 1891 while Rachmaninov was a student at the Moscow Conservatory. The composer and Josef Lhévinne premiered the piece at the student recital on November 12, 1891. Even though *Russian Rhapsody* was written one year earlier than his Piano Concerto in F-Sharp minor Op. 1, this work does not have opus number and stands alone. Perhaps the reasons for that was the fact that Rachmaninov never completely finished the piece and the random excerpts were found in the composer's archives. They were assembled by various editors into what is the present version of the *Russian Rhapsody*. Basically, the available version represents particularly an incomplete composition that cannot be played without the permission of the composer. Therefore, the interpretation of this piece can be very loose and flexible in terms of tempos, dynamics and articulation. It gives performers an enviable freedom for creating interesting yet every time different renditions.

## [Page 5 - Artist Biographies]

An accomplished pianist, **Désirée Paulsen Bakke** won prizes in prestigious competitions in her native Norway. She attended the *Staatliche Hochschule für Musik und Theater* in Hannover, Germany. Ms. Bakke performed her piano debut in Oslo shortly after she completed her undergraduate studies. She received a Master of Music degree from the Conservatory of Music in Oslo, Norway. She has performed with the Oslo Philharmonic and Bergen Symphony Orchestra, and had participated in music festivals in Sweden, Norway, Germany, Switzerland and USA. She was featured on radio and television programs in Europe and USA. Her teachers include Eline Nygaard, Einar Steen-Nøkleberg, Jens-Harald Bratlie, Albert Ferber, Karl Engel, and Malcolm Bilson. Recently Ms. Bakke was appointed an accompanist at the Norwegian Music Conservatory.

Native of Azerbaijan, **Saida Kafarova** performed her Carnegie Hall debut recital in June 2000, as a winner of Artists International Special Presentation Recital Award. Dr. Jerome Starr commented, "In my time I have heard all the great - Paderewski, Hofmann, Rachmaninov. You are top rank. Artistry, poetry, soul of the highest order..." She studied with the pupil of Medtner Elmira Safarova, Farhad Badalbeyli, Tatiana Nikolayeva, Anton Nel and Gregory Allen. Dr. Kafarova did a research on Nikolai Medtner's Opus 58 which resulted in a dissertation titled "An Introduction to Nikolai Medtner and Performance Analysis in Dialogue Form of His Works for Two Pianos - *Russian Round Dance* and *Knight Errant*." She performed recitals and presented lectures worldwide.

**The Duo Désirée Bakke and Saida Kafarova** was formed in 2003. Since then, the Duo performed at the Texas Music Teachers Annual Convention in San Antonio, Texas State University - San Marcos, at the International Duo-Piano Festival in Stavanger and recently at the Norges Musikkhøgskole, Oslo.
