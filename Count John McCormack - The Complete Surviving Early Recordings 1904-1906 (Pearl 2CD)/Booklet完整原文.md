## 艺术家介绍
```
COUNT JOHN McCORMACK

Let us arise gentlemen, a prospect of a delight awaits,
Today we hear a song from the greatest singer in the world
Herodotus Book 1, XXIII

This quotation, concerning Arion of Methymna, was written around 445 BC, and is the earliest dated reference to a singer in Western literature. After singing his song sailing on a Corinthian vessel, and fearing the reaction of his rude listeners, Arion jumped overboard fully dressed, but was later rescued by a dolphin.
This early example of stage fright poses the question as to how a performer of one age might be received in another.
Among vocalists of the last 400 years, few have been received with such universal acclaim as Count John Francis McCormack (14th June 1884-16th September 1945), whose combination of stylistic refinement and controlled nervous energy retains a perpetual appeal. It is his ability to sing all things well which entitles his admirers to claim that here was a supreme exponent of the full range of melodic vocal music.
His desire to create a fully integrated art form stemmed from an intention that his repertoire, in both words and music, should form part of some larger whole, making his performances satisfying to the mind as well as the other senses. In this he is a musical equivalent of Poussin, bringing a classical technique to bear on a romantic repertoire. This causes him to be rather different from the traditional cynical view of the Italian tenor – that of a bellowing larynx upon legs. His recordings of the runs in Pur dicesti, Il mio tesoro, Bach's Christ lag in Todesbanden BWV 4, and at the close of Jesus's aria in Beethoven's Christus am Ölberge Op. 85 confirm that he had, possibly, the fastest coloratura of any tenor since Mario (1810-1883). The Beethoven performance represents, in my opinion, McCormack's high water mark.
As an instrumentalist, he could play, with varying degrees of competence, the piano, organ, and harmonica, but for his 30th birthday in 1914 he bought himself a Stradivarius in London for £11,000. Gerald Moore wrote that McCormack's only equal as a sightreader is Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau.
Today, the best place to study the contribution made by McCormack to the history of song is the Archive of Boosey & Hawkes Ltd., in the basement of 295 Regent Street, London, where the huge leather bound tomes, one devoted to each year from 1878 to 1951, will forever attest to his eminence. In the volumes dated between 1907 and 1942 there are no less than 70 songs especially written for his voice, and superscribed "As sung by John McCormack". Many of these scores contain a pencil written date in the top left hand corner, and this is the date on which the score was printed and published. The Boosey concert programmes also contain many dates of first performance, and the following information is published for the first time:

John McCormack was 20 years and 3 months of age when he was invited by Jim White, manager of the London branch of Edison, to record a series of cylinders for them, and the actual session took place very close to 12th September 1904. McCormack had appeared that summer in the USA at the Louisiana Centennial Purchase Exposition in collaboration with the Palestrina Choir, and was regarded at that time as Ireland's most promising young vocalist, having previously won first prize in the tenor section of the Irish National Music Festival, held in Dublin on 19th May 1903, where the adjudicator, Luigi Denza, forecast that with a few years of conscientious study McCormack would stand in the front rank of tenors. McCormack's chosen pieces on that occasion were the aria Tell fair Irene from Atalanta, by Handel, and The snowy breasted pearl. It is good to be able to record that McCormack fulfilled Denza's prediction so completely that the maestro composed the song I held your hand, mentioned above, especially for his voice, whilst some regard the Irishman's recording of Denza's Funiculì, funiculà as perhaps the finest ever made.
Some Edison cylinders are sung extremely slowly in order to fill the cylinder with music, yet all the Edisons were recorded in the same keys as the contemporary Gramophone and Typewriter recordings. These very unnatural tempi, especially on the first two cylinders, are most unfair to the singer, causing the final phrase on The meeting of the waters to last no less than thirteen seconds. In the novel Ulysses, the action of which is set on 16th June 1904, James Joyce wrote, in anticipation of that evening's concert, that he would be hearing "John McCormack. The best in fact." That comment predates these cylinders by less than three months, and begs the question "Has there ever been a better 20 year old tenor?"
As always with early McCormack, the musical beat is marked, while in The snowy breasted pearl, he adopts considerable portamenti in the line, "I love thee, only thee, my colleen, gael, machree". When shall the day break in Erin? is sung allegretto and is hugely enjoyable, even if the orchestra does try to slow him down between verses! In The Irish emigrant, we have a beautiful, almost unplayed dubbing from The Getty Collection, and here we can hear the lovely mezza voce. The performance improves with every succeeding phrase.
Avourneen is one of the best songs of the Anglo-Irish tradition, which did not long survive McCormack's death. It is interesting to note that some of those who wrote and composed most eloquently concerning Ireland had never seen it! The Edison Killarney is sung moderato, as marked by Balfe, and the phrasing is scrupulous, while The green isle of Erin has many portamenti, most notably in the phrases. "And music's the soul of the world's rare enthralling", and "The voice of the green isle, my country and home". This was a feature largely discarded by him after completing his vocal studies in Milan with Vicenzo Sabatini (1825-1913). Love thee? Dearest? Love thee? is sung rather slowly compared with the marvellous 1942 recording, but is still a romantic effusion of love.
The Gramophone and Typewriter discs are sonically much superior to the cylinders, and require less discussion. The problem in transcribing 90 year old cylinders is that many have become oval in shape making tracking difficult, also causing the very occasional skipping of a groove. However, with electrical transcription equipment, the rumble from phonograph machines is avoided, and the hum inherent in cylinders can be completely removed.
The Sterling cylinders of 1906 made after McCormack had become a polished artist are excellent and give much pleasure, particularly where all the recording staff join in the last chorus of A Nation once again! There is fine dramatic fervour here, and the diction is good. Such ballads were extraordinarily popular, and McCormack frequently sang them in the greatest of England's drawing rooms.
A real favourite with the English military was The Boys of Wexford, who, far from minding with the words, greatly enjoyed McCormack's singing of the lines:
"We are the boys of Wexford, who fought with heart and hand. Who burst in twain the galling chain, and freed our native land".
The final cylinder recorded by McCormack was Home to Athlone, made for Edison Bell about 1st December 1906 in their new 4 minute format. It is very fine. This song was published by Phillips and Page.
In the Sterlings, Pathés and in the final Edison Bell recording, McCormack has started to use rubato as borrowed time, rather than stolen time. In this system the length of a musical passage remains constant, and if one phrase is sung slowly to illustrate a particular musical point, the next phrase is sung correspondingly quickly, to retain the shape of the melody. It was here that McCormack scored over his Italian rivals, who saw rubato as stolen time. This was part of the reason for his triumph in the singing of Mozart, Bach, Beethoven, Verdi and Wagner.
A further advance over the 1904 recordings is the use of refined dynamic proportions, causing no abrupt changes in volume, unless called for in the score. This results in continuous enjoyment when listening to an extended session of his records, as he does not fatigue the sensibilities of his listeners which often even short exposure to tenor record recitals can do. One thinks of John Steane's comment that no more than three or four of Caruso's records should be heard at one listening, for this very reason.
One hitherto puzzling point has now been clarified. It used to be thought that the Pathé discs were dubbings of the Sterlings. They are certainly Sterling variant takes. However, in the Pathé recording of The dear little shamrock, there is a mistake in the words on the line, "And still through the bog, through the brake, and the mireland" which does not occur in any other version he recorded.
Following a recent visit to the Edison National Archive in the USA, Mr. Peter Martland came across a letter dated 29th September 1904 from L.G. McChesney, Advertising Manager of the New York branch of Edison to W.E. Gilmore, President, National Phonograph Co. Orange, New Jersey, asking to be sent newspaper clippings of the young Irish tenor who was being recorded by the company at that time, these clippings having been sent to the USA by Jim White, manager of Edison, London.
A second letter, dated 1950, was also discovered, written by William A. Hayes, describing in detail McCormack's recording activities in the years 1904-1910. Hayes stated that he recorded the 1904 Edisons himself, and can recall the tenor returning to the London Edison office in November 1906, having completed his musical education in Italy. McCormack told the Edison manager, that he had done this because he felt the Edison company had given him his first career boost in 1904. According to the minutes of the 29th meeting of the Review Committee, on 8th November 1906, McCormack had recorded a further cylinder for them, and this was described as being "Very good". By this time White had been replaced as manager by Jim Schermerhorn, who insisted upon an exclusive contract, and to this McCormack would not agree.
During this period McCormack had acquired a repertoire of some 25 operas, with a further 12 or so in various degrees of preparation. His operatic career of some 350 performances can be summarised as follows. Covent Garden, 1907-14; Metropolitan, New York, 1910-1919; Monte Carlo, 1921-23; Boston, Chicago and Philadelphia, 1911-18; Manhattan, New York, 1909-10; Dublin, 1906; Paris, 1912-13; Australia, 1911-13. There were also invitations to appear at the Mariinsky in St. Petersburg, and at the Colón, in Buenos Aires. He also sang opera in Italy from 1906-1909.

Another invitation was to appear as Don Ottavio at the Mozarteum in Salzburg in August 1914, but this event was cancelled because of the war. His two operatic creations were the rôle of Lt. Paul Merrill in the world première of Natoma in Philadelphia on 25th February 1911, and that of Gritzko in Moussorgsky's La foire de Sorotchintzi at Monte Carlo on 17th March 1923. It was at Monte Carlo in 1921, where Jean de Reszke heard him in Barbiere, that he wrote, "Bravo. A thousand times bravo for your Almaviva. You are the true redeemer of bel canto".
His career in oratorio was scarcely less distinguished; he appeared in Samson, Elijah, and Messiah from 1907 onwards, and was the favoured Gerontius of Sir Edward Elgar, who wrote to HMV in 1932 proposing a complete recording of the work, with McCormack in the title rôle and the composer conducting. Unfortunately, the economic slump precluded such an ambitious project, and by the time the depression had ended Elgar was dead.
Rachmaninov wrote the song Luchinushka for McCormack, and the manuscript of this song is one of the proudest possessions of the Library of Congress in Washington. The five McCormack recordings of Rachmaninov songs were released with the express approbation of the composer. Records of the songs of English composers were similarly well received, such as two of the Chinese songs of Bantock recorded in 1927, and two of Parry's English Lyrics, made in 1930, also the three Shropshire Lad settings of Somervell made in 1941.
McCormack was also the first artist to release the lieder of Wolf, Brahms, Schubert and Schumann to gramophone audiences in all five continents.
Following three world tours in 1913, 1926 and 1933-34 McCormack retired with the unforgettable recital he sang to a packed audience at the Royal Albert Hall on 27th November 1938, over thirty years after his first recital there.
However, his hatred of Nazi Germany caused him to emerge from retirement in November 1939 to sing for our troops and sailors. The money from these concerts was sent to the Red Cross. It is also an interesting reflection on the complicated relationships within the British Isles that the only musical composition of this Roman Catholic Irish Nationalist was the English patriotic song, Our finest hour, written by him in 1941, published by Chappell, and issued by HMV on DA 1803. Despite suffering from tertiary emphysema, McCormack continued to sing for the cause of freedom and democracy until March 1943, when the voice gave out completely. After that date he could only speak in a hoarse and husky whisper. His health deteriorated rapidly, and he died of pneumonia at Williamstown, Co. Dublin on 16th September 1945. But John McCormack had more than done his duty in the defence of civilised values, and no finer man was ever a singer. He fully justified the remark of Homer Henley, made in the San Francisco Argonaut in 1932, that "John McCormack is the best loved singer humanity ever knew".
Brian Fawcett-Johnston
26th February, 1991
