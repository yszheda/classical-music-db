## Programme Notes

### Dmitry Shostakovich - Symphony No. 5 in D Minor

It is hard to imagine that the fifth symphony of Shostakovich, written in 1937, is the result of official criticism and it is equally hard for 21st century ears to imagine that this composer was taking any notice of political authority when he wrote it. Nonetheless, the previous year his opera *Lady Macbeth* was violently attacked by *Pravda* which called it "Chaos instead of Music" and denounced the "fidgety, screaming, neurotic score" as "coarse, primitive and vulgar". Shostakovich suffered this criticism in silence and resolved to answer it with music rather than words. He did not object when his fifth symphony was dubbed "The creative reply of a Soviet artist to justified criticism" and this work re-established him as the foremost Soviet composer of his generation and has become by far his most popular piece.

In it he retreated from some of the more overtly atonal aspects of his work to date and also resolved to compose a purely abstract musical work with the traditional four movements and a standard orchestra. All of the best qualities of his music come together in perfect balance in this work. There must have been more than just timid reception of official criticism to generate a work of this originality and character and we can safely conclude that to some extent or other he was saying what he was supposed to say in answer to officialdom.

### Leoš Janáček - Taras Bulba: Rhapsody for Orchestra

Janáček's orchestra rhapsody "Taras Bulba" is in reality a kind of musical narrative poem in three acts, its scenes depicting events in the life of the 15th century Cossack hero of Gogol's tale.

The first section deals with the homecoming from the great Seminary at Kiev of the two Taras sons, Andriy and Ostap. The father, Bulba, pleased with their grown maturity and their prowess as fighters, takes them to the Cossack headquarters during the siege of Dubno. There Andriy learns that his love, a Polish girl he has known in Kiev is in the beleaguered city, and he turns traitor in order to join the Poles and be with her. His father, later espying him as he rides with the Polish army, shoots him dead.

The second section of the composition tells of the capture and death of the other son Ostap, and of how the father in disguise witnesses the torture and execution of his own son in the Public Square.

The final movement depicts Bulba's own death; to avenge Ostap, he has raised an army and laid waste whole areas of Polish territory. Captured at last, Bulba is tied to a tree by the enemy and burned, his dying shouts of encouragement to his escaping Cossacks being the final tragic and triumphant note of the work.

## Jascha Horenstein

Jascha Horenstein was born on May 6th, 1898 in Kiev into a large Jewish family. After the 1905 Russian Revolution, the family moved away from the Ukraine, to Konigsberg initially and finally to Vienna where Jascha received much of his education. Here, he was brought up in the high noon of Richard Strauss, Weingartner and Furtwangler. Horenstein brought to music a unique heritage - that of the long line of the great classical tradition fused with the dynamism of all the new music ranging from Mahler and Strauss to Schoenberg, Berg and Bartok. He ranks as one of the greatest conductors of the classics - especially famed as a Beethoven interpreter - and as one of the most exciting exponents of the music of Mahler, Bruckner, Berg, Bartok, Janacek, Hindemith and Stravinsky.

Horenstein took theory with Joseph Marx and composition with Schreker at the Vienna Academy of Music and various Philosophy courses - never formally studying conducting. In 1920 he went with Schreker to Berlin conducting choral societies and assisting Furtwangler - whose rehearsals with the Berlin Philharmonic, he described as "my only conducting class". In 1922, aged twenty-four, he made his orchestral debut in Vienna with the Vienna Symphony Orchestra conducting Mahler's then virtually unknown First Symphony. By 1928 he was appointed Generalmusikdirektor of the Dusseldorf Opera. But by 1932 the Dusseldorf Opera House was receiving anti-semitic letters - "we want a German and not a semitic stranger... a stranger who has no artistic and racial integrity..." so that in 1933 when Nazi race laws forbade Jews from holding official positions, Horenstein fled, touring Europe as a guest conductor.

In 1938, he toured Australia and New Zealand and in 1939, was one of the four conductors who shared the leadership of the Palestine Philharmonic with Arturo Toscanini. By 1939 he settled in the United States, where he conducted all of the major North American symphony orchestras, touring South America extensively as well. He went back to Europe in 1947 for guest appearances with the Concertgebouw in Amsterdam, the Paris Conservatoire and Orchestre National, the London Symphony Orchestra, (setting aflame the British enthusiasm for Mahler) and the Santa Cecilia in Rome. In 1950 he introduced Alban Berg's *Wozzeck* to Paris and the following year, Janacek's *From the House of the Dead*. He went back to Germany for public performances in 1958 initially to conduct Schoenberg's *Transfigured Night* and Brahms' *Symphony No. 4* to huge acclaim. But, as he wrote to a friend, "....it is clear, that UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES can I live in Germany except in a luxury hotel and working as a 'guest' for a week, a month or longer, but only as a guest and not otherwise. Even then my life would be 'monastic' confined to a retreat: the orchestra would be almost a laboratory in which one did one's work and then back to the safety of the hotel."

As it happened, Horenstein was rarely to perform in Germany after his sensational public performances in 1958, but this relative absence was to be to the benefit of other countries, most notably Britain. In 1959 London was taken by storm with a renowned performance of Mahler's 8th Symphony in the Royal Albert Hall which was to play a major part in the significant growth in popularity of Mahler throughout the British Isles.

Apart from a notable collaboration with the London Symphony Orchestra in the 60's, Horenstein continued to be itinerant for the rest of his life retiring finally to Lausanne where in 1973, he died.

## Vox Discography

### Horenstein's recordings for Vox in the 50's

Horenstein's recordings for Vox in the 50's played an important part in establishing his international reputation. Indeed, his recording of Dvorak's 9th Symphony was what attracted the attention of Ernest Fleischmann who promoted his later career. Curiously, he was never taken up by any other label to anything like the same degree.
