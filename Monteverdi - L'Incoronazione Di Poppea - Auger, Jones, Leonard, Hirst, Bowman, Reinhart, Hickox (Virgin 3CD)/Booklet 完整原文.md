## Liner Notes - English (Page 6-7)

### L'incoronazione di Poppea

Here we represent these actions differently', wrote Giovanni Francesco Busenello in the prologue to his libretto for *L'incoronazione di Poppea*. It's one of the earliest operas to insist on the historical veracity of the story, as Alessandro Stradella was to do nearly 50 years later. Fiction in the hands of that drama's creators. But in composing seven years of history (58-65 AD) into a single day, simply altering the people he found in the writing of Tacitus, Suetonius and Cassius Dio in order better to set off all the loves of Nerone and Poppea - in 'real' life criminal and repulsive people.

Nerone's cruelty was soft-pedalled, the great philosopher Seneca appeared as pompous and silly, Nerone's second wife and Poppea's enemy became a murderous plotter and the anti-imperialist poet Lucan (who is described as castrato of the Emperor's singing, when it was later staged by his mistress).

Conventional morals appeared to be topsy-turvy; the 'baddies' came out on top as in Christopher Marlowe's *Doctor Faustus*, and the Great (or, to take a more recent example, Shakespeare's *Richard III*). So we find Poppea and Poppea who engage our sympathies throughout the opera with their 'wrongdoing' as do many of the supposed villains in the Jacobean revenge tragedy that was almost contemporary with Monteverdi's opera. In fact, every character shown in *L'incoronazione di Poppea* is in some way profoundly flawed, a dramatic device also used by Shakespeare in *Measure for Measure* or *Troilus and Cressida*.

Busenello's libretto has suggested that the staunchly anti-imperialist Venetian Republic might have had an agenda to undermine the imperial cult that was so critical to the legitimacy of the Habsburgs as Holy Roman Emperors.

The libretto of *L'incoronazione di Poppea* has survived in some nine printed or manuscript versions, the most important of which were discovered in Udine in 1997. It is thought to have had multiple performances in the composer's lifetime.

All performing versions are based on scores only rediscovered centuries after the opera was written and the surviving performing material gives us many problems. The working assumption is that it was composed in 1642, and that it favours the 'school' of Monteverdi's Venetian operas. But it was first given by a *musica positiva*, a group that probably included Francesco Cavalli, Benedetto Ferrari and Francesco Manelli, and others associated with the Teatro San Moisè in Venice.

The music itself also has its own compositional history, including a recitative style that was the most sophisticated of its day, arioso and complex, and which is also based upon a framework of basses assigned to particular characters. For example, Poppea's and Nerone's music are predominantly lyrical, using as arioso and aria, erotic but also gentle, loving and sometimes even tender music - but the plotting is expressed in dramatic recitative; the intentionally limpid image of Ottone's music illustrates his frustration at Poppea's betrayal of him and inability to take decisive action to bring her back. The music used to illustrate dramatic situation gives the opera a dimension of emotional complexity, be it the work of one composer or four.

© Mike Ashman, 2012
