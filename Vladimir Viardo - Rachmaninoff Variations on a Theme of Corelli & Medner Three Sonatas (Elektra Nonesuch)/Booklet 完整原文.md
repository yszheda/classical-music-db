## 英文说明 (Harlow Robinson)

When he went abroad, Medtner insisted that he had no intention of emigrating permanently; so certain was he that he would be returning within a few years that he declined (unlike Rachmaninoff) to sign any long-term contracts with foreign impresarios.

But Medtner never enjoyed the popular or financial success achieved abroad by Rachmaninoff, who remained throughout a steadfast and loyal friend and mentor. (Rachmaninoff even personally subsidized the publication of Medtner's book *The Muse and Fashion* in Paris in 1935.) It was only due to Rachmaninoff's dogged efforts over a four-year period that Medtner finally made his first tour to the United States in 1924.

Even his long-awaited American debut failed, however, to produce the effect Medtner had been hoping for. Whether because the American market was flooded with brilliant young European pianists in the aftermath of World War I, or whether because he and his dense, carefully-crafted compositions were simply not "fashionable" enough for the American audience and critics, Medtner's extensive concert tour, while generally well-received, did not turn his career around the way he and Rachmaninoff had been hoping.

"America is no joke," the modest, shy, unbusinesslike Medtner wrote to a friend, "and you can fall into the clutches of such people here that you will regret it bitterly."

Medtner's distaste for America and American ways was so great that he even declined a proposal to join the faculty of the prestigious Juilliard School. As an explanation, he said he had no desire to "burden himself" with pedagogical work, but his wife later said the real reason for his refusal was that America was even farther away from Russia than Europe. After returning to Europe, Medtner settled in Paris, where there was a large and lively Russian emigré community including such prominent figures as Sergei Diaghilev, Igor Stravinsky and Sergei Prokofiev.

Medtner's negative, even disdainful attitude towards Prokofiev's sarcastic, "futurist" and frequently dissonant music reveals a great deal about his aesthetic convictions. It also helps to explain why Medtner was less successful than Prokofiev in the faddish atmosphere of Paris in the 1920s. (Like Medtner and Rachmaninoff, Prokofiev left Russia soon after the Russian Revolution, and had been touring widely through the United States and Europe.) Once after hearing a work by Prokofiev at a concert, Medtner remarked: "If that is music, then I am not a musician."

What irked Medtner about Prokofiev was the way he intentionally flaunted the rules of the classical style, inserting parallel fifths, dissonant seconds and awkward chromatic leaps with gleeful abandon, thumbing his nose at accumulated tradition like an automobile outrunning a horse-drawn carriage. "Such talent," complained Medtner of Prokofiev's piano pieces, "and not a trace of formal logic. He goes off wherever he feels like going. Why even bother to have spent centuries working out rules of style and form?"

For his part, Prokofiev found Medtner's music stuffy and pedantic.

Prokofiev's arrogance was also colored by the fact that back in Moscow before the Revolution, Medtner and Rachmaninoff, as the most conservative members of the editorial board of the Russian Music Publishing House, had repeatedly refused to approve the publication of his early works, including *The Scythian Suite*. In a letter to the composer Nikolai Miaskovsky, Prokofiev, a severe and merciless critic, described one of Medtner's Paris recitals. His opinion was largely shared by the city's progressive—and often fickle—musical community.

His playing was technically impeccable, but tedious from the point of view of sound quality," wrote the critic. "Maybe he didn't realize that the piano had a sharp tone, and that the hall was small. Medtner played only his own compositions, two-thirds of them new. It was such dried-up old stuff, so archaic, so thematically and harmonically impoverished, that I thought the man sitting in front of us had suddenly gone crazy, for he had lost his sense of time and was stubbornly insisting on writing in the style of 1800."

Oddly enough, both Prokofiev and Medtner made concert tours of the USSR in early 1927. (Despite his aching nostalgia and insatiable curiosity about what was happening there, Rachmaninoff never expressed a desire to return to play in Russia after 1917.) While both musicians were received with enormous enthusiasm and affection in Moscow, Leningrad and Odessa, there was no question that the younger, more irreverent and ultimately more cynical Prokofiev made a much deeper and more lasting impression. His anti-classical music and athletic, noisy, sledgehammer-smashing image corresponded much more closely to the evolving aesthetic of Soviet music at the time than did Medtner's reflective, cerebral, "priestly" art and persona.

In the end, Prokofiev would return to live and die in the USSR, while Medtner would stay in Europe (he died in England, where he had settled in 1935), never having found a comfortable niche for himself outside Russia. True to his principles, Medtner refused to the very end to respond to political or commercial pressures.

Despite their sometimes fanciful titles, virtually all of Medtner's piano works were strictly to rules of classical form and harmony. Like Rachmaninoff, Medtner was perfectly capable of producing marvelous lyrical melodies (the *Sonata reminiscenza* makes brilliantly clear), but he always kept his themes in check, using them in often complex contrapuntal textures that had led some critics to call him the Russian Brahms.

Because of his fondness (even obsession) for tight thematic integration and structural symmetry, Medtner sometimes fails to achieve the simplicity and emotional power characteristic of Rachmaninoff. Significantly, Medtner found inexhaustible inspiration in sonata form, especially in one movement sonata form as practiced by Domenico Scarlatti. (Medtner's *Sonata in A minor*, Op. 30, ascribed to 1914-15, is a good example.) Rachmaninoff, on the other hand, produced only two sonatas for the piano, and generally preferred a less strictly controlled formal and harmonic approach.

At the same time, Medtner did like to combine his sonatas into larger cycles and to give them evocative titles, in the tradition of Romantic composer-pianists such as Schumann. The wistful *Sonata reminiscenza* (Sonata-reminiscence in Russian; sometimes referred to as Op. 38 in *Zabitye motivy*, Vol. 1 (*Forgotten Melodies*), apparently composed between 1918 and 1920, and incorporating eight pieces in various forms. Similarly, the *Canzona matinata* and the *Sonata tragica* are two of five pieces assembled in the Op. 39 *Zabitye motivy*, Vol II (also ascribed to 1918-20). The pieces in these two cycles are linked only by mood, however, not by any thematic or other musical integration.

Like Medtner, Rachmaninoff made his living primarily as a concert pianist after leaving Russia. Even so, he composed 50 solo works for the instrument between 1917 and 1931. It was the anodic dance melody *La Polka* that finally roused the composer from his long silence. Relaxing in Switzerland during the summer of 1931 with old friends like the Russian bass Fyodor Chaliapin, Rachmaninoff wrote what would also be his last composition for solo piano: a brilliant set of twenty variations, the *Variations on a Theme by Corelli*, Op. 42.

Rachmaninoff came to the *La Folia* tune as used by Arcangelo Corelli (1653-1713) in the *Variationi Seriose* of his *Violin Sonata*, Op. 5, No. 12, in D minor, published in 1700. Most likely, Rachmaninoff was inspired to write his *Corelli Variations* after hearing Fritz Kreisler—to whom they were dedicated—play the Corelli sonata. Before Rachmaninoff, many other composers besides Corelli—Scarlatti, Bach, Cherubini, Liszt and Nielsen—had also made use of this simple but highly productive melody in ¾ meter.

All but two of Rachmaninoff's twenty variations retain D minor as their basic key, and nearly all remain quite faithful to the original theme. The third variation transforms it into a stop-and-go minuet; the eighth (*Adagio misterioso*) foreshadows the sixteenth of the variations in *Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini* (for piano and orchestra), composed three years later. Consistent with the composer's simplicity and restraint, and never overwhelming the modest theme on which they elaborate, the *Variations* are among Rachmaninoff's most successful and popular works for piano.

When the composer gave their world premiere on October 12, 1931, in Montreal, the critical and popular verdict was far from universally favorable, however. In December, Rachmaninoff sent a copy of the score to Medtner (apparently hoping that he would perform them) with a letter that ironically described the reception.

"I have played the *Variations* fifteen times, but only one of these fifteen performances was good...I have not played them in full once," Rachmaninoff mused. "I was guided by the coughing of the public. When the coughing increased, I would leave out the next variation. When there was no coughing, I would play them in order. In one concert (I don't remember where—a small town) the coughing was such that I played only ten variations (out of twenty). My record was eighteen variations (in New York). But I hope that you will play them all and that you will not 'cough.'"

—*Harlow Robinson*

## Vladimir Viardo 简介

Russian pianist VLADIMIR VIARDO has dedicated much of his musical career to interpretations of the Romantic and Russian repertoire, and in particular to late Rachmaninoff. He received his training at the Moscow Conservatory, studying in the tradition of Heinrich Neuhaus under Lev Naumov.

Viardo at the age of 21 was winner of the third "Grand Prix" and the "Prix du Prince Rainier" at the 1971 Marguerite Long-Jacques Thibaud Competition in Paris. In 1973 he was named gold medalist in the Van Cliburn International Piano competition, prompting concert tours in Western Europe and North America, a New York debut at Carnegie Hall, and a position on the faculty of the Moscow Conservatory. His international career was abbreviated, however, when his travel visa was revoked in 1975 by the Soviet government.

For more than a decade, Viardo was forbidden from performing outside of the Soviet Bloc, and restricted in his opportunities to perform and travel within it. When the advent of *glasnost* and *perestroika* opened doors for the creative community, Viardo chose to pursue a career in the West, escaping creative suffocation by the Soviet government as so many Russian artists had done earlier. In 1988 he resumed his international concertizing, appearing again at Carnegie Hall after a 14-year absence. Soon after, he was appointed Artist-in-Residence at the University of North Texas and contracted to tour with the Dallas Symphony.

Vladimir Viardo has since presented recitals and concerts with major orchestras on five continents, which have been highlighted by performances of rarely heard Russian piano literature, including U.S. premieres of works by Tchaikovsky. His previous Nonesuch release features the second piano sonata (op. 61) and 24 preludes (op. 34) by Shostakovich.
