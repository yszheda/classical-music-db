Jennifer Vyvyan's distinctive timbre – pellucid, securely produced, with a fascinating bittersweet tang – was not, perhaps, conventionally beautiful. Yet hers was a voice that conveyed experience. Its individuality, its humanity, enhanced by what some heard as a trace of anxiety in the tone, was what appealed to Benjamin Britten, who wrote a series of major roles for her, from Lady Penelope in *Gloriana* (1953) to Mrs. Julian in *Owen Wingrave* (1970). Most famously, Vyvyan created, hauntingly, the role of the Governess in the UK of the Screws in *Owen Wingrave* and was a stately and dazzling Tyranna in the 1965 Sadler's Wells production of *A Midsummer Night's Dream*. Britten's decision to spell Shakespeare's 'Titania with a 'y' was a jokey tribute to Vyvyan.

Born in Broadstairs, Kent, on 13 March 1925, Vyvyan was descended from a long and colourful line of Cornish baronets, clergy and military men. Her father, a major, collapsed and died during a cricket match, leaving her mother (Biddy) with a three-year-old daughter. The pair then moved to London, where Biddy Vyvyan remarried. Like so many future singers, Jennifer initially showed promise as an instrumentalist, attending the Royal Academy of Music as a pianist before switching to singing. First, first as a mezzo then, encouraged by Roy Henderson (who had taught Kathleen Ferrier), moving up to soprano. After further study in Vienna and Milan, in 1951 Vyvyan became first British singer to win the Geneva International Concours, with a programme that ranged from Handel and Rameau, via Verdi, to Wolf and English song.

By then, though, the pattern was set for a career essentially based in Britain. Vyvyan's fluid and expressive soprano, a gift in Baroque and Classical repertoire, was in regular demand in London opera houses. Although her opera career was dominated by Britten, Vyvyan performed several Mozart roles, including both Idamante and the 1953 Edinburgh Festival Elettra in *Idomeneo*, Konstanze in *Die Entführung aus dem Serail* and Fiordiligi in *Così fan tutte*. The Mozart soprano of choice for Beecham and Busch, she sang in numerous performances of Handel operas, still rarities in the 1950s and 1960s.

Nineteenth-century opera held little appeal for Vyvyan. But her vocal precision and quixotic musical intelligence were ideally suited to the music of her own time. She

Accompanied by harpsichord – and thus intended for indoor rather than alfresco performance – Bach's six canzonette a due mimic a similar vein of refined gallanterie. With their singing, decorously flirtatious shepherds and shepherdesses, the canzonettas' texts can seem impossibly coy today. But as sung by Morison and Vyvyan, they are musically delightful, whether in the airy imitations of 'Gia la notte s'avvicina', the lilting soliloquy 'T'intendo si mio cor', the gentle minor-keyed pathos of 'Che ciascun', or 'Ascoltami, O Clori', an undraped operatic scena.

These prettily Arcadian figures of Clori and Tirsi also turn up in the two little-known duet cantatas by Alessandro Scarlatti exploring the pangs of love. With public performances of opera in early eighteenth-century Rome banned by papal decree, connoisseurs consoled themselves with chamber cantatas in the Sunday conversazioni held at the palaces of wealthy patrons, notably the high-ranking music lover Cardinal Pietro Ottoboni. Scarlatti was the most prolific and influential purveyor of chamber cantatas. His craftsmanship, harmonic inventiveness and melodic flair barely suffer by comparison with Handel, who modelled his own youthful Italian cantatas on Scarlatti's, and was not above pinching the odd idea from him.

Both cantatas here are based on duets – two in Clori e Lisa, four in Floro e Tirsi – with one aria apiece for the two characters. Dated 28 February 1706, Clori e Lisa is a dialogue between two shepherdesses who bemoan the sacrifices – loss of freedom, inevitable heartache – caused by love.

In Floro e Tirsi – evidently intended for a pair of castrati – two Arcadian shepherds grieve and commiserate with each other over their hard-hearted shepherdesses. In the wistful final duet the voices trail away disconsolately as the shepherds recede into the pastoral landscape – a magical ending. 'Beautifully matched [...] in intelligence as well as vocal colour' was Gramophone's verdict on the pairing of Vyvyan and Morison in these miniature undraped operas. To which one could add an impassioned intensity – not least in Floro e Tirsi's penultimate duet 'Ah! fato beato' – and dramatic flair that confirm both sopranos as true stage animals.

Yet it is with Britten's music – not only his operas, but also the Spring Symphony and *War Requiem* – that Vyvyan will always be most indelibly associated. One of her last performances, on 17 March 1974, just weeks before her death from the effects of a long-term bronchial illness, was a War Requiem at the Fairfield Halls, Croydon. Only weeks later, Peter Pears would read the lesson at her memorial service. The career of an equally cherished soprano of the same generation, Elsie Morison (1924-2016), was likewise cut short, not by premature death but by her decision to phase out her singing engagements after her marriage to conductor Rafael Kubelik in 1963. Born in Ballarat, Victoria, Morison made her major professional debut in Messiah in Melbourne in 1944, then moved to study in England where, like Vyvyan, she became a member of Sadler's Wells Opera.

Although she sang in a performance of the Spring Symphony to mark the coronation of Elizabeth II, Morison never worked with Britten's English Opera Group. Yet her operatic repertoire ranged wider than Vyvyan's, from Gilbert and Sullivan to Blanche in the 1958 British premiere of Poulenc's *Dialogues des Carmélites*. She sang Anne Trulove in the British premiere of *The Rake's Progress* at the 1953 Edinburgh Festival, and the same year garnered rave reviews as Mimi at Covent Garden. At Covent Garden she was later acclaimed for the unaffected purity of her singing and sincerity of her acting in roles including Pamina, Antonia in *The Tales of Hoffmann*, Manon in the *Battered Bride* and Micaëla in *Carmen*.

'Clear', 'pure' and 'radiant' were the epithets most habitually reached for when describing Elsie Morison's lyric soprano. Her timbre was slightly brighter than Vyvyan's, more naturally attuned to sweetness and untrammelled by the fluent coloratura and grace of phrasing – shared with Vyvyan – made a consummate singer of Baroque and Classical repertoire, as can be gauged by contributions to this disc of songs and cantatas by two members of famous musical dynasties: Johann Christian Bach (1735-1783), youngest and most worldly son of Johann Sebastian, who made his career primarily in London; and Alessandro Scarlatti (1660-1725), father of the more celebrated Domenico.

Jennifer Vyvyan is on beguiling form in five typically mellifluous songs written by J.C. Bach for his former pupil Frederick Westbead. Both composer and soprano were favourites at the open-air concerts held in London's Vauxhall Gardens, most lavish of the Arcadian idylls created in Georgian England. Among the immense pleasure resort's motley attractions, some more reputable than others, was the opportunity to enjoy an eclectic range of music while consuming wafer-thin slices of ham (from which it was allegedly possible to read a newspaper) and liberal quantities of liquor in special supper booths.
