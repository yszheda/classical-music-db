### Liner Notes by János Kovács

**Allegro barbaro**

Although Bartók's major composition written in 1911 was the Bluebeard's Castle, this Barbarian Allegro piece vies with it in importance as far as the future of the musical language of Bartók is concerned. In the new tone in his works (the Bagatelle No.10, the Allegro barbaro). Yet it had many disappointments. No.1 had already prepared the way for it four years earlier (but it was only published in 1923, and then only as the fifth in the set of Ten Easy Pieces). The title itself is symbolic: Allegro barbaro, meaning barbaric allegro, refers to the first version of the manuscript. According to Ervin Major's research, the title was chosen by a French critic during their first appearance in Paris, where the composer wanted to emphasize the wild, primitive character of the piece. Originally, Bartók described the work in his manuscript as follows: "Allegro Barbaro - a short but very brutal piece in Hungarian style." This composition connected the 1-96-84 metamorphic making of the work. The first version of the work, written in 1911, was then revised in 1918. It is customary to mention the rhythmic pattern recurring with a barbaric stubbornness - the characteristic rhythm of the minor third in the bass of the motif's narrow sphere, as the main feature of the Allegro barbaro. **Three Burlesques Op.8c**

Of Bartók's solo piano works, the Burlesques (1908-1911) and the Allegro barbaro (1911) belong in style to those compositions in which Bartók took the same pioneering attitude as far as idiom and style are concerned as in the case of his other compositions (1908-1911), and as yet with a number of setbacks (the failure of the application for the Kossuth Prize, the criticism of the New Hungarian Musical Society, etc.) in his homeland.

He threw himself into ethnomusicological studies: in 1913 he collected over three hundred authentic Ruthenian folksongs in Máramaros County, between 15 and 27 March. In June, he made a journey to Biskra, in Northern Africa, where he collected Arab folk music in the Biskra region. Later in the year he made another journey to North Africa and Morocco to collect folk material from the Rumanian areas of Hungary.

The Three Hungarian Folk-Tunes which Bartók arranged and harmonized in 1914 are not folksong arrangements in the strict sense of the word. Only a small part of Hungary remained open to his studies—he wrote later in his autobiography—that is the region in the north and north-east of the so-called "Rumanian year" of 1915 in which he devoted his attention to Rumanian folk music. Then, barred from his collecting tours, he took the opportunity to compose several of his most famous works: the Rumanian Folk Dances and Rumanian Christmas Carols. There is another piano composition of this disc, one of the best-known of Bartók's works, the Suite, Op.14 (1916) which ranks third.

**Sonatina**

The Sonatina is the most worthy of concert performances of those written during the "Rumanian year". The work has three movements, and its form follows the sequence of the movements of the classical sonata. The first movement is devoted to bagpipe melodies, the second to a bear dance, and the third to a fast Romanian folk dance.

**Romanian Folk Dances**

The Romanian Folk Dances consist of six short dances, all based on authentic Romanian peasant melodies. They were originally scored for violin and piano, and later arranged for various instrumental combinations. The six short movements remain faithful to the original folk melodies in their melodic contours, rhythmic variations, and retain their original, exotic natural character. The accompaniment, however, cannot be viewed as a mere harmonic framework. In harmonizing these folk themes which are often based on old modes, Bartók was not restricted to using them solely as melodic material, but also incorporated their modal characteristics into his own harmonic language.

**Romanian Christmas Carols**

Rumanian Christmas Carols were written in 1915 and were originally entitled Rumanian Colinde Melodies from Hungary (Melodies de colinde din Ungaria). It consists of two series of arrangements of Rumanian colindas each. Colindas are Rumanian peasant carols, sung at Christmas time by young men going from house to house. Bartók published a whole volume of colindas in 1927, containing 996 pages. As president of the 1918 Universal edition, Bartók with the conscientiousness of a true musicologist, printed the melodies and texts of all the twenty songs, as well as the place where he collected them. The character of each carol is defined by its pedagogic intentions for they contain no embellishment. It was only for concert performance that he added octave pairs for the adult hand at the keyboard, when he found it himself necessary through his own concert experience that the piano was suitable for playing these songs.

As we know, Bartók introduced the first series of six Rumanian carols in 1923, the 'Andante' and 'Allegro' versions, and the second series on 21 March, 1925, in Budapest.

**Suite Op.14**

The Suite, dated "Nákoskeresztúr, 1916. II. 6." is one of Bartók's most important piano compositions of the period. Since the Allegro barbaro, completed in 1911, Bartók had not written a major work for solo piano. In this four-movement Suite we can see Bartók returning to the sonata form, but with a completely new approach to harmony and rhythm. The work is dedicated to his wife, Márta Ziegler.

The miniature movements of the more popular of First series: played attacca; can be divided into three parts played attacca. Allegro moderato (Mesto—giocoso), (I)-(II)-(III). Andante. Allegro giocoso. The second is built on a theme of the colinda.

**Concerto for Piano and Orchestra No.3**

Bartók began to write his Third Piano Concerto in 1945, as a birthday surprise for his wife Ditta Pásztory-Bartók. He worked on the composition until his last days, but it was not complete by the time of his death on 26 September 1945. After the death of the composer, Tibor Serly completed the orchestration of the missing 17 bars. Apart from the orchestration of the missing 17 bars, the short score of the work was completely finished (save the last bar which of course he could not write down). The end of the work is unusual in its practice and thus perhaps unusual: Bartók wrote the last four bars of the score in manuscript on a separate sheet, and the final manuscript score was also completed. Apart from the bars of the last movement (bars 371-377) they were taken by Bartók from a hospital bed a few days before his death.

The concerto has a three-movement form—the first movement is in sonata-allegro form, the second is a ternary form with a central section that functions as a slow movement, and the third is a rondo. The work is characterized by its use of Hungarian folk melodies and its integration of these melodies into a classical concerto form.

**Three Hungarian Folk-Tunes**

The Three Hungarian Folk-Tunes (Sz.66) were arranged by Bartók between 1914-1918. The first tune, "Andante tranquillo rubato," is based on a Hungarian folk melody collected in 1914. The second tune, "At the Jánoshida Fairground," captures the lively atmosphere of a Hungarian country fair. The third tune, "White Lily," is a majestic and stately arrangement of a Hungarian folk song.
