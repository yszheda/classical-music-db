## Inside Booklet - Essay: WEBERN: STRING QUARTETS AND TRIOS
by Paul Griffiths

"Quartet playing is the most glorious music-making there is," Webern wrote to his brother-in-law Paul Königer in 1910, reminiscing about a piece he had played in the cello under his teacher's coaching. For Schoenberg, too, quartets were central, and it became so for Webern. During their first two years together he produced, besides numerous songs, three works that belong here: a slow movement written in Vienna in 1905; the String Quartet that followed during the summer holiday; and a rondo, dated to 1906, which was to have been the finale of a student work.

The essays of a student, and particularly of a Schoenberg pupil, are interesting to us now, partly because the two 1905 pieces, the Six Bagatelles, the Three Pieces for String Quartet, which had been completed in the interim, are the two works he would be proudest of: the 1905 String Quartet, which is by some distance Webern's longest movement, draws energy from Schubertian antecedents and ranges from Webern's first full-scale attempt at writing a sonata-form opening, which is as thorough as it was to dominate, three decades later, the Concerto op.24. But this must be reckoned not for prestige but as mastery - or at any rate a mastery of the 'music as sound' idea in his own work. Originally, in 1924 he put them together to make the Six Bagatelles op.9.

Placing the 1911 movements between the fast piece and a rondo for some 40 seconds of Webern's, and suppressing the song, which he may have felt to the death of his mother. The other sadder that most of the compositions since his op.1 "relate" while working on the Bagatelles, he recalled, 'I had the feeling of having said what I had to say; the piece was finished.'

For a decade after 1914 he did not complete any quartet music (though he did begin a quartet in 1917-18, when he was playing in an army ensemble). As the works he achieved were songs, with words to give them armature. But then Schoenberg's serial method provided a way of carrying on 'once the twelve notes had run out'. Indeed, composition became a strategy for making the twelve notes run out again and again along the same lines. Among his first serial exercises, dating from 1924-25, were various sketches and pieces for piano, string quartet and string trio, including a short, slow movement marked 'Ruhig fließend' (summer 1926), which keeps the tonal gesture, restrained intervals and changing colours of op.9 but restores the 'fließend' motion. The finale is a pulsed, and serialism encourages the quasi-canonic imitation that had been an intermittent feature in his music since the 1905 quartet. Similar features persist in the work that emerged from these studies, the String Trio op.20 (begun in the summer of 1926 and finished a year later), though over the wider span of two substantial movements. Webern later intended to make a three-movement work, but he abandoned the third rondo, for the piece he had written in tradition - of first-movement form, with slow introduction and repeated exposition.

His next and last string chamber piece, the Quartet op.28, was achieved in a little more than a year between November 1936 and March 1938. This time he was able to fulfil his plan for a work in three movements, which could be regarded as a set of variations with some elements of sonata form, a scherzo and a fugue. Each movement of the Quartet is canonic throughout; each is, too, an outgrowth of variations on the four-note cell B-A-C-H (B♭-A-C-B♮), which makes it possible for Webern to create a serial art of fugue that had been important in his music since the Five Movements op.5, and his relations give op.28 a new clarity, which was for Webern the clarity of the Austrian Alps and the Dolomites. But the few details, the shapes, the compressed, the spaces of silence - are as they always were.

- Paul Griffiths
