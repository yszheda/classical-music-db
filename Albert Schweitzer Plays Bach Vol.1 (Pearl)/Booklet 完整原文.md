## 内页 1-2 (HIPPO_20220313_0001.jpg / HIPPO_20220313_0002.jpg)

**SCHWEITZER plays BACH**
**Volume I**

Organists and Bach scholars of the late nineteenth century aided the publicizing of uncritical editions located and codified the surviving manuscripts and the earliest editions of his music. Albert Schweitzer (1875-1965) was deeply involved in the analysis of Bach's musical style, and left articles and a pioneering five-volume set of books that ventured far beyond the confines of biographical scholarship. Together with his teacher, the legendary organist-composer Charles-Marie Widor (1844-1937), they prepared an exemplary edition of the organ works.

Schweitzer met Widor in 1893. In his autobiography "Out of My Life and Thought" he writes: "My life at Mülhausen had brought me so well, that after hearing a recital, Widor took me as a pupil, although he normally directed his instruction to the members of the Organ Class at the [Paris] Conservatoire. This instruction was for us an event of decisive importance. Widor led me on to a fundamental improvement of my technique, and made me strive to attain to perfect plasticity in my playing... We drew our most varied and deepest impressions from this time; they are, thanks to him, the best in the art of interpretation of music."

While studying the organ in Paris, he worked on his Doctorate in Theology and took piano lessons from both paths. As a list pupil, and his close Philipp, Jazil used him both in his study on his physical and experiments on the influential role finger movements have on tone production and the consequent development of one's sensitivity to the nuances of musical art. Her "Étude" first appeared in French and then in German, in Schweitzer's "annotated" graduation, "Etude. Marie. Jazil's guidance I completely. I owe it to him that I developed practice taking but little time. I became through him completely master of my fingers, with great benefit to my organ playing. I became through him, Philipp, which moved more along the traditional paths of piano pedagogy, was also extraordinarily valuable, and protected me from what was one-sided in the Jazil method." Schweitzer had to keep secret his studying with both instructors since "conservatories had a poor opinion of each other."

A trip to Berlin in 1899 brought him in contact with the local organists, which disappointed Schweitzer "because they aimed to execute outward virtuosity than at the true plasticity of tone. But Widor attached so much importance, and how did all others compare with the new Berlin organs compared with that of Cavaillé-Coll's instruments in St. Sulpice and St. Dune."

Schweitzer's existence was divided between theology, medicine and music. He approached Bach in a scholarly way, striving for accuracy and unity of expression. The organs he was most acquainted with were of his taste. While satisfied with the organ at All Hallows's Church, he preferred working the organ of the organ in Strasburg. An awareness of Bach's voice led him to believe that this organ builder made him relish the survival of such an instrument.

Appreciation for the construction and design of this and all the instruments he played originated with Schweitzer's grandfather, who had been an organist with a keen awareness of organ sound. The introduction to the edition prepared with Widor, "The Art of Organ Building" on "The Interpretation of the Preludes and Fugues" outlines his aesthetic in the baroque. His observations venture beyond acoustics: they serve to define the sound of the organ evident here in its own playing. The notes of the chorale preludes are to be played with great clarity: we know nothing positive concerning the tempi in which Bach played his organ works. As in all his works, however, we are guided by Voices of tradition apart that the music demands of us. We play in very lively time. We should beware of drawing from these assertions, too sweeping conclusions with regard to his organ playing. He never went beyond what the organs of the period permitted. Two assistants were to draw the stops: one at his right hand, the other directly behind him, posted at that section of the organ located at the rear. Even such stops as Schweitzer could himself reach, even if it were just a matter of a ledger, as, with proper and characteristic conscientiousness he would not allow himself for any reason to interrupt the contrapuntal lines.

"One forgot everything for all the moment: the awkward manipulation of the stops, the noise of the shrunken mechanism, even the player himself. Only Bach remained." That was the complete relegation of all agencies of performance to a position of total unimportance, with a corresponding glorification of the music itself. Modern virtuosity of every type has too often created a barrier between the composer and the listener. Too often, indeed, is the music no more than a vehicle for the self-expression of the interpreter. Of all that there was nothing on that evening in Strasbourg. For once there was the realization of that so oft dreamed ideal, the artist at one with the composer."

Davidson captures the essence of Schweitzer's Bach performance. Even his choice of registration is rendered unobtrusive, as if to avoid any reminders of the non-musical elements. The instruments available could hardly have been a great improvement in sound and choice.
