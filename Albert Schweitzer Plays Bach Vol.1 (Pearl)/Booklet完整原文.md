ALBERT SCHWEITZER (1875-1965)

Pupil and disciple of the great Widor, Albert Schweitzer, scholar, theologian, musician and lover of mankind was a most remarkable organist and, later in his life, probably the most famous man in the world. His recordings (on this disc dating from 1935/36) reflect his view of Bach: massive, approachable and filled with his own personal spirituality.

## Booklet正文

SCHWEITZER plays BACH Volume I

Organists and Bach scholars of the last generation were united in the publication of unedited scores located and codified the main sources for authoritative editions of his music. Albert Schweitzer, as physician, scientist, and organist, became involved in the analysis of Bach's musical style, and left articles and a profound biography of the Cantor of Leipzig, which ventured far beyond the boundaries of the traditional musical dictionary. Together with his teacher, the legendary Maréchal-Widor (1845-1937), Schweitzer helped to prepare an exemplary edition of Bach's organ compositions. Schweitzer met Widor in 1893. In his autobiography On Music and Thought (1958), "My teacher taught me how to sight read, and under his guidance I acquired the technique of my predecessors, and made myself familiar with the great art of organ playing. At the same time there dawned on me, thanks to him, the genius of Bach's music."

After studying theology in Paris, he worked on his Doctorate in Theology and took piano lessons with a pupil of Liszt, Marie-Jacqueline Jaell, and with the organist Lidia Philip. Jaell had him perform experiments on the influence of finger movements, and this had some bearing on Schweitzer's later production and the later development of one's sensitivity to the physical act of playing the keyboard. "Touch" first appeared in French pedagogy of piano in a "Method" published in 1834 by an "anonymous" translation. "Under Marie Jaell's guidance I considerably improved my piano playing, and became acquainted with the complexity of my fingers with great benefit to my organ technique...M. Jaell, like Widor, Philip, who moved more closely to the traditional paths of pedagogy, was also extraordinarily valuable, and protected me from what is known as the "American method." Schweitzer had to keep secret his studying with both his teachers, because "both of them had a poor opinion of each other." At that time he also came in contact with the local organists, and through Widor he had access to the organs to which he paid so much attention. Widor had a predilection for the new Berlin organs compared with that of Cavaille-Coll's instruments in Paris; and this had particular importance.

Schweitzer's existence was divided between theology, medicine, and music, even if his first works are scholarly; his approach was that of a practitioner and a man of duty and experience. The organs suited his temperament, as well as the best of his taste. While satisfied with the acoustics of the Silbermann organ in Mulhouse, he preferred working on the Silbermann organ in Strassburg. An awareness of Bach's love of the Silbermann organs led this organ builder to make him relish the survival of such an instrument.

Appreciation for the construction and design of this and all the other organs can be attributed to his association with Schweitzer's grandfather, who was an organist with a keen interest in organ construction, and in the edition prepared with Widor, he stressed the importance of the pedals. "The interpretation of the Preludes and Fugues had to be both monumental and lyrical instincts. His observations venture beyond advice they serve to remind us of the methods evident in his own playing. We may know nothing positive concerning the instruments that in which Bach played his organ in his various posts, in his lifetime. Voices of tradition assert that this organ was similar to the surviving organs of the period. At that time there were about a dozen organs of the eighteenth century, which are still in existence (the Silbermann organ of Mulhouse, the only one especially mentioned) afford opportunity for experimenting with tempi and establishing approximate limits. Anyone who has played on the Tandena organ and chastened himself with the heavy action and deep-toned voicing must feel that under such conditions this is the only way. It sounds only a modern instrument.

Once again, one considers the instruments to which the compositions in question were originally restored, or, if their original actions (their weight and dimensions) were replaced long before Bach's day, or, if Bach himself (as in Leipzig) found no suitable instruments permitted Schweitzer's playing maintains a strict, unbroken beat; one must be deaf indeed not to hear that the Toccata in D minor is written on the very principle of rhythmic freedom intended to exhort the player to indulge in freer playing. The same is true of the Toccatas in F minor (BWV 538) and C minor (BWV 911). Certainly the strict beat is permissible only for the "historicity-seeking" purists.

Like Mozart's own metronome marking for adapting his compositions to modern musical taste, the space in which a work will unfold itself, it is imperative that the performers in the auditorium take the regulation of the tempo is recognized. In practice, however, it is too often not taken into calculation, and one receives more or less, from the general impression that, under the given conditions, it makes no difference. There are not many who neglect to go downstairs from the loft to hear how the fugues and preludes executed by Schweitzer sound at his strictly fixed metronome tempo. He will be surprised at the relative effect of the acceleration or retarding in choosing a tempo simply because it sounds beautiful on a modern instrument.

This loosening from the body of the church is indispensable in settling the question of tempi, particularly when establishing the right tempo is "first determined by the acoustics of the building." The note should be ushered over; when the point should be hot. When playing Bach's cantatas, he notes, the tempi would have been far swifter.

Schweitzer was equally meticulous in his choice of instruments. His edition contains notes to this indication. The performer need not be familiar with all of Bach's music to appreciate Schweitzer's distinctive performance style. Schweitzer writes:

"He writes for the woodwinds as if they were able to phrase like singers; and this he did on the organ. He takes the nature of the instrument in so far into account, that at the point where he would have made the melodic progress in decidedly simpler lines in a concerto or chamber orchestra, in respect to the klavier or flauto traverso, he intended for the violin or oboe. He has the phrasing of the clavier-organ style as well as that of the violin-bow. From the beginning he wanted to write organ music for organs. He arranges violin concertos of an Italian master as organ pieces, transcribed by the violin itself. But for the organ-style was merely to imitate the orchestra with a keyboard instrument. In his compositions Bach writes exactly as he would for the string orchestra. To play an organ piece perfectly means to render its orchestral elements as they were controlled by bow instruments controlling their phrase and articulation.

Schweitzer's interest in violins bow capable of sustaining the polyphony led to a very interesting experiment described in an article, "The Round Violin Bow and Albert Schweitzer," in which he collaborated with a Danish bow maker to produce the Vega bow, a round bow fitted with a thumb-lever capable of altering the tension, and therefore the tone-quality, on the Romantic-style bow.

Outstanding recordings of the Bach organ works provide a vivid impression of an autobiography of the music." (Professor Archibald E. Davison of Harvard, who studied under Schweitzer in Strasbourg in 1921, wrote of a 1925 recital of Schweitzer playing on a Silbermann organ.) "When Schweitzer sat down to play the Prelude in E flat major it became a memorable evening, one was literally transported back to the day of Bach. And there came vividly to mind all of the qualities that Schweitzer had always been known to demand in his admonitions born not out of what one ought to do, nor what Bach had he had at his disposal the instruments of today, but, rather, that this music, therefore, should be played as Bach actually composed, and, if necessary, on the organ, which he played." "Before Schweitzer began to play, he made sure that all was in readiness, then the first performance. Two assistants were there, one on his right hand, the other one the left. They were posted at the two ends of the organist located at the rear. Even such steps as Schweitzer considered necessary required the care of a helper, as, with proper and characteristic modesty, he himself would not allow us to interrupt the contrapuntal lines.

"One forgot everything for the moment: the hour, the place, the audience, the noise of the atrium mechanism, even the place where the music was. There came the complete religious of music itself without consideration of position of total unlikeness, with that of a concert of music without itself. Modern virtuosity of that type too often created a burden, the strain of listening for the listener. Too often, indeed, it is the very antithesis of self-expression of the interpreter. Of all that was there was nothing of the kind. For there was the presence of that so-to speak dreamed ideal, the artist at one with the composer."

Davidson captures the essence of Schweitzer's attitude here. Even his choice of registration was governed by his desire to create no distraction from the musical elements. The instruments available in his home did not permit much improvement in sound and choice. He had recorded Bach on the harmonium in London in an ordained ceremony opened by his wife in Neuchâtel, Nov. 11, 1936, and some four months later in the concert hall at Queen's Hall, did not turn out in his favor. He sought in London for an instrument that would be the equal of that on the organ of All-Hallows-by-the-Tower, but the sessions that were successful and hence published are presented in this volume. He also played in Strasbourg in February 1936 on a Silbermann organ. After an advance trial of "expert" he decided to begin. An exhausting series of exhaustive work produced his two volumes of his two volumes of Bach, from which two Chorale Preludes and the A minor Fugue on this recording derive (see Track 47). Although Schweitzer undertook recording with a care peculiar to his character, he later (see Master Band 4) re-recorded the selections on different days. The approved results, made with a fine feeling, the later take begins four measures ahead of the earlier breaking point, thus obscuring the return of the A min. 47. Other than this oversight, hear Schweitzer's view of the opening of the Prelude as approachable, and filled with his personal spirit.
