## Liner Notes: Brahms and Fiedler

**Johannes Brahms (1833-97) and Max Fiedler (1859-1939) are both associated with Hamburg. Brahms, the son of a double bass player in theatre orchestras remained a bachelor, always living in furnished rooms. Strong coffee, cigars and ale (he died from cirrhosis of the liver) were as much a part of his daily life as was his music. Before leaving Hamburg for Vienna he conducted a ladies choir. The latter half of his life was spent in Vienna except for his annual holidays spent in neighbouring countries. He refused a degree offered by Cambridge University as he had no wish to travel to England.**

**Brahms' Academic Festival Overture was first performed in 1881 when he received a doctorate from Breslau University, which he did attend. Cambridge was not to be outdone and the following year a performance was given with a choir singing Gaudeamus igitur, with Brahms' permission.**

**Brahms' Symphonies are romantic in effect whilst following construction clearly derived from classical works. Brahms started to compose his Second Symphony while on holiday on the Carinthian Alpine lakes in 1877. When not composing he went for long walks in the surrounding countryside. Hans Richter conducted the first performance with the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra in the following December. It was great success from the start. The Fourth Symphony was the product of his holiday seven years later at Mürzzuschlag, on the outskirts of Vienna. He finished it there during his holiday in 1885. This time the Viennese public took longer to accept the work. By 1897 when Brahms attended a performance in Vienna, the audience gave a standing ovation at the end of each movement! Although this response may have been in part due to the audience knowing this was to be the last time they would see Brahms, already known to be terminally ill.**

**August Max Fiedler started out as a pianist. While at Leipzig Conservatory he studied conducting. Later Fiedler was appointed to a teaching post. Apart from conducting in Hamburg he conducted the London Symphony Orchestra in 1907, the Boston Symphony Orchestra from 1908 to 1912 and was thereafter associated with the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra.**

**Max Fiedler displays a flexibility in his tempos typical of the early years of the twentieth century. During Fiedler's association with the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra the style of playing was closer to modern orchestras than other contemporary orchestral styles. However portamento, no longer in fashion today, was still popular in the 1920s, even if it less marked in the BPO than other contemporary orchestras.**

## Fiedler's Brahms Symphonies – A Personal View

**Listening to the acoustic recording of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony conducted by Artur Nikisch with this same Berlin orchestra, gives me no idea of what Nikisch was like than does watching the short piece of silent film of him that also exists. In a silent film we obviously cannot know what the piece is (although theories range from Beethoven to Tchaikovsky) and we never see any orchestra (my belief is there wasn't one).**

**There was not exactly an orchestra on the acoustic recording either since poor Artur Nikisch was actually engaged in steering a sort of enlarged chamber ensemble, with trombones trying to sound like double-basses, into a large papier-maché horn.**

**It is tantalising because it would be quite wonderful to hear Richter's Wagner, or Mahler's Beethoven, and the value of the present recordings is that Max Fiedler conducted these symphonies in front of their composer who, not given to reticence when expressing himself, does not appear to have complained. There may have been some things Brahms liked and some he did not, and over the years Fiedler himself may well have changed how he played things. But that in no way detracts from their interest and, unlike poor Nikisch, this conductor is standing in front of a complete orchestra – and a great one; and they are being recorded clearly with the benefit of the new electrical process.**

**And what an amazingly good process it was! The trouble is frequently that well-intentioned people cannot resist interfering with it. The only point in listening to this kind of thing is, surely, to participate in something that took place long before we were around, and if the price for that happens to be an accompanying 78rpm surface noise, well, fine. As soon as you mess around then it is no longer the truth. I suppose the tempos remain the same but that is about all. The real sound of an orchestra is replaced by something more anaemic, and the balance (one of the main things a conductor does after all) is gone forever. To be honest, I have been respectfully bored by these performances for several years until really hearing them now for the first time.**

**Furtwangler once remarked to Dr. Friedrich Schnapp, his personal tonmeister from 1939, "The best thing about your recordings is that you don't do anything." I know what he meant.**

**Max, no relation to Artur, although both had links with Boston (albeit somewhat different ones), was seventy-one when he recorded Brahms' Fourth Symphony, and the Second Symphony was recorded a year later. The same age as Klemperer and Gunter Wand, and all three of them ten years younger than Bruno Walter when he last recorded them.**
