## Liner Notes by David Fanning

With very little formal training, Heitor Villa-Lobos mastered first the cello and then the guitar, and before he turned twenty he was already living a vagabond existence, picking up jobs in dance bands and writing down the popular tunes he heard. After seven years or so of this, he went home to Rio de Janeiro and, still impatient with teachers and classes, taught himself composition by studying the scores of the old masters.

From 1923 to 1930 Villa-Lobos lived in Paris and was lionized by forward-looking musicians from pianist virtuoso Artur Rubinstein to the avant-garde composer Edgard Varèse. Back home in Rio again, he was given the task of organizing musical education for the masses and in the public schools. Ironically for this previously disinterested student, he ended up founding both Brazil's national music conservatory and its honorary academy for distinguished musicians.

Villa-Lobos was a prolific composer: his works for the stage and concert hall number high in the hundreds, and he lived long enough to see his music vilified as too avant-garde in the 1920s and dismissed as too Romantic and "pop" by the postwar generation of serialist composers. Today, with such controversies mostly behind us, we are free to enjoy the undimmed freshness of his Brazilian melodic gifts, and to be thrilled by his flair for drama and virtuosity. Several pieces by Villa-Lobos have remained concert favorites ever since he introduced them. Leading this list is *Bachianas Brasileiras No. 5* for soprano and eight cellos (or rather, eight cello parts, since the piece may also be performed with a larger ensemble, as on this recording). In fact, the entire *Bachianas* series became popular enough to make Villa-Lobos complain that his other work were being neglected in their favor.

Upon returning to Rio in 1930 to take up his educational duties, Villa-Lobos found himself thinking about the difference between the universal and the particular in music: the latter represented by its native Brazilian tunes and rhythms, the former by the music of his beloved J.S. Bach, whom he considered a universal folkloric source, rich and profound, with all popular sound materials from all countries, intermediary to the astral infinite to take up the motto adopted by the Bach chorale, the music of Bach comes from the astral infinite to find itself in the earth as folk music.

Villa-Lobos gave musical voice to this idea in a series of suites for various combinations of instruments and voices, each called *Bachianas Brasileiras*. The title itself is ambiguous: when translated literally it can be just one suit, might be translated as "Tributes to Bach in a Brazilian style." Between 1930 and 1945, Villa-Lobos composed nine such works. They are in no way pastiches or slavish imitations of Bach's techniques and style; rather, they are Latin-American music "inspired," the composer wrote, "by the musical atmosphere of Brazil." Most of them begin with an ABA form: an exuberant, contrasting middle section, and return to the first theme. Villa-Lobos underlined the music's dual nature by giving each movement two titles, one from the nomenclature of Bach's Baroque era and one from popular Brazilian music.

**Bachianas Brasileiras No. 4** was composed in 1930 for piano solo and orchestrated ten years later, begins with a "Preludio (Introdução)" which is Baroque in the uniform shape of its phrases but Brazilian in its suggestion of a vast, lonely landscape. Similarly, "Coral (Canto do Sertão)" is derived from religious songs of Northeast Brazil's open country, accompanied by sounds of nature, especially the peeping single note of the araponga bird. The same regional influences continue in the two last movements: "Aria (Song on a Northeastern Theme)," which quotes and varies an actual folk song, and "Dança (Miudinho)," whose title refers to a fast, intricate couple dance that came to Villa-Lobos's salons from the beaches and village squares of Bahia state.

**Bachianas Brasileiras No. 5** begins with the famous "Aria (Cantilena)," which (except for a central, declamatory interlude) seems to pass by in a nostalgic dream. Villa-Lobos indicates this identification with the song by having his own instrument, the cello, play it in unison with the vocalist. From the soprano's song, an octave above the cellos, becomes a mystical, half-heard overtone to the melody. Often the cello's seem to be imitating the composer's other favorite instrument, the guitar; at other times, he looks for ways to make them sound like a whole orchestra. To the first movement, composed in 1938, Villa-Lobos added in 1945 the "Dança (Martelo)"—a punning reference ("hammer" in Portuguese) both to the constant rapping of repeated notes in the music and to the intricate meter. Martelo is the name of the song-form that are traditional in Brazil's Northeast. Villa-Lobos has the singer imitate the calls of many species of birds, as if the feathered creatures have their own martelo.

**Bachianas Brasileiras No. 7** was composed in 1942, when he composed *Bachianas Brasileiras No. 7*, Villa-Lobos was writing in a Dvořákean nationalistic idiom, using the orchestra of the Romantic era—with Brazilian percussion, of course. No. 7 begins with a "Prelude (Ponteio)" that develops a song (in the style known as *modinha*) from a quiet serenade into a grand, populist march: the layered orchestration is inspired by Bach's great toccatas and fugues for organ. "Gigue (Country Quadrille)" is one of many typically Irish-French, then French jig (jig) viewed through the lens of a European square dance (quadrille) as interpreted by the African, Indian, and Iberian peoples of Brazil. "Toccata (Desafio," or "challenge") turns a country dance into an orchestral showpiece, driven by ostinato figures on the xylophone. The piece ends with a splendid "Fugue (Conversa)," again reflecting Bach's organ style.

In 1945 Villa-Lobos concluded the *Bachianas* series in a nostalgic mood with No. 9, which contains allusions to other pieces in the series, including the "preludio" of No. 2. His indication that it could be performed by string orchestra, wordless chorus, or both, as on this recording. It consists of a prelude and fugue, the one ineffable and mysterious in character, the other marked by an unusual 11/8 meter and a "primitive" main theme. Although Villa-Lobos saw this latter as reflecting the music of Brazilian Indians, it appears here in a highly sublimated, Bachian form.

For explicitly Indian-influenced music by Villa-Lobos, one can turn to his series of works entitled *Chôros*, dating from the mid-twenties. These pieces, named for the street musicians of Brazil, are composed for various media, ranging in size from a solo piano to a full orchestra with Brazilian percussion and chorus, as in the masterpiece of the series, *Chôros No. 10*. It was written in 1926. The composer's aim in this tone poem was ambitious: to evoke not just the life of Brazil's European-style cities, nor the spiritual character of the Indian rain forest, but to bring both together in a meaningful encounter. In so doing so in an era that saw the East-West dynamics of Stravinsky's *Firebird* and *Rite of Spring*, and the influence of African art on the fauve painters and Picasso. His motile-riots mix samba, baião, and European dance rhythms, do not last their freshness and power, and this self-taught composer's handling of large orchestral and choral forces is nothing short of spectacular.

Remaining vibrancy and warmth, soprano **Renée Fleming** enraptures audiences with her natural beauty of tone and lustrous timbre. She made her Metropolitan Opera debut in 1991 as the Countess in *The Marriage of Figaro* and in 1995 sang alongside Plácido Domingo as Desdemona in Verdi's *Otello*. In the 1995 season she has sung the title role in the San Francisco Opera's production of Dvořák's *Rusalka* and performed in Gounod's *Faust* with the Lyric Opera of Chicago and the Bastille Opera in Paris, and in past years has appeared at La Scala, Covent Garden, Vienna State Opera, Glyndebourne and the Teatro Colón. Completing her operatic engagements have been a host of solo recitals at prestigious international opera houses: the Requiem Mass in Paris and in Strauss's *Four Last Songs* on tour with the Metropolitan Opera Orchestra under Levine. In recital, she has performed recently at the Vocal Arts Society in Washington and in New York at the Great Performers series at Alice Tully Hall. In March 1996 Renée Fleming was awarded the first Soli Prize of L'Académie du disque lyrique.

The **New World Symphony** is an intensive learning and performing experience that prepares highly gifted graduates of prestigious music schools and conservatories for leadership positions in orchestras and ensemble groups throughout the world. Established in 1987 under the artistic leadership of Michael Tilson Thomas, the New World Symphony provides the opportunity for graduates to enhance their music education in order to gain a competitive edge in seeking permanent orchestral and ensemble positions. Musicians perform with distinguished conductors and soloists, take part in master classes with leading pedagogues, and are exposed to both traditional and modern repertoire; alumni are now members of such orchestras as the Boston Symphony, Cleveland Orchestra, Los Angeles Philharmonic, San Francisco Symphony, as well as ensembles in Germany, Hong Kong, Spain and Sweden. Although only eight years old, the New World Symphony has already performed at such prestigious venues as New York's Carnegie Hall, London's Barbican Centre and Argentina's Teatro Colón. It has toured extensively in Europe, Brazil, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Japan, Morocco and throughout the United States. At its home base in Miami Beach the New World Symphony presents a full season of concerts from October through May at the Lincoln Theatre and at the Gusman Center for the Performing Arts.

**Michael Tilson Thomas** is a conductor, pianist, educator and composer. At the age of 51, Michael Tilson Thomas's accomplishments include more than 200 recorded works, appearances with the major orchestras of the world, a series of television and video releases, international pedagogical work and a life-long devotion to holding the positions of Founder and Artistic Director of the New World Symphony, Music Director of the San Francisco Symphony, and Principal Conductor of the London Symphony Orchestra.

Born in Los Angeles in 1944, he belongs to a family that has followed artistic careers for three generations. He studied at the University of Southern California. Director of a masterly achievement with Stravinsky, Boulez, Stockhausen and Copland on premieres. During this same period he was pianist and conductor for Gregor Piatigorsky and Jascha Heifetz. A student of Friedelind Wagner, he was Musical Assistant and Assistant Conductor at the Bayreuth Festival.

In 1969, having won the Koussevitzky Prize at Tanglewood, he was appointed Assistant Conductor of the Boston Symphony Orchestra. That same year he made his New York debut with the Boston Symphony and gained international recognition after replacing Music Director William Steinberg in mid-concert. He was later appointed Principal Guest Conductor of the Boston Symphony Orchestra, where he remained until 1974. He was Music Director of the Buffalo Philharmonic from 1971 to 1979, and Principal Guest Conductor of the Cleveland Orchestra from 1981 to 1985. Beginning in 1986, Mr. Tilson Thomas held the post of Principal Conductor of the London Symphony Orchestra. His tenure produced a number of critically acclaimed recordings.

In June of 1990, along with Leonard Bernstein, Michael Tilson Thomas inaugurated the Pacific Music Festival in Sapporo, Japan. Since then he has returned each summer as Artistic Director. Michael Tilson Thomas was named Music Director of the San Francisco Symphony in June 1995 under a contract that will take him into the next century.
