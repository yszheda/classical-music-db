## Liner Notes by Stanley Henig

There is a tendency amongst many collectors to regard cylinders as of less significance than discs. This is perhaps engendered by absence of facilities for playing them. What we loosely call the 'gramophone' was the major access to recorded music for more than eighty years. During that time there were huge advances and improvements made to domestic players, but these were not paralleled for machines to play cylinders. However, the true aural quality of most early cylinder recordings is certainly not inferior to that of the earliest discs. Perhaps this needs to be highlighted particularly with this present disc. All of the tracks on the CD were not made at any dawn of commercial recording, indeed some of them pre-date that dawn. Apart from intrinsic rarity, their importance rests on the reputations of the singers, and interest in the era they represent.

Aramburo was a star and most of those of Lassalle are on cylinders. All the most important operatic careers in the nineteenth century and are amongst the earliest singers to record. The names and recordings of Caruso, Chaliapin and Litvinne are of wider currency. However, their early cylinder recordings add to our knowledge of their art.

Several of the artists here appeared previously on Symposium Compact Discs. (The transfers are on symposium with the transfers from Professor Henig's cylinders numbers are in an appendix.) Accordingly, this note focuses particularly on artists new to the series, especially Aramburo. His cylinders, of those who have survived most are included in this album, were only recently discovered. Aramburo has had nothing published about him—far less than is the case with most of the other artists featured.

Spanish born, ANTONIO ARAMBURO (1838-1912) was almost certainly the very first artist to have robustly recorded. For late nineteenth century opera-goers this was a relatively new kind of voice of which Enrico Tamberlik was perhaps the great exponent. It lends particular historic significance to these recordings even though they were made in 1901, when he was sixty and a veteran of a career that he was virtually retired from the concert platform. Aramburo's operatic career extended from 1871 to 1896, a quarter century particularly noteworthy for first rate sopranos, indeed the great critic Hermann Klein devoted a whole book to them. The careers of Lilli Lehmann and Adelina Patti covered the entire quarter century. Ilma de Murska, Pauline Lucca, Christine Nilsson and Theresia Tieflens were amongst the great names of the early seventeen. Singers like Calvé, Nellie Melba, Lillian Nordica and Marcella Sembrich their equivalents by the mid-'90s. It would not be possible to list a contemporary 'top ten' group of tenors of the same status. Jean de Reszke, Ernesto Tamagno, Francesco Tamagno were certainly at the very apex of the singing profession, but others do not come to mind.

London opera-goers were able to hear Aramburo only in 1873, very early in his career. It seems that the impresario Mapleson was struggling to get first class tenors to match the sopranos at his disposal. The previous season the tenore scheduled to sing Tamino in Die Zauberflöte was taken ill when the company was on tour to Newcastle. Contrary to Mapleson's intention, so Mapleson's biographer, Marie de Mesiakn "she made such an impression that some of the critics suggested that in the present scarcity of tenors (she) should give up dramatic roles." Mapleson was, of course, a great showman and his company included Titiens and Trebelli with Mongini in the tenor part of Gennaro. Musical World appraised the company: as well as Tietjens, Mapleson could boast of Nilsson and de Murska, Clara Kellogg and Marie Roze. A laconic comment followed, "The permanent tenor is Signor Ardavani". It was in this way, says Mapleson, that he got Campannini, Fancelli etc. among the tenors, in addition to Signor Antonio Aramburo, about whom there has been a lot of talk." Perhaps most of the talk was by the impresario himself, William Mapleson. His advertisements claimed him "From the leading theatres of Milan, Rome, Barcelona, Messina etc." Interestingly, the chronology by Tom Kaufman lists no previous appearances in Rome or Messina, whilst his performances in Milan were at the Teatro dal Verme and not La Scala.

This album ends, as it began, with a tenor. It is almost a triumph that the fame of ENRICO CARUSO (1873-1921) is sufficiently secure to need no further note. His three Pathé cylinders were long regarded as his very first recordings. Modern scholarship has demonstrated that in fact they were recorded after both the main sessions for the Gramophone and Typewriter records. For wholly reasons, cylinders, made by the Anglo Italian Commerce Company and issued by Pathé were not seem to be as successful as many recordings which appeared on the Pathé label. However, they are of special interest in two other ways. They were never otherwise recorded by Caruso, and in addition, the company's policy of including announcements allows us brief samples of the singer's speaking voice. Caruso's reputation was far larger than that of Aramburo, and he sang most of the same roles associated with the older artist. To be fair, any comparisons must take into account that when their cylinders were made Aramburo was in his sixties, over twice Caruso's age. Perhaps the most interesting reflection is that since the time of Caruso, most of the parts which both artists vocalized have become the preserve of the lirico spinto rather than the tenore robusto.

Stanley Henig
