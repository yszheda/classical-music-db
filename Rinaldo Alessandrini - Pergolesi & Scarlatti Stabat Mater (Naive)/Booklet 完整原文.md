## Back Cover Notes

Both Pergolesi's famous *Stabat Mater*, which Bach himself admired, and Scarlatti's setting, with its superbly flowing polyphony, show a positively theatrical concern with expressiveness – whether dramatic or poignant – and musical illustration of the text. These features are brought to the fore in this new interpretation, which brushes aside a few misconceptions and uses the original dynamics and tempi to thrilling effect.

Beide Werke, das *Stabat Mater* von Pergolesi, das Bach so bewunderte, und auch Scarlattis Vertonung mit ihrer subtil gewebten Polyphonie, sind – durch ihre beschwörende Ausdruckskraft und eindringliche Textillustration – in höchstem Maße theatralische Kompositionen. Diese neue Interpretation unterstreicht diese Qualitäten, indem sie mit den originalen Tempi und der ursprünglich vorgesehenen Dynamik die faszinierende Dramaturgie beider Werke wiederherstellt.

Le *Stabat Mater* de Pergolèse, que Bach lui-même admirait, et celui de Scarlatti, avec sa polyphonie superbement tissée, sont, par leur intense expressivité et leur manière évocatrice d'illustrer le texte, des œuvres véritablement théâtrales. Cette nouvelle interprétation souligne ces traits, en utilisant les tempi et les nuances d'origine pour restituer la dramaturgie saisissante des deux œuvres.

*An outstanding performance from start to finish.*

## Liner Notes by Rinaldo Alessandrini

### English Version

**"Stabat Mater dolorosa" = The Theatre in Church**

Nowadays most music is played in the churches, and never without instruments [...] Since composers are reluctant to labour over works that are not heard, they bend all their efforts to the writing of psalms and motets which are more lightly valued than anything else.

— Pietro della Valle, Lettera

It is a show for which no entrance fee is charged at the door – a celebration of passion in the context of a convert or parish church where the most celebrated cantanti are to be performed, arriving just in time for the motets. They sit facing the singers, turning their backs on the altar. They feast their eyes on the singers' faces and listen to the music that sounds they have.

— Le Corf de la Vieville, *Comparison de la musique italienne et de la musique française*

LE MARQUIS DE VILLAROSA, Pergolesi's first biographer writing in 1831, we learn that from the very outset, even before completing his *Stabat Mater*, the young Neapolitan composer Alessandro Scarlatti had the intention of replacing the version written by the famous Pergolesi with a new composition. The two *Stabat Mater* settings date from approximately 1708 and 1736. Ces œuvres étaient alors démodées dès l'office; et, bien des personnes devaient avoir assisté à l'événement. Mais il n'est pas impossible de savoir comment ces œuvres furent accueillies à l'époque. Pergolesi, à cette époque, était déjà célèbre. Il avait composé *La Serva Padrona* et d'autres œuvres qui avaient été représentées avec succès à Naples et ailleurs. Sa musique était appréciée pour sa grâce et sa simplicité.

The fame of Pergolesi's *Stabat Mater* spread rapidly throughout Europe. As early as 1739, Charles Burney described it as the greatest setting of its time: of a sacred Latin text and a new setting of Pergolesi's profound art of harmonic writing, the understanding of his contemporaries was that he behaved in mind. Even the great J.S. Bach (among others) paid homage to the piece by "parodying" the music for a German adaptation of Psalm 51, the result was the motet *Tilge, Höchster, meine Sünden*. And in 1810 Paisiello performed a new version of the work in Naples Cathedral, adding some wind parts and a chorus besides redistributing the solo parts between a quartet of boys' male and female voices.

Bach's reaction testifies to the fact that Pergolesi's *Stabat Mater* had indeed become a symbol of the perfection and universality of Italian music – "such is the fascination of Italian music that, when you listen to it you desire no greater happiness in this world," wrote Charles de Brosses, who in 1739 described Pergolesi's setting as "the finest piece of sacred music that I know". The work is without doubt Pergolesi's best-known work of music; it stands in the middle of the century. Such great esteem was not slow to arouse violent polemics, especially in France where the "Querelle des Bouffons" had begun to gather around the person of Pergolesi. In various publications that appeared soon after his death, the composer is depicted as having an angelic, ethereal face surrounded by flowing golden locks, while a cherub (with the face of the castrato Caffarelli) at Pergolesi's side holds to his face disfigured by a snub nose, thick and vulgarly protruding lips and his right leg lozenge than has left, in the act of directing the *P major Mass* in the church of San Lorenzo in Lucina.

Even today, the *Stabat Mater* continues to exercise its fascination and to keep alive a sense of reverence for a composer who, until a short time ago, was regarded as nothing more than a child prodigy and whose legacy reduced to even smaller proportions by the discovery that many works formerly attributed to Pergolesi were in fact not his at all.

It is a pity that Charles de Brosses never had the chance to hear the above-mentioned *Mass* in Rome after 1737, when Pergolesi had re-worked it three times before achieving the final, impressive version for four voices, two choirs and two orchestras. Even Pergolesi's *Stabat Mater*, written in Pozzuoli, in the cathedral of Naples, where the young composer died on 16 March 1736. He was 26. Those are the bare facts. What follows has however the status of an *exemplum fictum*.

But what is one to say about Scarlatti's hapless *Stabat Mater*, so soon replaced and forgotten? The inevitable comparison leads to some interesting conclusions even after a single reading of the score.

Scarlatti's predilection for extremely dense textures (a bone of dissonance much gnawed over in the corresponding essay by Francesco Cerone, and frequently encountered in this Stabat Mater), led the composer's reluctance to subscribe to the new melody-dominated style which was gaining ground at the beginning of the century. But his innovative and open-minded approach to harmony would have given De Brosses something to think about. In the second aria, for example, he makes no attempt to disguise his sources; his instrumental and vocal lines are of a piece.

## Latin Text with English Translation

**Stabat Mater dolorosa**
Stabat Mater dolorosa
juxta crucem lacrimosa,
dum pendebat Filius.

Cujus animam gementem,
contristatam et dolentem
pertransivit gladius.

O quam tristis et afflicta
fuit illa benedicta
Mater unigeniti!

Quae moerebat et dolebat,
pia Mater, dum videbat
nati poenas incliti.

Quis est homo qui non fleret,
matrem Christi si videret
in tanto supplicio?

Quis non posset contristari
Christi matrem contemplari
dolentem cum Filio?

Pro peccatis suae gentis
vidit Iesum in tormentis,
et flagellis subditum.

Vidit suum dulcem natum
moriendo desolatum,
dum emisit spiritum.

Eia Mater, fons amoris,
me sentire vim doloris
fac, ut tecum lugeam.

Fac ut ardeat cor meum
in amando Christum Deum,
ut illi complacere.

Sancta Mater, istud agas,
crucifixi fige plagas
cordi meo valide.

Tui nati vulnerati,
tam dignati pro me pati,
poenas mecum divide.

Fac me vere tecum flere,
crucifixo condolere,
donec ego vixero.

Juxta crucem tecum stare
et me tibi sociare
in planctu desidero.

Virgo virginum praeclara,
mihi iam non sis amara,
fac me tecum plangere.

Fac ut portem Christi mortem,
passionis fac consortem,
et plagas recolere.

Fac me plagis vulnerari,
cruce hac inebriari,
ob amorem Filii.

Inflammatus et accensus
per te, Virgo, sim defensus
in die iudicii.

Fac me cruce custodiri,
morte Christi praemuniri,
confoveri gratia.

Quando corpus morietur,
fac ut animae donetur
paradisi gloria.

Amen.

*(English translation follows the same structure as the Latin text)*
