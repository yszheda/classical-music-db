### Symposium Records

We believe that this series will come to be regarded by collectors, historians, and musicologists as the major means of access to a number of the most important of the earliest generation of recorded artists whose voices have survived only in recordings of surpassing rarity, and which are presented in this collection in the finest known specimens.

---

Here is another compilation consisting of twenty four early red G&T's each of which is a major rarity.

**EMMA CARELLI** who features in the first ten records was a leading figure in the verismo movement. She was born in Naples in 1877 and was brought up in a very musical atmosphere, for her father was a well known teacher of singing. She had her first lessons with him, before going at the early age of twelve to the Musical College of San Pietro a Majella.

She made her operatic debut in 1895 in Altamura and made rapid progress, singing in Bellini's *Gioletta e Romeo* and achieving great success partnering the young Caruso. In the same year she was given a contract at the Liceo in Milan, singing the role of the Infanta in Massenet's *Le Cid*. She was promoted the title role in the world premiere of Giordano's *Fedora* but as the influenza epidemic intervened and insisted that part be given to the virtually unknown Mary Tracy, who after a heated exchange with the management Carelli stormed out of the opera house and left for Vienna. When relations were less strained, she returned to the Liceo, and Giordano, who heard her, asked her to sing in *Fedora* in Mantua. She made a great impression and this was indeed the starting point of a great career, for shortly afterwards she was engaged at La Scala Milan, where she made her debut on the 26th December 1899 singing Desdemona in the Othello of Tamagno.

She toured South America in 1900 but returned to La Scala in October of that year. She soon established herself as an outstanding exponent of verismo opera, her roles title being probably Zaza. She was by now married to Walter Mocchi who had very leftist views, was something of a political agitator, and helped to organize a strike in Milan. As a result Carelli was quite unjustly shouldered by impresarios and fellow artists alike. She was deeply in love with her husband, although she did not share his political views and in despair she attempted suicide and her husband filled with remorse decided to give up his political activities. He eventually became the impresario of the Costanzi in Rome, and (finally) its owner. Carelli's career was now once again highly successful and she created the role of Elettra in Rome, but when, after repeated requests, she undertook the management of the Costanzi while her husband was in South America, she died of an infection. Carelli retired from singing. She was in charge at the Costanzi until 1926, when she retired. She remarked at that time, "I have three loves in my life, Walter Mocchi, the Costanzi Theatre, and this accursed motor car that one day or another will destroy me". How right she was! On the 17th August 1928 her car crashed near Montefiascone and she was killed instantly.

Carelli's G&T recordings are all of great interest. She had a voice of highly individual quality which she used with great skill. The duet with Sammarco from Mascheroni's *Lorenza*, suffers rather indifferent recording even for 1904, but there are light touches in the soprano's performance and she is of considerable historical importance since the composer is of the piano.

Santuzza's aria is sung with dramatic intensity allied to moments of quiet restraint which make it all the more effective. Mimi's brief aria from *La Boheme* is given with a much lighter quality of voice and is one of her best records. Even better is her *Morte di Fedora* where she uses her lower and medium registers with great sensitivity, although her Lovo, Ventura is the rather conventional type of lacrimose Italian tenor, adequate rather than distinguished.

The records as a group are models of technical perfection, although they lack the individuality which the great Marcella gradually acquired and she was able to show in later records.

One of the great experiences for most of us, is to hear a dramatic soprano voice soaring easily and riding majestically over the orchestral accompaniment in the vast spaces of a large opera house. These today, are one of the finest experiences it is possible to enjoy on a record player. The recording, and of course in the early days of the gramophone it was not possible to give more than the rudiments of an accompaniment, was in any case quite inadequate for so magnificent a large voice, like the one that Amelia Pinto almost certainly possessed always presented a dramatic and emotional interpretation of her roles.

Carelli was well known for her portrayal of Massenet's Manon and she sings *Addio o nostro picciol desco* with touching simplicity although the very free use of portamento is more Italian than French in style. The aria from *Iris* has delicious high notes in it, and she is again at her very best in *La mamma morta*.

*Dopo* was an obvious favourite of hers (she recorded it for G&T and Fonotipia) and she sings it with a suitable glad laden tone. Her *Vissi d'arte* is not quite as fine as the others it must be said, being well sung in a rather conventional way.

If there is an exception, Tosca is recorded slightly less fine than the others it must be said, being well sung in a rather conventional way. Carelli has very little to do but to achieve a tremendous dramatic effect, both in the soliloquy in Act II and finally in the Duetto.

**GIUSEPPE DE LUCA** was born on Christmas Day in 1876 in Rome and at the early age of sixteen he started his musical studies at the Santa Cecilia Academy in Rome, and reached La Scala Milan in 1900, and was a triumph. He had made his debut at the Liceo in Barcelona, creating the role of Flicker there in Franchetti's *Germania* opposite Caruso, in 1902. He appeared in the 1908 season at the Colon Buenos Aires, singing *Tristan und Isolde*, *Siegfried*, *Iris*, *La Gioconda* and *Don Giovanni*.

In 1915 he made his debut at the Metropolitan Opera New York on November 25th, as Figaro in *Il Barbiere di Siviglia*, and for the next quarter of a century he was to be one of its leading artists. He undertook appearance in 1935, at Covent Garden also as Figaro with Lily Pons, Dino Borgioli and Conchita Supervia and had a triumph. His voice had a naturally beautiful quality, even at the upper range, the voice full and warm throughout his range, and he acted the part brilliantly. He continued to appear regularly almost up to his retirement from the Met in 1946. He was still good enough to sing at the age of 73 in 1949. He died in New York on August 24th 1950.

De Luca was extremely versatile and appeared frequently in opera. He occasionally appeared in verismo opera, but his true greatness is shown in the singing of the great Verdi and Donizetti baritone roles.

It has been suggested that the great artist made some cylinder records before 1900, but to my knowledge none has been reported to exist. Suffice to say that by the time he came to make his first discs, his velvety baritone voice is already used with all the grace of a great artist. His recording of Michonet is a creator's record of great historical importance, the piano accompaniment being by the composer Cilea. The arias from *Le Roi de Lahore* and *La Favorita* are models of their kind, as indeed are those from *Amleto* and *Il Ballo in Maschera* he sang. Serenata from *Don Giovanni* is rather rushed and includes a high F sharp which he interpolates unnecessarily and somewhat unmusically. There is little about this great artist, he returned to his best form in the duet with Ceresoli from *La Favorita* and his aria from *Don Pasquale* is a gem.

**AMELIA PINTO** was born in Palermo in 1878 and studied singing at the Santa Cecilia Academy in Rome. She made her operatic debut in 1899 in La Gioconda at Brescia, and reached La Scala Milan in 1900, and was a triumph. She had made her debut at the Liceo in Barcelona, creating the role of Flicker there in Franchetti's *Germania* opposite Caruso, in 1902. She appeared in the 1908 season at the Colon Buenos Aires, singing *Tristan und Isolde*, *Siegfried*, *Iris*, *La Gioconda* and *Don Giovanni*.

At the beginning of the season she married a Sicilian doctor and retired from the stage, but after much persuasion from the directors of La Scala she returned in 1914 to sing Isolde under the direction of Toscanini. In 1915 she went to Warsaw for a season but then went into hiding under the German occupation, displaying a voice of the quality used with great skill and dramatic intensity. The creator's record from Germania, which we include here is quite different from that of the singer whose name is on the label, and for some reason most of the very high notes shown in my copy of the score are omitted. It is sung in the manner typical of the verismo school and shows the singer's voice to be extremely dramatic and very much in the verismo tradition, bringing vividly to life the despair of the German students as they await departure to face Siberia, with low notes of extraordinary richness middle register at this record and *Suicidio* are my favourites among the soprano's G&T's. The two *Vissi d'arte* are interesting to compare, the first is from the period of her first recollection. As I have mentioned, it was not easy to record the large dramatic voices in 1902 but these recordings give more than a hint of the excitement which Pinto could obviously create in the theatre.

**Dr. Wayne writes:**

Carelli's G&T's are numbered amongst the world's major rarities, virtually all having been deleted within a few months, by January 1905. 054028, which features Sammarco for the most part, although what there is of Carelli is absolutely thrilling, does turn up occasionally in Italy and South America; the disc sold for Sammarco who was even more internationally famous.

My source for 53341, 053034, 054028 and 053034 was South America; in the early sixties the latter item was thought to be, and possibly still is, unique. Another copy of the "Lorenza" item did not surface until the early 1990s in the collection of an Italian dealer, Sig. Monteverde of via Garibaldi, Milano. A famous English collector ceded by way of exchange, the "Dopo" 053035, the only copy known of export from Yale library.

Roberto Bauer had 053029, 053029 and 053032 and the duet 054028. Roberto had his first collection confiscated by the invading Germans when Italy was occupied at the beginning of the Second World War. Bauer was a known anti-Fascist who helped many people escape from the Nazis. The soldiers were ordered to break all his records and throw them in the street. The only one miraculously survived was Carelli's "Manon" which was found in the gutter by his friend Otto Muller. This rather battered copy he eventually gave to me and is transferred onto this compact disc for posterity.

Colin Shreve had two examples of the ultra-rare "Iris" and when he disposed of his personal collection 30 years ago both came into my possession via Dr. Dick Alexander. Both were superb copies.

I found a lovely specimen of "Boheme-Addio" in a back street in Palermo in 1964.

De Luca's Cano mio ben I have never seen but it does exist. Only two years ago an American friend sent me a tape of it, it has a master plate crack defect similar to the Fernera-Aponzolo "Faust" 54015 and the Bruno Pomio Fernando from Favorita which I found in the Dick Alexander collection in 1960.

The next rarity is the "Adriana Lecouvreur" 52420 of which Bauer had a near mint copy. Arthur Renfros found far less 52425 and 52426, I have never seen the latter again, but they were pressed by the Victor Company in 1903 for a very short time and then turned in this format. The Ceresoli-De Luca duet is almost the rarest together with 52444 "Don Pasquale" Eri tu is the only one I have seen several times, in Italy. His "Siberia" creator duet are quite another story for another time.

Amelia Pinto: there were actually 3 editions of "Tosca", one being 52232X. Only one matrix, 1782B, was found with any regularity when I first went to Italy in the late 50s. Apart from 53234, which is inordinately rare, the most difficult are "Ero e Leandro" and "Germania", which are favourites of mine of this vintage. Incidentally her Fonotipia's are at least as rare as her G&T's, probably rarer, a number of her Fono. titles I hope will released by Symposium in the near future.

John Freestone
