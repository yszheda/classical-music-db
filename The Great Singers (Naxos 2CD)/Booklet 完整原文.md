## 内页说明

### NAXOS Historical

From the earliest days of opera, that combination of all the arts, singers, the prima donna and the primo uomo, heroes and hero, have had extraordinary power over us, dramatic, social and commercial. The present anthology includes examples of the art and charisma of many of the greatest singers of the earlier ages of recording.

Among the legendary prima donnas is Dame NELLIE MELBA (1861-1931), the Australian diva who claimed to have put her native country on the map. There are many stories about her regal behaviour and apparent lack of privileges of those she disapproved. She arrived at the Metropolitan Opera in New York and at La Scala, Milan, towered in Australia and did much to promote music there. In spite of her reported advice to Clara Butt to "sing no more, it's all they can understand", content that she inoffensively devoted having given. She made her farewell appearance at Covent Garden in 1924 and spent her final years at home in Australia. She recorded from 1904, and the aria here included is Sweet bird that shunn'st the noise of folly, from Handel's setting of Milton's Il penseroso. This was recorded in 1904 at Melba's London home in Great Cumberland Place, a retake, after an earlier recorded lapse in concentration.

Dame MAGGIE TEYTE (1888-1976) left Mary Garden in Debussy's Pelléas et Mélisande. She was a pupil in Paris of Jean de Reszke, the tenor who had the unfortunate task in 1896 of singing Siegfried in the inauguration Bayreuth of Debussy. After earlier operatic success that also brought appearances in London and America, she turned her attention to operetta and studied seriously in the later 1930s to re-establish herself in French recital repertoire, often performing songs by composers whom she had known in France, Debussy, Ravel, Reynaldo Hahn and others. She recorded Debussy's Chansons de Bilitis in 1934 and with Alfred Cortot.

One of Maggie Teyte's later appearances in London was with the Norwegian soprano KIRSTEN FLAGSTAD (1895-1962) in Purcell's Dido and Aeneas at Morley Hall, the newly established Mermaid Theatre. Flagstad's earlier career had been in Oslo, where she sang a variety of roles, and in the 1930s began to appear in Wagner. She sang Isolde in Oslo in 1932 and followed this with the same role in London in 1934, and in the next ten years at Covent Garden, she appeared at the Met as Sieglinde, followed by Brünnhilde, and Kundry, establishing her position as one of the great Wagnerian sopranos. She spent the war years at home in German-occupied Norway, where her husband was later accused of collaboration, and her reputation in the states, while welcomed in London, was badly opposed in America, though she was eventually able to dispel the smirch of her former position. Based here in England, she was partnered by the Danish singer Lauritz Melchior. He studied at the Wagnerian tenors of the day, and was heard in his Naxos recording of Wagner's Tristan und Isolde. Flagstad recorded with Melchior Kundry's oh das Kind an seiner Mutter Brust, from the third act of Parsifal, in 1936.

Flagstad's predecessor in Wagner at the Met had been FRIDA LEIDER (1888-1973). Born in Berlin, her ambition was aroused by hearing Geraldine Farrar and Frida Hempel at the Imperial Opera. She began her career singing Wagner's in Tannhäuser at Halle in 1915, following this with Brunnhilde in the Walküre in Nuremberg. In 1929 she appeared in Hamburg, undertaking a variety of roles in addition to Wagnerian. In 1923 she made her first appearance at the Berlin State Opera, and had her own house near the castle, where her career until 1940. She made her debut at the Met in 1933 as Isolde, a role in which she excelled, but went on to Germany to her return home, after her first season there, leading to the theatre's revocation of Pfanzmuller, with which husband, the violinist Georg Schumann, leader of the Berlin State Orchestra, in 1945. She made her final recital in Switzerland. She gave her last concert in 1946. Her Abscheulicher, wo eilst du hin? from Fidelio was recorded in 1925.

Between 1929 and 1933 ERNA BERGER (1900-1990) sang at Bayreuth, appearing as the Shepherd Boy in Tannhäuser, the Forest Bird in Siegfried and Woglinde in Rheingold. She had made her CND debut at the Dresden State Opera at first two in The Magic Flute, and she continued with a number of characteristic Mozart roles to which her voice was well suited, first appearing at Salzburg as Blonde and ending her last season there in 1941 as Zerlina. Guest concerts brought her to Italy and in 1939 she made her debut at the Met as Sophie. Particularly famous were her Queen of the Night, and, as here, her Konstanze.

ELISABETH SCHUMANN (1888-1952) made her debut in 1909 at the Hamburg Stadttheater as the Shepherd Boy in Tannhäuser, remaining with the company until 1919, when she moved to Richard Strauss's persuasion, in Vienna. In Vienna she sang until 1927 and then in 1938, when she settled in New York, like Erna Berger her voice was particularly suited to roles by Mozart. Zerlina and Susanna in Mozart, and she made a notable Sophie in Der Rosenkavalier, a role which she sang in London in 1931. She also won fame as a Lieder singer, and is represented here by Schubert's The Shepherd on the Rock, which she recorded in 1927 with the English clarinettist Reginald Kell.

Born in Viborg, JUSSI BJÖRLING (1909-1960) left Russia at the Revolution, studied in Paris and made her American debut in 1930, singing later at the Opera-Comique in Paris, for short periods at the Met 1935 and in the 1940s, and, notably, in 1951 at La Fenice, where she created the role of Eurydice of Gluck. She recorded Mussorgsky's Sérénade with Leonard Bernstein in 1945.

The American-born soprano ROSA PONSELLE (1897-1981), the daughter of Italian immigrant parents, made a remarkable operatic debut at the Met in 1918, where she partnered Caruso in the first Met performance of Verdi's La forza del destino. She continued her career at the same house, undertaking 29 different roles, until 1937, when she decided to retire. She made her first recordings in 1918 and recorded Mira d'acerbe lagrime from Il trovatore two years later.

PIA TASSINARI (1903-1995) began her career as a soprano, later changing to mezzo-soprano roles. She appeared at La Scala and other Italian houses in the 1930s and after the war also performed in America. At the Met in 1947 she sang Tiresia, with her husband Ferruccio Tagliavini as Caravaggio. Her later, mezzo roles included Carmen, Charlotte in Massenet's Werther and Ulrica in Un ballo in maschera. She is heard here in the Grand Aria from the important act of Orfice's opera Chopin, recorded in 1949 and with her husband in a duet from Massenet's Werther. FERRUCCIO TAGLIAVINI (1913-1995) made his operatic debut in Florence in 1938. In 1945 he joined the New York City Opera, returning there in 1956 and in 1958. At the Scala and San Francisco he returned for a season in 1961. He sang at La Scala and won praise in London for his Nemorino in L'Elisir d'amore. He is heard here also in M'appari from Flotow's opera Martha, recorded in 1949.

It was in the operas of Richard Strauss that the German soprano LOTTE LEHMANN (1888-1976) won her early fame, starting when she sang the Composer in Ariadne in 1916 in Vienna. Her career in Vienna continued until 1938, when she moved to America, having already established her reputation from her further appearances at the Met. She excelled also as the Marschallin in Der Rosenkavalier and continued to give recitals until 1951. She recorded Manetta's Lied: Selig, Glück das mir verblieb, from Korngold's Die tote Stadt in the late 1930s, in 1947 with Richard Tauber.

The English contralto KATHLEEN FERRIER (1912-1953) had a relatively short career, appearing principally in oratorio and in recital. She made her operatic debut in 1946 in Britten's The Rape of Lucretia at Glyndebourne, where she sang Gluck's Orfeo the following year. Her last two appearances were in this role at Covent Garden in 1953, set short by her illness. Under Bruno Walter she had sung Mahler's Das Lied von der Erde at first Edinburgh Festival in 1947 and they recorded the work in 1954 in Carnegie Hall. The movement Von der Schönheit is included here.

The Swedish tenor SET SVANHOLM (1904-1964) made his operatic debut at the Royal Swedish Opera in 1929 in baritone roles, but by 1936 had changed to undertaking heavier tenor parts in Wagner and Verdi. After the war he was the First Dyer's Son in London and Stockholm at the Met, and appeared regularly at Covent Garden. He served as director of the Swedish Royal Opera from 1956 to 1958. He is heard here in the Prize Song from Wagner's Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg, recorded in 1947.

One of the most famous operatic duets is O soave fanciulla, from Puccini's La Bohème. Here it is sung in a recording by the great Italian soprano, LICIA ALBANESE (b.1909) and the tenor GIUSEPPE DI STEFANO (b.1921). He had made his solo operatic debut, first sang at La Scala in 1946 and starred his association with the Met the following year, continuing to appear there until 1960. He first appeared in Britain in Edinburgh in 1957 as Nemorino, part of a short season that, however, briefly, included an appearance by Maria Callas in La Sonnambula.

The name of ENRICO CARUSO (1873-1921) won legendary fame as perhaps the greatest tenor of the twentieth century, whatever the earlier technical deficiencies in his training, gradually overcome as his career advanced. He made his debut in Naples in 1894, but it was in 1901 that he won his first great success at La Scala in L'Elisir d'amore, as opera in which he also appeared in the same year in his native Naples, in an indifferent reception that dissuaded him from further appearances there. In 1902 he made his debut at Covent Garden, going on to appear in leading opera houses throughout Europe. 1903 brought his debut at the Met, where he returned in the following years, appearing there for the last time in 1920 in Italy's opera La Juive. He is heard here in a selection of songs and arias of the day, including Una furtiva lagrima, recorded at Milan in 1902. The commercial success of the first records opened the way for a series of other singers, eventually including Melba, with whom Caruso had made his first appearance at Covent Garden.

RICHARD TAUBER (1891-1948) was later associated with lighter lyric tenor repertoire and Viennese operetta, and made a popular reputation for himself both in his native Austria and in England, his adopted country. He had made his operatic debut at Chemnitz in 1913 as Tamino in Die Zauberflöte, a role he sang at Covent Garden in 1938, with those of Belmonte in Die Entführung and in 1939 in the title role of Mozart's Idomeneo. He was heard at Glyndebourne as Don Ottavio in 1947, the year before his death. He first sang Wilhelm Meister in Mignon in Dresden in 1913 and recorded the German version of Adieu Mignon (Leb' wohl Mignon) in 1923.

The Russian bass FEODOR CHALIAPIN (1873-1938) appeared first with the Imperial Opera in St Petersburg, then with the privately owned Mamontov Opera and, from 1909 until 1914, at the Bolshoy. He started his international career in 1901, when he sang Mephistopheles in Boito's opera at La Scala, a role repeated at the Met, where he made his debut in 1907. He appeared in the Paris seasons staged by Diaghilev before 1914, and returned briefly to the Mariinsky in St Petersburg, before leaving Russia in 1921. His repertoire ranged from the Russian operas in which he had first made his name to standard Italian and French works. It was for Chaliapin that Massenet wrote the title role of his 1910 opera Don Quichotte. Part of the final scene between Don Quixote and Sancho Panza, Oh, mon maître... Oui! Je fus le chef, was recorded in 1927.

BENIAMINO GIGLI (1890-1957) made his operatic debut in 1914 in Ponchielli's La Gioconda, a work that had brought Caruso his first success in Palermo in 1907. He went on to establish a reputation in various houses in Italy and then in Spain before his first triumph at La Scala in 1915 in Boito's Mefistofele, the work in which he first appeared at the Met in 1939. He continued in New York at the Metropolitan for forty-two consecutive seasons, making his last many roles. He discontinued his association with the Met in 1952, declaring a reduced salary offer and then devoted himself in Italy, other European countries, and South America. Well known for the role in which, whether in the lugubrious of Verdi's Requiem or in poignant operatic roles. He is heard here in Canio's Un tal gioco, credetemi from Pagliacci, recorded in the opera in 1934.

In a professional career of some thirty years the Swedish tenor JUSSI BJÖRLING (1911-1960) first appeared as a boy in the family vocal quartet established and toured by his father. He joined the Royal Swedish Opera in 1930, appearing first in that year in Don Ottavio. Towards the end of the decade he was heard in London, San Francisco, Chicago and at the Met in New York. His earliest recordings are of Swedish, Italian and American songs, Verdi and Puccini, that was at the heart of his achievement. He is heard here in Nessun dorma from Puccini's last opera, Turandot, recorded in 1944.

With a reputation as one of the greatest singing actors of his time, the Russian bass FEODOR CHALIAPIN (1873-1938) appeared first with the Imperial Opera in St Petersburg, then with the privately owned Mamontov Opera and, from 1909 until 1914, at the Bolshoy. He started his international career in 1901, when he sang Mephistopheles in Boito's opera at La Scala, a role repeated at the Met, where he made his debut in 1907. He appeared in the Paris seasons staged by Diaghilev before 1914, and returned briefly to the Mariinsky in St Petersburg, before leaving Russia in 1921. His repertoire ranged from the Russian operas in which he had first made his name to standard Italian and French works. It was for Chaliapin that Massenet wrote the title role of his 1910 opera Don Quichotte. Part of the final scene between Don Quixote and Sancho Panza, Oh, mon maître... Oui! Je fus le chef, was recorded in 1927.

The Italian bass EZIO PINZA (1892-1957), made his debut in 1914 at Cremona in Bellini's Norma. He appeared in the 1930s at La Scala under Toscanini and made his debut at the Met in 1926, when he sang the Pacifico, Maximin in Spontini's La vestale. He continued at the Met for some 22 seasons in many different roles. His later career, after 1948, was largely in operetta and musical comedy. He sang with Maria Caniglia, Die Stigiani and Beniamino Gigli in the historical 1939 recording of Verdi's Requiem, from which the Confutatis maledictis of the Dies Irae is included.
