### 第 4-5 页 - HIPPO_20250106_0002.jpg & HIPPO_20250106_0003.jpg ( liner notes 1)

**Frederic Rzewski**
Four Pieces (1977)

Frederic Rzewski wrote *Four Pieces* (1977) just two years after his monumental variation cycle, *The People United Will Never Be Defeated!* (1975). Both works were written for pianist Ursula Opans, and in 2005, Rzewski described the *Four Pieces* as "a kind of sequel [to *The People United!*]". Despite their seemingly abstract nature, the pieces are not cut free from extra-musical, indeed political, underpinnings. In fact, Rzewski argued that the music was "firmly attached to the west coast of South America, a meditation on Chile four years after the coup d'état."

The first piece opens with a gentle "dramalink" theme in thirds and fifths. According to Rzewski, this hopeful theme is "supposedly reminiscent of traditional music of the Andes." Leighton Kerner, a music critic for the Village Voice, referred to the theme as a "phantasm and pure fantasy David Burge described it as 'a folk tune wanting to be born.'" There is certainly something powerful and mysterious about the melody, which makes an appearance in all four pieces, sometimes fragmented, sometimes in full, but not always in transformation. In the first piece, for example, the second iteration of the theme is already "no longer so innocent; with its tremulous, menacing accompaniment," writes Burge. Rzewski instructs the pianist to play "with a heavy feeling," causing the theme to temporarily lose hopefulness.

The second piece, marked "light and bouncing," is playful in character and may remind one of a scherzo. Composer Christian Wolff describes this movement as having "a distinct kinship with jazz in the crisp, shifting rhythmic movement of its opening, its harmonic patterning, and its overall rhythmic structure." The third piece is freer rhythmically and generally more reflective. Wolff suggests that the transformation of the melody recalls the process of grieving. He explains how the melody, "at first tentative and distant" gradually "moves towards something like infectious, public song." Wolff also notes that the third piece concludes, after a series of repeated chords, with the word final sustained chord, a "suspended cadence," which longs for the beginning of the final piece.

Listeners have often likened the fourth piece to a toccata. The piece opens with a massive ostinato crescendo, beginning at the top of the keyboard and moving all the way down the instrument. Rzewski gives the pianist detailed performance instructions, explaining that "the notes should be 'stroked' rather than struck, as if one were exciting a large gong: the object being to make the entire instrument speak throughout its spectrum." Approximately two-thirds of the way through the piece, the "phantasm theme" breaks out in a fiery hocket, and the listener may very well expect a brilliant finale of pianistic fireworks to follow. Instead, Rzewski concludes the work with a crescendo, beginning in the depths of the keyboard and ending in the highest octave on a single repeated note. Rzewski's performance instructions here are clear: "The final notes should not have the character of a cadence but rather the contrary, of something unfinished." Looking back in 2005, he explained that this inconclusive ending suggested that "the story is not yet over."

### 第 6-7 页 - HIPPO_20250106_0004.jpg & HIPPO_20250106_0005.jpg (liner notes 2)

**William Albright**
Five Chromatic Dances (1976)

The *Chromatic Dances* are often regarded as William Albright's masterpiece for piano. Composer Evan Chambers, first a student and later a colleague of Albright at the University of Michigan, went so far as to characterize the *Chromatic Dances* as "one of the great solo piano works of the century." Commissioned by pianist Thomas Warburton and completed in 1976, Rzewski's *Chromatic Dances* are cyclically diverse, yet unified one after the other in seemingly logical order.

The opening dance, "Procession and Rounds," begins in the low registers of the instrument. Albright's instructions to the pianist read: "as even as possible, colorless." Thus, the stately "Procession" initially advances somewhat monotonously, but gradually gathers momentum and melodic direction. The second half of the piece, with its shimmering, unpredictable figurations in the upper registers of the keyboard, is perhaps best described by the composer himself: "delirious, obscurantist, and crazy." "Drunken." Several commentators have viewed the second piece, "Masquerade," as reminiscent of Claude Debussy. "Masquerade" features an element of humor and self-interruption, perhaps calling to mind such pieces as Debussy's "Golliwog's Cakewalk" from *Children's Corner* or *Le dodécaphonisme* from Préludes, Book II. The third dance, "Fantasy-Mazurka," makes a more conspicuous reference to an older composer, opening in the dreamy sound-world of a Chopin nocturne. Albright works to convey an aura of mystery throughout, giving the piece instructions such as "lunatical, inextricable and exotic" in two perspectives." The piece concludes with a breathtaking four-quadruple octave of Chopin's Mazurka, Op. 17, no. 4, evoking a nostalgic similar to that engendered by George Crumb's quotation of Chopin in his 1972 *Dream Images* (from *Makrokosmos*, Vol. 1). The next dance, "Hoedown," provides a decided contrast: "Joyous and ecstatic"—the dance can hardly be contained. Somewhat sarcastically, Albright places a seductive Habanera at approximately the midpoint of the movement. He then returns to what pianist Robert Weirich terms the raucous "country fiddling" of the opening, yet the piece ends with a surprise as well. After a flurry

of stacked fifths and "agitated" figuration, one expects the pianist to dance off the keyboard. Instead, almost apologetically, the pianist seems to back out of the room with a quiet, descending chromatic cadence.

The *Chromatic Dances* conclude with "The Farewell." Here, Albright comes full circle, focusing, as in the opening movement, on the physical limits of the keyboard. Whereas the "Procession" took place primarily in the piano's lower register and the "Rounds" in the upper register, "The Farewell" deals with both simultaneously. As pianist David Burge notes, the final movement begins with an incredibly quiet, simple tone played seven octaves apart at the extremities of the keyboard." In Burge's words: "Eventually, a tremolo reverberation is established in the center of the instrument and builds to a giant wave that enters piano whispering, before the movement dies away, ushering back the return of this simple tone." Burge regards this movement as "extraordinarily poignant." "To be sure, 'The Farewell' has the perfect last word, its reflective character tying together the chromatic diversity of the preceding movements."

—Monika Herzhberger

**Piano Robert Satterlee** has toured throughout the United States and Europe, in addition to making appearances in China and Thailand. He has been heard on radio broadcasts throughout the United States, most notably on Minnesota Public Radio and WFMT in Chicago.

Music of our time plays an important role in Satterlee's performing activity, and he has given premieres of works by Frederic Rzewski, William Bolcom and Gabriela Lena Frank, among others. His identification with the music of Frederic Rzewski dates back to his student days, when first met the composer in a class at Yale University. Currently a resident of Ann Arbor, Michigan, Satterlee is Professor of Piano at the University of Michigan. Satterlee started a commissioning project centered around William Albright, a long time member of the faculty at the University of Michigan. Satterlee commissioned Albright's former students and colleagues to write solo piano works in his memory, which he performed in concert with Albright's *Five Chromatic Dances*.

Satterlee's solo debut in Carnegie Hall was met with enthusiasm. In addition to chamber music, he has performed as soloist with the Chicago, London, Philadelphia and Detroit Symphony Orchestras in chamber music performances, and he was co-artistic director of Chamber Music Quad Cities, a music festival based in Iowa. Satterlee is on the faculty of Bowling Green State University in Ohio and teaches at the Interlochen Arts Camp during the summer.

**Composer and pianist Frederic Rzewski** was born in Westfield, Massachusetts in 1938. He studied with Randall Thompson and Walter Piston at Harvard, earning a BA in 1958, and with Roger Sessions and Milton Babbitt at Princeton, completing his MFA in 1960. Rzewski has spent a year in Florence on a Fulbright scholarship, studying with Luigi Dallapiccola. He remained in Europe and built his reputation as a formidable virtuoso pianist specializing in the music of the avant-garde. Rzewski co-founded the live electronic ensemble Musica Elettronica Viva (MEV) with Alvin Curran and Richard Teitelbaum in Rome in 1966, subsequently drifting away from the piano for a number of years.
