### Michael Ponti biography

Michael Ponti's extensive recording activity and world-wide concert appearances have established him as one of the important pianists of his time. Since 1968, an output of more than 69 records, including the complete works of Tchaikovsky, Scriabin, and Rachmaninoff have received world-wide critical acclaim. *High Fidelity* magazine was prompted to refer to him as **"Ten pianists in one."**

Hundreds of concerts and return engagements have made his name and art familiar to audiences in most of the great cities of the world: London, Buenos Aires, Capetown, Tokyo, Prague, Vienna, Berlin, Rome, Athens, New York, Sydney.

Ponti, born October 29, 1937, studied piano from 1954-1955 in Washington, D.C. with Professor Gilmor McDonald, who was a pupil of Leopold Godowsky and Vernon Spencer. From 1955-1961 he continued his studies with Professor Erich Flinch in Frankfort, Germany, who had been a pupil of and assistant to the legendary Emil Sauer at the Vienna Music Academy and had concertized extensively throughout Europe.

In 1960s, Ponti won prizes in most of the important international piano competitions, including the coveted first prize Busoni award in Italy in 1964. Soon after this he made a memorable Vienna debut with five performances of Bartók's 2nd Concerto (Wolfgang Sawallisch conducting) to a raving press and public. His busy schedule has not faltered since, performing as he does internationally as a recitalist, soloist with orchestra, and as a member of the Piano Trio Ponti-Zimansky-Polasek.

## Program Notes

### Paraphrases, Reminiscences, & Fantasies of Liszt, Tausig, Moszkowski, and Grainger
*by Richard Freed*

Fantasies on themes from popular operas constituted perhaps the most successful single category in the arsenal of the virtuoso-composer in the heydey of nineteenth-century musical showmanship. Liszt, Thalberg, Gottschalk, and others who are forgotten now wrote dozens of them for the piano. Paganini wrote them for the violin, and there were more than a few such works even for the double bass. In some cases a theme or themes from an operatic work would be subjected to a series of variations, in others nothing more was attempted than reconstituting the vocal line and orchestral accompaniment in pianistic terms, but for the most part what was offered (and especially by Liszt) was neither a conventional variation work nor a simple transcription, but rather an adaptation of the basic thematic material in a thoroughly pianistic context, replete with florid embellishments and elaborate filigree work. At its best, such a showpiece might be more than a mere virtuoso vehicle and evoke the essence of the drama—or at least the mood of a particular scene—in the nature of a tone poem for the keyboard.

Liszt alone composed more than seventy piano pieces based on operatic material, variously titled "fantasy," "reminiscences," "paraphrase," or *divertissement*. Nine of these be derived from works of Donizetti, Bellini, and Meyerbeer, but only one from Mozart and the so-called "Don Juan Fantasy," composed in 1841, is both the most ambitious and the most successful in the entire vast series—no mere potpourri, but a synthesis of the very character of *Don Giovanni*. The work begins at the end of the opera, with the Don being dragged down to the Underworld by his Stone Guest; then we have the duettino *Là ci darem la mano* (with two stunning variations), a reprise of the opening material, and finally a spectacular elaboration on Don Giovanni's first-act champagne aria, *Finch' han dal vino*.

The *Rigoletto* Paraphrase, the fifth of Liszt's nine Verdi pieces, was composed in 1859. It is much shorter than the *Don Juan* Fantasy, and less broadly focused; it is based entirely on the famous Act IV quartet, *Bella figlia dell' amore*, and makes no attempt at either dramatic summation or character study, but does simulate various orchestral effects.

Tchaikovsky, like his idol Mozart, was also honored only once: Liszt made his glittering transcription of the Polonaise from the third act of *Eugene Onegin* in 1880 (ten years after the Hamburg premiere of the opera) and dedicated it to Karl Klindworth, the revered pedagogue, pianist, and conductor who had studied under him nearly thirty years earlier (and to whom Tchaikovsky dedicated his own Piano Sonata, Op. 37, in 1878).

The musical life of Karl Tausig illustrates a classic example of the development of a child prodigy. Born in Warsaw on November 4, 1841, his father was an accomplished pianist and teacher who had been a pupil of Sigismund Thalberg. Early study in the Thalberg tradition (keep perfect composure regardless of emotional or technical demands!) with his father was followed when he was thirteen by the usual pilgrimage to Weimar, where Franz Liszt had established his musical court.

Liszt heard Karl Tausig at 6 P.M. on July 21, 1855. He recounts the experience in a letter to Marie zu Sayn-Wittgenstein: "A little piano-playing prodigy, age 13, from Warsaw, and named Tausig came to see me.—He'll probably spend a year or two at Weimar. He's an amazingly gifted boy whom you will enjoy hearing. He plays everything by heart, composes (fairly well) and seems to me destined to make a brilliant reputation for himself very quickly."

Tausig was well known as a performer by 1862 but for the following several years he devoted himself to study, he composed, he married (in 1864, to the pianist Sophie von Vrabely), he promoted the work of Liszt in Vienna, and he met and formed a friendship with Brahms. He performed on the same program with Brahms at the latter's debut in Vienna.

At the age of thirty, on July 17, 1871, Tausig's life was tragically cut short by typhoid fever. Liszt wrote: "He had the intensity, the intelligence, the unique personality, the temperament, the talent, and the patient work habits of a great artist. Moreover, he was a true scholar, with superb practical skills, and quite properly occupied a dominant position in the musical world."

Today Tausig is best remembered for his transcriptions and his book of piano exercises. The exercises are still considered standard and his transcriptions of the Bach *Toccata and Fugue in D Minor* and those of several Scarlatti Sonatas remain on the shelves of music shops. Of his original compositions, hardly any are performed nowadays. If, perhaps, his debt to Liszt is too apparent in these works, it is time for an old debt to be forgotten. The music in this welcome album is the product of an important musical talent, a thoroughly cultured mind, and a highly disciplined musician.

All of the music was written during the 1860s. *The Fantasy on Themes of Moniuszko's Halka* (Reminiscences de Halka, Fantasie de Concert) was perhaps Tausig's most famous original work. It was in vogue for many years and expressed the great surge of mid-19th century romanticism through the pen and piano of one of that period's most famous musical figures.

Moritz Moszkowski, born in 1854 (in Breslau) lived into the present century, dying in Paris in 1925. Like Thalberg and Pabst, he knew Liszt, who regarded him highly and on at least one occasion performed a duo recital with him. Moszkowski was by all accounts an extremely likable man, remembered as a distinguished pedagogue, for his help in getting Paderewski's music published, for coaching the young Thomas Beecham in conducting and orchestration, and for his brilliant wit. As a composer, curiously enough, Moszkowski became best-known for his music in the Spanish vein—the ingratiating sets of *Spanish Dances*, Op. 37, composed in 1876, and the ambitious opera *Boabdil, der letzte Maurenkönig* (1892). Although the Hispanic element in his music is only skin-deep, it happened that after he settled in Paris in 1897 he taught piano to a number of important Spanish musicians, among them the composer Joaquín Turina. Moszkowski's most successful "non-Spanish" works are his *Piano Concerto in E major* and the dazzling solo fantasy *Etincelles*. Like nearly everyone else connected with the piano in the 1870s and 1880s, Moszkowski wrote his share of these pieces; his inventive setting of Offenbach's beloved Barcarolle was published as Op. 27.

The *Venusberg Bacchanale*, a concert paraphrase from music by Wagner, was not published until the outbreak of World War I.

The *Danse Bohème*, after music from Bizet's *Carmen*, was published during the first decade of the twentieth century.

Percy Grainger, whose charming British Folk Music Settings—*Country Gardens*, *Shepherd's Hey*, *Mock Morris*, *Molly on the Shore*, *Irish Tune from County Derry* (the so-called "Londonderry Air")—are among the most widely beloved such pieces in all music, was, like Liszt, a formidable keyboard virtuoso and a source of stimulation and encouragement to younger colleagues till the end of his long life. Like Liszt, too, Grainger used the term "paraphrase" to title some of his brilliant piano transcriptions of orchestral and vocal works by other composers. One of the most ingratiating to the listener—and most demanding on the performer—is the "paraphrase" on the "Waltz of the Flowers" from Tchaikovsky's ballet *The Nutcracker* (the concluding section of the concert suite Tchaikovsky himself extracted from that score). Neither this nor the Liszt setting of the *Onegin* Polonaise had been recorded before this.

### Wagner Paraphrases of Brassin, Moszkowski, and Tausig
*by Gary Lemco*

#### Wagner-Liszt: *Lohengrin: Festspiel und Brautlied* (G. 446)

Whatever Liszt's reservations about Wagner as a son-in-law, he admired the music unconditionally, and still he was able to infuse in his piano transcriptions some sense of his own style and lyrical fluency. The opening *Festspiel* annunciatory of the wedding of Lohengrin and Elsa, is musically dazzling: arpeggios, scales, and clarion trumpet calls. The music proceeds directly to the bridal chorus. It is fanfare explosion of color, with vigorous main theme against a shimmering background. Liszt adds an 8-bar introduction to Wagner's original.

#### Wagner-Brassin: **Feuerzauber** from *Die Walküre*

Louis Brassin (1840-1884) was the son of a man engaged by the Leipzig Opera, an arrangement most influential on young Louis. He became a pupil of Ignar Moscheles, then was appointed to the Stern Conservatory, Berlin. He traveled to Brussels (1869-78) and then to St. Petersburg (1878) where he died. His virtuosic transcription of the closing music of Wagner's second *Ring* opera has long been a favorite, Josef Hofmann having made a Brunswick inscription of its blazing 13 pages in 1922. The music picks up after Wotan's Farewell, where the Magic Fire surrounds Brünnhilde, the Valhalla motif resounding as the flames dance in ever-increasing color. The piano's ability to capture the orchestral tissue, particularly the harps' cascades, is beguiling.

### Wagner-Moszkowski: "Isolde's Liebestod" from *Tristan und Isolde*

The German pianist of Polish descent Moritz Moszkowski (1854-1925) studied in Dresden then in Berlin at the Stern Conservatory and Kullak Conservatory. He taught in Berlin in between various successful tours in England. He retired in Paris in 1897. A composer of numerous salon pieces, his most famous compositions are two sets of *Spanish Dances*, a dozen *Virtuoso Etudes*, and selected short, brilliant pieces often programmed by the late Vladimir Horowitz. Albert Lockwood has said of Moszkowski's music that it requires a pianist with delicacy and accuracy, without both of which qualities the pieces make little appeal. The challenge of scoring the *Liebestod* for piano, as Liszt and Moszkowski knew, resides in its having no percussive qualities as such, being rather laden with surging crescendos and sustained chords. Therefore *fortissimi* must seem like an overwhelming flood of sound and not hammer-blows. Moszkowski's 19-page transcription differs from Liszt's in that it retains many of the vocal lines elided from the other master, while still utilizing arpeggios, tremolos, and multiple-textured sonorities fully consonant with the exquisite, passionate outpouring of the original.

### Wagner-Tausig: "Ride of the Valkyries" from *Die Walküre*

Carl Tausig (1841-1871) was a pupil of both Liszt and Thalberg. He came to Liszt in 1855, when Tausig was only 14. Dresden soon became his musical headquarters where he championed the most contemporary compositions. Liszt called him "the infallible, with his fingers of steel." Between 1862 and 1865 Tausig scored major triumphs from Vienna to Berlin, where he established his own School. Tausig is remembered primarily as the transcriber of Bach's *Toccata and Fugue in D Minor* and Schumann's "The Smuggler." His virtuoso arrangement of the "Ride of the Valkyries" (Schott ed., 13 pp.) is rife with arpeggios, trills, and extremely fast passages of alternating hand patterns as the warrior-maidens gather the souls of the honored dead for assembly in Valhalla.

### Fantasies of Thalberg
*by Daniel Hitchcock*

Sigismund Thalberg was perhaps not a great composer, but he was a great musical personality who wrote in a unique way for the piano. He was born near Geneva in 1812 and through unclear circumstances became the responsibility of the repressive Count Moritz Dietrichstein in Vienna, who acted as patron for Beethoven and Schubert and tutored Napoleon II. Aristocratic by association, therefore, he accordingly assimilated the decorum and knowledge befitting a nobleman which worked to his favor in his chosen career as a pianist.

Never-the-less, advantage only fosters and does not create talent: Thalberg labored over his piano with such persuasive results that the Count relented his anticipation for a budding diplomat. His lessons with such notable masters as Moscheles, Hummel, and Kalkbrenner lasted a surprisingly short time, while his avowed teacher, August Mittag, survives today only as a reference book name. Thalberg studied composition and theory with Simon Sechter, returning to him as late as 1850 for additional advice.

Up to the age of eighteen Thalberg's concert appearances tended to be selective, avoiding the exploitation often given a young talent. After 1830 his public exposure began in earnest. Already he had supplemented his repertoire, including Bach, Mozart, Moscheles, Dussek, Hummel, and Beethoven, with works of his own creation which quickly gained acceptance. Although he subsequently drew criticism for concentrating on composition rather than performing classics, the development of his individual style, weaknesses aside, has become the primary reason for his niche in musical history.

Bound up almost inseparably with Thalberg's artistry was his genteel personality. He clearly reflected a distinguished background—qualities of tact, restraint, gentleness, roughly summed up as good breeding. Absolute self-control governed his performance so that he underscored his astonishing virtuosity with as little motion as humanly possible. The absence of visible activity as much as his faultless technique quickly captivated both amateurs and professionals in his audience.

Thalberg was not only handsome, he was extensively educated. With a command of German, French, Italian, and English (which well equipped him for his lifelong addiction to puns) and a special interest in geography, he at least partially realized the Count's hopes as a perfect musical ambassador. Besides steady touring of the European mainland and the British Isles, he managed two circuits each of the United States and Brazil. During his active concert life Thalberg took only one brief respite at the age of forty-six; he re-emerged four years later with unimpaired powers for two seasons but thereafter gave himself to overseeing his vineyards near Naples until his death in 1871.

Paradoxically, Thalberg's virtuosity in both performance and production tended to minimize itself because he kept melody uppermost in consciousness; the elaborate embellishments still only modified and colored the melody. Under skilled hands the arpeggios and multi-handed illusions that have come to characterize his style became a means to sustain the short-lived sonorities of the piano. One of his most valuable bequests to pianists was his series, *L'Art du Chant appliqué au Piano*, which stressed the sensitive development of tone production through transcriptions of well-known vocal selections. In his transparent intention for effect, however, Thalberg imbued his music with a distinct personality that defied duplication. Others have used his vocabulary with impressive results, but a work from his own pen reveals his unique approach.

In Jules Verne's story "Doctor Ox," a traditionally interminable fourth act of *Les Huguenots* is resourcefully compressed into eighteen minutes through the introduction of a mysterious gas. Perhaps a more agreeable condensation of operatic material can be achieved with the operatic fantasy, and in this genre Thalberg was an indisputable master. Happily, he differed in selection and treatment from that other master, Liszt, giving us twice as much to treasure. While Liszt evokes the dramatic essence of the original, Thalberg puts the music into a purely flattering light. The individuality of both virtuosos permeates yet does not obscure their subject. In his *Fantasy on Meyerbeer's Robert Le Diable* and *Concert Fantasy on Meyerbeer's Les Huguenots*, his repertoire of effects which at this date does not emphasize those famous arpeggios which characterize his late works. Several Thalbergian devices appear, however: fugato sections, anticipation of themes to come, juxtaposition of themes, extended trills. Present also are chromatic runs and extended repetitions of octaves and tenths. Thalberg was accused of including these solely to show off the vaunted double escapement action of Erard's pianos. Schumann reviewed this fantasy when he was in a good humor—his disposition toward Meyerbeer's original revealed far less charity.

### Paraphrase of Pabst
*by Richard Freed*

Paul Pabst (1854-1897), like his father and his older brother in Königsberg, was a performer and pedagogue as well as a composer; he was one of the several younger musicians in whom Liszt took an interest, and he actually studied with Liszt before moving to Moscow to accept an instructorship in piano at Nikolai Rubinstein's Conservatory. Pabst produced a great quantity of music in various forms, but was most successful in the operatic paraphrases modeled after those of his mentor, and of these it is the *Paraphrase on Tchaikovsky's Eugene Onegin* that was the most admired; it is, in fact, virtually the only composition of Pabst's ever referred to by title, let alone performed. Liszt himself composed a brilliant Paraphrase on the Polonaise from Act III of *Onegin* in 1880; Pabst's treatment, which came a few years later, is more than twice as long and makes use of themes from earlier portions of the opera. Essentially, it concerns itself with the well-known Waltz from Act II, with a reference to the opera's *Prelude* by way of introduction; the contrasting middle section cites Lensky's Act I declaration of love of Olga (itself in Waltz rhythm), which in turn encapsulates a fragment of Onegin's aria from the same act.
