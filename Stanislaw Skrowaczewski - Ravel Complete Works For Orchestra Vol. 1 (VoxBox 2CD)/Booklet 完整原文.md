## Ravel and His Concertos
### By Richard Manuel

Both of Ravel's piano concertos were written at the same time, begun in 1930 and completed in the fall of 1931. Except for the song-cycle *Don Quichotte à Dulcinée*, which followed the next year, they represent the end of a curious assignment for a film score offered to several composers simultaneously and ultimately awarded to Jacques Ibert; they were his last compositions in any form. The Concerto in G was begun first, but the Concerto for left-hand finished and performed first; this last is the more often heard of the two.

Ravel spoke of them in an interview with a correspondent for the *Daily Telegraph* of London:

> I was an innocent victim of American publicity men. When they announced that I was writing two concertos for simultaneous performance in America, I didn't know how to extricate myself. I propose to play myself, in a concerto in the strict sense, written in the spirit of Mozart and Saint-Saëns. I believe that a concerto can be both gay and brilliant without necessarily being profound or aiming at dramatic effects. It has been said that Mozart's *Concertos*—and it is in this spirit that I have written mine for the piano, have been written against it. And I think that this criterion is quite justified.

> The word *Concerto* in my title ought to be taken literally: the first meaning of concerto is indeed "contest"; in Bach's time, it signified a contest between two groups of instruments. In my concerto there is no contest between the piano and the orchestra: I have written a concerto in the true sense of the word.

> The Concerto for left hand alone is quite different, and has borrowed from jazz, but only in moderation. There is no attempt at aping or copying. I have tried to write in the pure Ravel manner: the style is as near as possible to that of the G major Concerto.

> After an introduction in 3/4 time, there is a section in 2/4 time which has an improvisation character. This is followed by a jazz section. Only afterwards is it seen that the jazz episode is actually built on the themes of the 3/4 introduction.

In a letter of 19 December 1929, the G major Concerto was "the belated materialization of a plan that was once in youth (Ravel) had kept at the back of his mind. His intention was to play the solo part himself, and in 1913 he had given serious thought to the matter but had to give it up as he lacked the necessary technique to tackle the work."

Ravel acknowledged that, unlike Mozart and Saint-Saëns, he was no virtuoso, and he gave the first performance of the G major Concerto at a concert given in his honor, with the Orchestre Lamoureux under the baton of Pedro de Freitas Branco, with the solo part played by Marguerite Long, who introduced it under his direction at the Salle Pleyel on January 14, 1932. The soloist for the premiere of the left-hand Concerto was Paul Wittgenstein. The Concerto in D, "for the left hand alone," is one of several such works commissioned from various composers by the pianist Paul Wittgenstein, who had lost his right arm in World War I. Among other concertos written for him are the Concertos No. 4 of Prokofiev and Strauss's *Parergon* to the "Symphonia Domestica"; Wittgenstein also commissioned works from Hindemith and Britten.

The Concerto in D major is a Vernon on November 29, 1931. ## The String Quartet

The Quartets of both Debussy and Ravel use the string quartet instruments in very novel combinations, and in this essential respect they stand apart from the rest of their musical oeuvre, as if the two composers, trespassing the boundaries of true chamber music (not a small wonder), accounts for their description as impressionists in this medium.

In reality, both works follow the classical four-movement scheme on fairly regular lines, in direct opposition to the evolving developments of the Frankist School.

Ravel's String Quartet was written when the composer was 28 years old (1903), and the young man of twenty-eight dedicated his youthful masterpiece to his venerated teacher Gabriel Fauré, who indeed had every reason to be proud of his pupil. But Fauré was unable to appreciate the young man's genius, who urged him "not to change a note of it, in the name of the Gods of Music and his own."

The F major Quartet remains a youthful utterance of irresistible charm, showing an open tenderness, an unfaithful humanity not indexed in Ravel's later works.

Formally, it is constructed roughly along the same lines as Debussy's Quartet, but, rather surprisingly, even more closely modeled after the classic pattern of Franck and his pupils. It is an extremely personal work, and is perhaps more perfect. Here, some transitional material shows slight similarities with corresponding sections in Debussy's Quartet, but on the whole, Ravel's piece is broader, more melodic, less ardent and semantically in feeling.

The Scherzo bears the same heading as Debussy's (Partly first very rhythmic, and uses similar devices for pitch production. It combines, as in Debussy, a quite typically Belgian "Squinté" theme, and introduces a rhapsodic, brooding Trio (Slowly) as an effective contrast.
