## Handel's Orlando

For the five winter seasons between December 1729 and June 1734 Handel, assisted by the impresario J.J. Heidegger, was effectively in sole charge of Italian opera at the King's Theatre in London's Haymarket. In the previous decade opera had been produced by the Royal Academy of Music, a group of noblemen and landed gentry who financed the operation in company with other subscribers; but after the collapse of the Academy in 1728 the directors ceded their managerial rights to Handel and Heidegger for five years. The new arrangement allowed Handel the privilege - rare for a composer at this time - of producing operas to some extent according to his own tastes. Thus the series of serious, heroic operas favoured by the Academy was soon broadened by the production in February 1730 of *Partenope*, a setting of a highly amusing libretto by Stampiglia which had been considered by the Academy in 1726, but rejected apparently on the grounds of its 'depravity'.

Pasticcios and adaptations of operas by leading Italian composers also appeared, and in the spring of 1732 came the major innovation of English oratorio in the shape of a revised version of *Esther*, shortly followed by a new version of *Acis and Galatea* as a serenata, mixing music from Handel's earlier Italian and English settings of the story. Handel had good casts for these productions, led from the autumn of 1730 by the great and popular castrato Senesino (Francesco Bernardi), who had also played the major roles in the earlier Academy operas.

Not all the supporters of opera were happy with the situation, however, and *Orlando*, the new opera of Handel's fourth season (1732-33) may have played a part in precipitating a crisis. According to the dates entered in the autograph score, Handel completed Acts I and II by November 1732 and finished the whole score ten days later. The first performance, with Senesino in the title role, took place at the King's Theatre on 27 January 1733. In the so-called 'Colman Register' - a diary of operatic performances in London - the anonymous author noted on 3 February that the 'Clothes & Scenes' were 'all new" and that the opera was 'extraordinary fine & magnificent - perform'd several times'. Nevertheless, the first run consisted of only six performances up to 20 February, after which Handel revived *Floridante* and directed performances of his new oratorio *Deborah* and of *Esther*.

Four more performances of *Orlando* followed, between 21 April and 5 May, but the work was not heard again before the twentieth century. Within a month of the final performance Senesino had resigned from Handel's company.

(having been told, according to a press report, that the composer 'had no farther occasion for his service') and on 15 June several noblemen, supported by the Prince of Wales, met to found a new opera company (later known as the 'Opera of the Nobility') in open opposition to Handel. The rivalry of the two companies continued for three more seasons and though Handel eventually prevailed, the experience clearly soured his involvement with opera. A move to John Rich's new theatre at Covent Garden in 1734 inspired him to one more great and glorious operatic season, with the first performances of *Ariodante* and *Alcina*, but after that it was only in ode and oratorio that Handel was able to display the full range of his genius.

It would not be surprising if *Orlando* itself had contributed to the rift with Senesino. Its many unusual and innovatory features could easily have been puzzling and even upsetting to a singer who had enjoyed 25 years of fame on the operatic stage and was thoroughly steeped in the conventions of the opera seria form. In the whole of the opera he had only three full-length da capo arias, none of which appeared in the final act; his only duet with the leading soprano was short and highly irregular in form; and he was absent from the most substantial ensemble number, the trio at the end of Act I. True, he had the stage to himself for nearly ten minutes for the great 'Mad Scene' at the end of Act II, but it gave him little opportunity for vocal embellishment and, here as elsewhere in the opera, he may well have been confused as to whether he was playing a role which was seriously heroic or subtly comic; if the latter, was it the singer or the character that was being mocked?

This ambiguity of tone, combined with musical magnificence, gives *Orlando* a special place among Handel's operas. It stems from the ultimate source of the libretto, the *Orlando furioso* of Ludovico Ariosto (1474-1533), an epic poem describing the adventures of the knight Orlando (or Roland) and the terrible frenzy he brings upon himself through his frustrated love for Angelica, Queen of Cathay - all dazzlingly elaborated with stories of many other characters and much incidental comment on the human condition. Angelica, to the annoyance of all her noble suitors, eventually yields to the young African soldier Medoro (elevated to princely status in the opera). More specifically, the immediate model for Handel's *Orlando* was an earlier operatic libretto by Carlo Sigismondo Capeci (1652-1728) entitled *L'Orlando, overo La gelosa pazzia* ('Orlando, or the Madness of Jealousy'), set to music (now lost) by Domenico Scarlatti and produced in Rome in 1711.

Capeci's original text had to be substantially altered to fit Handel's company. (We do not know who undertook this task.) The roles of Orlando, Angelica and Medoro presented no problems; the title role naturally went to Senesino, Angelica to the soprano Anna Strada del Po (Handel's leading female singer since 1729 and the only one to remain faithful to him when the rest departed for the Opera of the Nobility) and Medoro to the reliable contralto Francesca Bertoli, who specialised in male roles. The semicomic character of Dorinda was well suited to the buffa soprano Celeste Gismondi, a newcomer to Handel's company whose skills may actually have suggested the choice of the libretto. She is almost certainly to be identified with Celeste Resse, an accomplished singer who took many roles in intermezzi at Naples between 1724 and 1732; her part in the opera was significantly expanded from Capeci's original.

The most drastic revisions to the text were required to provide a fitting part for the only other member of the company, the great bass Antonio Montagnana. Capeci had an important sub-plot involving two royal lovers, the princess Isabella and the young Scottish prince Zerbino; but it was not the practice of the time for a bass to play a young prince, and so Isabella and Zerbino were virtually eliminated and the striking Magus-like character of Zoroastro was invented for Montagnana. (Isabella remains a silent presence in the opera, however: she is the mysterious princess rescued by Orlando in Act I.)

These changes had a profound effect on the tone of the opera, placing extra emphasis on Dorinda, almost (but not quite) transformed into a tragic figure, and altering the whole context of Orlando's madness, no longer the direct effect of his jealous obsession, but a curative frenzy designed and imposed by Zoroastro to clear the hero's mind.

Above all, the new version of the libretto provided a framework for music of far more variety and formal flexibility than could be accommodated in a conventionally organised opera seria; this, surely, was its great attraction for Handel. The opening of Act I, showing Zoroastro contemplating the heavens and reading Orlando's destiny in the stars, brings the first of the many expansive accompanied recitatives which are a prominent feature of the opera. Also unusually prominent are a number of short songs without da capo repeats.

The first of these, Angelica's 'Ritornava al suo bel viso', is delightfully and unexpectedly extended by Medoro as he enters to greet her. In Act III comes the hauntingly memorable 'Già l'ebro mio ciglio' for Orlando, sung as he falls into the healing sleep induced by Zoroastro. Here Handel

scores for two 'violette marine', originally played by the Castrucci brothers; these were violas with sympathetic strings giving additional plangency to the characteristic veiled tone of the instruments. Finally there is 'Per far mia diletta', again for Orlando and sung as he determines on suicide. Stark string unisons in a powerful dotted rhythm dominate the accompaniment, but the finest and simplest effect comes at the end: the final cadence, for continuo alone, is extended by a soaring phrase in the relative major key from Angelica as she begs Orlando to live on.

There are, of course, several formal arias as well, all of high quality, and some fine ensemble pieces. In the beautiful and moving trio at the end of Act I Angelica and Medoro attempt to comfort Dorinda as she realises her love for Medoro must remain unfulfilled, but she remains inconsolable: the music conveys an extraordinary depth of feeling, though spun from a deceptively simple triple-time tune in G major.

Two decidedly unconventional duets appear in Act III. In the first the crazed Orlando begins to woo Dorinda with a gentle minor-key song; she first answers in faster, common-time music, but soon replies in recitative only, leading Orlando to rave about fighting the warrior Ferraù (one of his rivals for Angelica's love in *Orlando furioso*) in the short but quite extraordinary aria 'Già lo stringo': here the main section is a parody gavotte, the middle section is a slow arioso passage moving into the remote key of A flat minor. Orlando's duet with Angelica 'Finchè prendi ancora il sangue' is another remarkable piece, giving the impression that it has two alternating tempos, one for each singer, though in fact the beat remains constant.

The scene which closes Act II is the most astonishing stretch of music to be found in the opera - perhaps in any Baroque opera. Driven mad by the sight of Angelica and Medoro carved on the trees, Orlando imagines himself on a journey into the underworld. The music begins as accompanied recitative, first vigorous, then slower, with ill-defined, shifting harmonies as Orlando declares himself to be 'a shade divided from myself. Brief passages of 5/8 time, played by unison strings, appear as he imagines himself entering Charon's boat and crossing the Styx. The final section is a rondo on a plain yet very touching tune in gavotte rhythm, its statements separated by two episodes. One of these is based on a chromatic ground-bass, surely intended as an allusion to the old-style laments of seventeenth-century opera (such as that found in Dido and Aeneas); the other, marking Orlando's change to vengeful mood, is dominated by fast and furious writing for the violins.

Handel's achievement throughout this scene, as elsewhere in the opera, is to convey a sense of disorder and chaos in music which by its very nature has to be highly organised; this he does by juxtaposing ideas often disparate in tempo and rhythm but held together by an unremitting control of the underlying harmonic progressions. In the opera as a whole there is a general sense of disturbance, not completely dispelled by the jaunty nature of the final coro (in which Orlando's little solo remains in the minor key and is broken up by manically jerky rhythms). If Orlando was one of the reasons that Handel's singers decided to leave him and join a new operatic company, sensing that he was pushing judgment would not have been entirely wrong.

In this performance of *Orlando* the orchestral layout is modelled on contemporary illustrations of opera orchestras, with first violins and woodwind in front of the second violins and violas, and with cellos, basses and the two harpsichords placed at the sides.

*Anthony Hicks*

## Synopsis

### Act I

Countryside at night, with a view of a mountain on which Atlas is seen supporting the heavens on his shoulders.

Zoroastro contemplates the constellations, obscure in meaning to ordinary mortals, which tell him that Orlando will one day return to deeds of glory ('Gieroglifici eterni'). Orlando appears, torn between conflicting desires for love and glory ('Stimulato dalla gloria'). Zoroastro rebukes him for his devotion to love, and with a wave of his wand causes the view of the mountain to change to the Palace of Love, where heroes of antiquity appear asleep. He urges Orlando to abandon Love and follows Mars, the god of war ('Lascia Amor e segui Marte'). Orlando is at first shamed by the vision, but then considers that glory can be obtained in pursuit of love: Hercules remained a hero despite his affair with Omphale ('Non fu già men forte Alcide'), as did Peleides when in a damsel's soft array he threatened Asia's realms to blast' (an oblique reference to Achilles, son of Peleus, who as a boy was disguised as a girl and sent to Scyros to avoid the Trojan war; he was discovered when he puguaciously brandished a sword offered to him by Odysseus).

A little wood with shepherds' huts.

The shepherdess Dorinda reflects on the beauties of nature, once delightful, but now filling her with grief, perhaps - she is not sure - because she is in love. Orlando rushes past with a princess he has just rescued (identified later as Isabella); he too - thinks Dorinda - may be affected by love. She really does not know what she feels: Orlando's attentions she has fallen in love with Medoro, whose wounds she healed while he was being looked after by Dorinda ('Ho un certo rossore'). Orlando overhears this, and declares his love for Dorinda. She is at first flattered by such noble attention, but as Orlando becomes more ardent ('Unisca amor in noi') and addresses her as the goddess Venus, it becomes obvious that he is still raving. Suddenly Dorinda becomes identified in his mind with Angelica's brother Argalia, murdered by Ferraù, another of Orlando's rivals. He squares up for unarmed combat with Ferraù, throwing away his helmet and sword ('T'ubbidirò ... Fammi combattere'), and leaves. Dorinda tells Angelica of Orlando's madness. Angelica expresses her pity for him and hopes that he will be able to overcome it ('Così giusta è questa speme'). Dorinda delivers her final thoughts on love: it's a wind that sets the brain spinning, bringing as much pain as joy ('Amor è qual vento').

### Act II

A wood.

Dorinda finds the melancholy song of the nightingale appropriate to her sadness ('Quando spieghi i tuoi tormenti'). Orlando appears and asks her why she has been suggesting that he is in love with the princess Isabella. Confused, Dorinda says she has been misunderstood: she was speaking of Angelica and her new-found love for Medoro. Orlando immediately recognises the bracelet from Zilante that he once gave to Angelica: she has betrayed him, he says; surely she has yielded to one of his great rivals? No, says Dorinda, just the young man named Medora, whose face she sees in every flower; the sounds of stream and forest seem to tell her that he still waits for her ('Se mi rivolgo al prato'). Orlando gives vent to his anger: he threatens to kill himself so that he can pursue Angelica into Hell itself ('Cielo! Se tu il consenti').

On one side a laurel grove, on the other the opening of a grotto.

Zoroastro rebukes Angelica and Medora for arousing Orlando's anger: flight is their only course. He warns them that the minds of mortals wander in darkness when they are led by the blind god of love ('Tra caligini profonde'). The lovers are sad to leave. Medora decides to

carve their names on the laurel trees to declare their love to the world ('Verdi allori'). Angelica resolves to return to Cathay with Medora. Though she is grateful to Orlando for once having saved her life, she believes he will understand that love cannot be compelled by gratitude or reason ('Non potrà dirmi ingrata').

Orlando enters the grove and sees the names of Angelica and Medora on the trees. He rushes into the grotto in pursuit of Angelica. She, however, appears from the grove on the opposite side and sadly bids farewell to the trees and the streams ('Verdi piante, erbette liete'). Orlando chases Angelica into the grove. Medora appears and follows them in hot pursuit. Zoroastro's magic now intervenes: Angelica is engulfed by a large cloud which bears her away in the company of four genii. Orlando finally loses his reason ('Ah Stigie larve!'). He believes that shades from the underworld have taken Angelica from him. He will follow them, becoming a shade himself. He crosses the Styx in Charon's boat and sees the smoking towers of Pluto's kingdom. Cerberus barks at him and Furies attack him ('Già latra Cerbero'). The greatest Fury of all takes the form of Medora, who runs into the arms of Proserpine (Pluto's queen). She weeps, and Orlando's rage abates as he sees that even in Hell love can arouse tears. He begs the weeping to cease ('Vaghe pupille, non piangete, no'), since his pity has already been obtained; but finally his rage returns - no tears shall prevail against his hard heart. As he runs back into the opening it bursts open to reveal Zoroastro on his chariot. The magician gathers Orlando up in his arms and flies off with him.

### Act III

A grove of palm trees.

Medora explains to Dorinda that Angelica has sent him to her for refuge. She is annoyed that he has not come to see her on his own account. He explains that his heart is no longer his to offer ('Vorrei poterti amar'). Dorinda is glad that he is no longer deceiving her, declares his love for Dorinda. She is at first flattered by such noble attention, but as Orlando becomes more ardent ('Unisca amor in noi') and addresses her as the goddess Venus, it becomes obvious that he is still raving. Suddenly Dorinda becomes identified in his mind with Angelica's brother Argalia, murdered by Ferraù, another of Orlando's rivals. He squares up for unarmed combat with Ferraù, throwing away his helmet and sword ('T'ubbidirò ... Fammi combattere'), and leaves. Dorinda tells Angelica of Orlando's madness. Angelica expresses her pity for him and hopes that he will be able to overcome it ('Così giusta è questa speme'). Dorinda delivers her final words: he will be able to overcome it. Dorinda tells him what has happened but instead pretends to be jealous and taunts him about the princess Isabella he has just rescued. Zoroastro prevents the untimely approach of Medora by causing him to be concealed by a fountain as the whole scene is transformed into a delightful garden. Angelica tells Orlando he must prove his faith by never seeing the princess again; he cannot have Angelica's love while there is suspicion in her heart ('Se fedel vuoi, ch'io ti creda'). Orlando says he will obey her, and that he would fight the most terrible monsters to show the strength of his love ('Fammi combattere').

Medora finds Angelica and demands to know whom she has been talking to. She tells him it is Orlando, and persuades him (with little difficulty) not to fight such a rival. They arrange to meet again. Their parting embrace is seen by Dorinda, who finally forces Angelica to explain that Medora is now her betrothed lover. She thanks Dorinda for her previous kindness and gives her a piece of jewellery. Dorinda says she would sooner have had a gift from her beloved Medora. Medora begs her to forgive him, but she says she has been hurt in a way that he will never understand. Angelica and Medora try to comfort her, but she remains inconsolable (trio: 'Consolati o bella').

Zoroastro appears with his genii and orders them to change the scene to 'a horrid cavern'. He promises to restore Orlando to his former glory. Just as a tempest yields to clear skies, so the faults of those who err will retreat as they are recognised ('Sorge infausta una procella').

Dorinda, in tears, tells Angelica that Orlando has destroyed her house and buried Medora in the ruins. Orlando himself appears, addressing Angelica as the sorceress Falerina and threatening to kill her; but she defies him, grief-stricken by the news of Medora's death (duet: 'Finchè prendi ancora il sangue'). Orlando throws her into the cavern, but as he does so it changes into a beautiful temple of Mars. Orlando claims he has rid the world of all its terrible monsters. Drowsiness comes over him, and, believing he has drunk the waters of the river Lethe, he lies down to sleep ('Già l'ebro mio ciglio').

Zoroastro appears, declaring the time has come to restore Orlando's senses. He sends for the eagle of Jupiter which, guided by the genii, flies down with a golden vessel in its beak. This contains a liquid which Zoroastro sprinkles over Orlando's face. He awakes, his senses restored. Dorinda tells him he has murdered Medora in his frenzy. Full of remorse, he decides to kill himself ('Per far mia diletta'), but Angelica stops him, bidding him to live on. Medora was in fact saved by Zoroastro, who now implores Orlando to accept the betrothal of Angelica and Medora. A statue of Mars, with fire burning on an altar, rises as Orlando proclaims victory over himself and hands Angelica to Medora. He wishes them joy ('Trionfa oggi'l mio cor'), Angelica and Medora promise to be true to each other, and Dorinda, inviting them all back to her cottage, says she will forget her sorrows. All join in praise of love and glory.
