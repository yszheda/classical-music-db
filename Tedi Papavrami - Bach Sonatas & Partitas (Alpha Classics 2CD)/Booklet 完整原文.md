## BOOKLET ESSAY BY JACQUES DRILLON

### "BACH RENEWED BY JACQUES DRILLON"

*"One doesn't create by adding, but by subtracting"* (Robert Bresson)

In the 18th century, the bass part was the father, head of the household, protector: provider of food; his children were able to feed themselves, but they owed the food to him. Deprived of a father, a family had to make do with a fleeting transient boss, often implicit, oblique: rather like someone who, unable to speak, seeks proper to satisfy a need.

By writing *senza basso* and denying himself the comfort of taking orders from the bass part, Bach subjects himself to a tremendous constraint, comparable to the one Ravel would later observe in his *Boléro* (no modulation, no development, no rhythmic variation). And Bach undergoes this restriction, not just for one or two pieces, as a passing exercise, but throughout three sonatas and three partitas: an immense obligation, and to fully appreciate it we have to recognize the original difficulty of the task — making no concession, not even here, to the convenience of familiarity. (For as time passes, we are no longer astonished by anything.)

Bach Papavrami has certainly no intention of being a modest or timid violinist. He too has been through a radicalization process. He has renounced all sorts of uso vibrato, except for a very few rare and wondrous exceptions. He vibrato in his notes, or highlighting the brilliance of the high register, shunning all the flamboyant virtuosity generally employed by violinists — effects that characterize the most celebrated performances of these works to the extent that they now pass as indelible traditions. To achieve this renunciation, he has evidently chosen to confine himself to phrasing and sound. He does this partly with minuscule gradations in the lengths of the notes, in their articulation and in their breathing, while extremely emphatic. However, the musical discourse can be followed in the same way as a text spoken by a great actor able to make even the densest himself by phrasing and sound.

On the other hand, Papavrami gives the violin its full sound, a strong, expansive, affirmative sonority — aided by an acoustic that respects with interest everything the instrument is genuinely bringing to it. In the hands of a performer who knows how to have a true dialogue with the ambient sound.

As shattering in its impact as were the first Brandenburg recordings 'on period instruments' by Harnoncourt in 1964, this revolutionary new project spurns both Misschien nor Kuijken, but reconciles them with each other; and at the same time makes us forget them. To put it in visual, dramatic terms, this project is situated somewhere between Rob Wilson and Robert Bresson, with the hallucinatory technique of the former, and the latter's economy of means. It applies Bresson's principle of 'emptying the pool' to get at the flesh, but with Wilson's virtuosity, sophistication and laser-like precision. (Incidentally, the image of the violinist alone on the stage of the annual Grecian theatre of the Metz Arsenal is itself a very Wilsonian vision, the patch of light around his feet finishing off the tableau.)

In his first recording of the Sonatas and Partitas by Bach, Papavrami took the most demanding classicism to the greatest possible height, showing that if a tradition must be respected, he was prepared to go right back to the work in order to understand and love it better, before extracting the same in writing his brilliant 'classical' novel. Les mots, ce doit Go d'accord in his film *Le mépris*, and so particularly did Bresson in putting the final touches to *Les anges* (Bois de Boulogne) before turning his back on everything that did not lead to the truth pure and simple. Picasso too: let nobody come along and say he did not know how to draw! All these creatives gave every proof of mastery, and got all that fruit out of their gift.

Perhaps because Tedi Papavrami's intelligence stimulates his talent instead of curbing it, he burns with the same fire, one that consumes habit and the acquired practice. To overturn is not the same as to smash, and a resonance does not mean an accident practice. To overturn is not the same as to smash, and a resonance does not mean an accident. It is not a question of undoing what has been done, but of doing it differently. And if it could be that playing Paganini, Vivaldi, Brahms or Tchaïkovsky were merely a matter of heritage and not an advantage, and that Bach demands a particular treatment, one equally appropriate to the different violin and styles of David Oistrakh and Pierre Dervaux. Let alone to a post-modernist aesthetic that is still trying to go beyond the conflict, seeking to win over both camps by casting the widest possible net — a campaigning politician; but rather in a series of subtraction operations applied to each of the two camps as they say to say 'This you can do without, this too, that is not essential either, so let's slash, cut away. Empty the pool.'

Out of the doubtless difficult, painful process, a drastic surgical resection no less, is born a unique unity, incomparable in the real sense of the word, made up of sweetness and modesty, and a readiness to publicity prudence, and at the same time a sense of private reserve; this is a style in which a wealth of sparkling colour and the etched line of a drypoint needle are no longer mutually opposed. One can begin to understand how Pascal and Bossuet could preach humility in such sumptuously high-toned phrases. In a way — his way — Papavrami has joined their company.

**Jacques Drillon**

The manuscript of the *Seri Solo à Violino senza Basso accompagnato* dated 1720, is — from the brilliant pen of the artist — from the Service of the Prince of Anhalt-Cöthen. Bach, which explore the instrumental with a respect methodical that let it be seen: ces partitas (françaises) et sonates (italiennes) pour violon sans basse. Les six suites seules pour violon seul (sauf basse dessus), les six Concerto Brandebourgeois (avec leurs diverses pièces), et pour le Clavier les six suites Françaises et les œuvres palésagogiques. (Le premier livre du Clavier bien tempéré [avec ses 24 pièces ordonnées par demi-tons], les quinze Inventions à deux voix et les quinze Symphonies à trois.)

On a cherché un plan dans l'enchaînement des tonalités des Sei solo [sic], la, ré, sol, ut, mi, mais il semble à la réflexion que Bach se soit contenté d'alterner les formes italiennes (sonates en quatre mouvements, dont un prélude lent suivi d'une fugue) et françaises (suites de danses enrichies de diverses manières).

L'écriture pour violon sans basse n'était pas nouvelle (Valther, 1676 et 1687; Biber, 1676, Westhoff, 1696), mais Bach fut le premier à la porter à ces sommets de virtuosité d'écriture et de spéculation. Ses contemporains ne s'y trompèrent pas, qui copièrent abondamment le manuscrit (lequel finit dans la collection d'autographes de Brahms). Il semble que Bach, violoniste éminent, les ait joués lui-même.

**Jacques Drillon**

### BACH À NOUVEAU ET DE NOUVEAU PAR JACQUES DRILLON

*"On ne crée pas en ajoutant, mais en retranchant"* (Robert Bresson)

Au XVIIe siècle, la basse, c'est le père, celui qui commande, protège et nourrit: on s'alimente, mais c'est à lui qu'on le doit. Privée de père, la mélodie doit composer avec une basse passagère, souvent oblique, décalée; comme on trompe sa faim avec des fruits et des baies.

En écrivant *senza basso* — en s'interdisant la facilité de suivre les indications d'une basse — Bach s'impose une contrainte terrible, comparable seulement à celle que Ravel observera dans son Boléro (pas de modulation, pas de développement, pas de variation, ni dans le rythme, ni dans le tempo); il s'impose pas pour une pièce ou deux, il s'impose pour un exercice, mais dans trois sonates et trois partitas: une immense défi, dont il faut retrouver aujourd'hui, pour en apprécier la portée, l'inouïe de la difficulté première — sans céder ici, en rien plus, au confort de l'habitude et de la familiarité. (Avec le temps, on ne s'étonne plus de rien.)
