## Pages 4-5 (Liner Notes)

### Elgar – Boult – Haendel: Violin Concerto

In April 1977, when Sir Adrian Boult commenced the last of his three recordings of Elgar's Violin Concerto, his knowledge of the work covered the entire 67-year span of its existence. He had attended the premiere, given at Queen's Hall, London in November 1910, with Fritz Kreisler as soloist and Elgar himself as conductor. Sir Adrian remembered this as a great occasion and he was invited to a supper after the concert, hosted by a friend of both Elgar and Boult, Frank Schuster: it was he who had first introduced Boult to Sir Edward some six years earlier.

In February 1918 Boult was due to conduct Elgar's concert overture *In the South* for the first time, and he asked the composer to go through the score with him. Sir Edward readily agreed, and two years later he attended a Boult performance of his Second Symphony which inspired a remarkable note of thanks to the younger man. "I feel", wrote Sir Edward, "that my reputation in the future is safe in your hands. It was a wonderful series of sounds. Bless you". In the same year, 1920, Boult conducted his first performance of the Violin Concerto. This was at a Royal College of Music concert with Mannus O'Donnell as soloist. Once more Boult sought Elgar's advice, which was again given at a play-through of the work by soloist and conductor.

Over the next five decades Boult frequently conducted the Violin Concerto, several times with two admired early interpreters of the work, Albert Sammons and Yehudi Menuhin (it was they, of course, who made the first complete recordings of the work, in 1929 and 1932 respectively – Sammons with Wood, and Menuhin, as a 16-year-old, with the composer conducting). Boult's own first recording of the work took place in October 1954, at Decca sessions in London's Kingsway Hall, with Alfredo Campoli as soloist.

During the early 1960s Sir Adrian was rather ignored by the recording companies, but this was also the time when Elgar's music began to gain favour among the musical public, following a long period of comparative neglect after the composer's death in 1934. Boult was regarded as the most authoritative Elgar conductor though and Sir John Barbirolli's Elgar interpretations also enjoyed a high reputation, and on the wave of the composer's revival in popularity Sir Adrian was asked to make some new recordings. In December 1965 he undertook his first EMI session for six years, when he worked with Yehudi Menuhin and the New Philharmonia Orchestra on a new recording of the Elgar Violin Concerto. It was indicative of the disfavour into which Elgar's music had fallen that it had taken 13 years for Menuhin to be given the chance to remake his 78rpm recording, using more modern techniques. EMI took every opportunity to exploit the 'historical' aspect of the new recording. In 1932 a photograph had been taken of the teenage Menuhin with the composer on the steps of the Abbey Road studios. Now a new photograph was taken with Boult and the older Menuhin, posed at the same spot as before. Both photos were reproduced on the record sleeve.

Boult's regard for the Concerto seems to have been a little ambiguous. Writing in his autobiography, *My Own Trumpet* (Hamish Hamilton, 1973), he acknowledged the work's novel use of concerto structure, particularly in the way that the cadenza "grew out of the final movement and gathered up material from the whole week". But he also remarked of the Concerto that "modern criticism may find it rather long-winded and cumbersome with all its great beauty". Yet in April 1969, when Boult celebrated his 80th birthday with a special Royal Albert Hall concert, one of the three works he chose was the Violin Concerto, which was played by Menuhin.

Ida Haendel has no reservations about the Elgar Concerto. She regards it as a "giant of a concerto", worthy to be mentioned in the same context as the Beethoven and Brahms Concertos. Despite its "Englishness" she sees it as a composition which speaks an international language. She loves to play it since she feels that it suits her musical personality. It has depth of feeling, a certain melancholy, tension, drama, a strongly virtuoso element, and an "inspiring" cadenza.

Before making her recording she had played the work in public for many years, and she remembers her very first performance of the work to have been in collaboration with Sir Adrian. As a very young artist she was very much championed in England by Sir Henry Wood, whose fatherly warmth and help she remembers to this day with great affection. In earlier times she also worked with a certain extent with Boult, but by comparison with Wood she found him a more formal, remote colleague.

In 1977 Ida Haendel recorded a very successful coupling of the Britten and Walton Concertos. British music was then very much 'in the air' as a result of the Silver jubilee of Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth II, and it seemed logical to ask Haendel to record a third great British concerto, the Elgar. Her partners in the Britten/Walton coupling had been Paavo Berglund and the Bournemouth Symphony Orchestra, but now a new idea emerged. For a decade Boult had enjoyed a popular "Indian summer" in the recording studio, recording not only British repertoire, but the music of Brahms, Wagner and other mainstream composers. Though very frail in his late eighties ("I wish all this had happened 15 years ago, when I was stronger", he complained to a friend), he was still able to take on a limited amount of session work, but had now committed most of his important repertoire to tape. There was talk of a new Beethoven Eroica Symphony, but Sir Adrian felt there would be little point in this exercise. "My *Eroica* is very much like Bohm's", he declared. The chance could have been taken to re-record a somewhat unsatisfactory 1956 cycle of the Schumann symphonies, but instead Sir Adrian recorded his fifth version of Holst's *The Planets*.

The Elgar Violin Concerto was one important work by the composer which had so far eluded the regular team of Boult, the London Philharmonic Orchestra, producer Christopher Bishop and balance engineer Christopher Parker. To collaborate with a gifted younger colleague was just the thing to stimulate Boult's interest at this late stage of his career. A friend queried the wisdom of re-recording a work which had been successfully committed to disc with Menuhin only a dozen years previously. Sir Adrian's response was firm enough. Referring to Haendel's interpretation he said, "It really is very good, you know". And to Michael Kennedy, on 13 June 1977, he wrote, "Ida Haendel is playing the Elgar superbly. We have recorded movements I and II and we hope for III in July. I haven't heard of her for years but she can play". In the event sessions for the Concerto which had begun on 24 April and 10 June were not completed until 10 and 15 January 1978.

If Ida Haendel had found Sir Adrian a somewhat formal colleague in earlier years, these late sessions were another matter. Boult had now mellowed: there was an excellent rapport between conductor and soloist, and Haendel remembers that they had "really a good, good time", and that there was a "joyful" atmosphere in Abbey Road's Studio No.1.

Seventeen years later Ida Haendel returned to that same studio for a very different project, a recording of the complete Bach solo Partitas and Sonatas. This was the first original recording made by Testament (CD SBT 2090, LP SBT 1090), and it had two unusual features. Firstly, analogue tape was used in Studio No.1 for the first time in several years, in order to capture the string sound to best effect. And the performances were issued in two formats – compact disc and vinyl LP - the latter in order to satisfy the needs of 'audiophile' record collectors.

From this highly successful set is extracted the great Chaconne from the Second Partita. Ida Haendel studied with two great, but very contrasted, exponents of Bach's violin literature, Carl Flesch and George Enescu. But she played the Chaconne from an early age. Her independent studies of Bach's works revealed another composer to the music. "What, in my youth, I had heard in the romantic composers, I discovered, to my amazement, in the music of Bach, but in a different style. The drama, the tempest, the tragedy and tears, are all there. My aim in performing Bach is to be totally faithful to the score, yet to incorporate, in a controlled way and within the framework of the piece, all the emotions that are so clear to me".
