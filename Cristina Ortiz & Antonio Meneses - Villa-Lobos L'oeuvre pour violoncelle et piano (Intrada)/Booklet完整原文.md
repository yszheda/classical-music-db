## Heitor Villa-Lobos Biography (English extract)
Heitor Villa-Lobos was born in Rio de Janeiro on 5 March 1887. As the son of a Brazilian musician, he played a pioneering role in the development of classical music in his country. Alongside composers like Alberto Nepomuceno (1864-1920) and Ernesto Nazareth (1863-1934), he is credited with bringing Brazilian music to international prominence, paving the way for composers such as Carlos Chávez in Mexico and Alberto Ginastera in Argentina.

A highly productive composer, he created over a thousand works spanning almost every genre: operas, numerous piano pieces, vocal and choral works, string quartets, orchestral scores for all manner of ensembles. He had a particular affinity for two instruments: the guitar, for which he composed 12 Études, 5 Préludes and a virtuosic Concerto; and the cello, which inspired three concertante pieces, numerous chamber works and the two pages of the *Bachianas Brasileiras* where the "savagery" of the Amazon is presented under its most tender aspects: the final toccata of *Bachianas Brasileiras n° 2*, *O trenzinho do caipira* (The Little Train of the Peasant), with its vibrant cello solo, and the *Aria* for soprano and eight cellos of *Bachianas Brasileiras n° 5*.

The son of Noêmia, an employee of the Spanish-origin National Library, and Raúl, a civil servant and keen music lover who initiated him on the clarinet and cello, Villa-Lobos was twelve years old when his father died. Despite showing great musical promise, the young man abandoned his conservatory studies, preferring to play the cello in theatres, cafes and cinemas, thus ensuring the financial support of his mother and sisters. Above all, he secretly joined the choroes, groups of choro players (a popular dance form characterised by virtuosic improvisations), and developed an extraordinary talent as a guitarist.

In 1905, he undertook a journey through the Amazon in search of the songs of primitive tribes and the sounds of the forest. Other expeditions followed, which Villa-Lobos would shroud in mystery, claiming for example that he was captured by a cannibal tribe. He was fascinated by the richness of this tradition, where Amerindian, Portuguese, Spanish and African influences blend together. In 1906, Villa-Lobos tried his luck at the Rio music institute, but gave up after a few weeks. His only "scholarly" training was as an autodidact: he read Vincent d'Indy's *Cours de composition musicale*, studied the scores of ancient and contemporary composers, with a preference for Bach, and received occasional advice from senior conservatory professors Nascimento and Braga.

At the turn of the century, his early works show the influences of Wagner, Puccini and Franck, before revealing those of Debussy and Stravinsky. But the choro and Amazonian folklore – birdsongs to indigenous percussion – would remain his major source of inspiration: "My first treatise on harmony was the map of Brazil", he would say. And his great works of maturity are the synthesis of all these experiences.

Even if the first concert dedicated to his works in November 1915 provoked hostile reactions, Villa-Lobos' talent was soon recognised. He received the support of the great pianist Arthur Rubinstein, who presented him to patrons and ensured the creation of three partitions. The young composer made several trips abroad, particularly to Argentina. In 1923, he settled in Paris where he continued to travel, notably to Dakar where he was imbued with African music. He performed in the French capital the series of *Chôros* before a disconcerted public. Rubinstein presented him to the publisher Max Eschig, who took him under contract, as well as to Picasso, Edgard Varèse and Florent Schmitt. The latter, who defended his works in *Le Temps*, painted the portrait of "this three-quarters god of fire with his braise eyes and crocodile teeth smoking Havanas". As much as the "barbarian" rhythms of Stravinsky, the extravagant Brazilian fascinated a musical avant-garde hungry for strong sensations. Like Milhaud, who met Villa-Lobos during one of his stays in Brazil, Varèse and Prokofiev succumbed in turn to this unpredictable and luxuriant music like the Amazonian forest. On Sunday afternoons, one would go to the feijoadas that Villa-Lobos hosted in Saint-Michel.

On his definitive return to Rio in 1930, Villa-Lobos was entrusted with the organisation of Brazilian musical education by the revolutionary government. He took his new duties very much to heart: "I consider music as a vital nourishment for the human soul. Consequently, it is an indispensable element in the education of young people" (1946).
