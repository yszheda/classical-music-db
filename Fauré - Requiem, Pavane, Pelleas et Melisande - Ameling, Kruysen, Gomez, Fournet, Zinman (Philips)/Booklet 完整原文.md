## FAURÉ'S "LULLABY OF DEATH"

In one sense, Fauré's Requiem of 1877-1900 is a requiem against Berlioz: it began life in 1887, and "In Paradisum" of January 25, 1904 is wrote of the latter as being a score "as noisy as the large-scale symphony's effects and indifference towards religious music... may find equal satisfaction." Fauré expressed particular distaste for the huge orchestras of Berlioz's "Tuba mirum" but, as the dates 1877-1900 attached to his own Requiem suggest, that is not exactly the quite different effect which he desired.

Fauré's idea was that this Requiem should be peacefully remote yet intimate, altogether different in intention from the convention of the concert-hall found in so many nineteenth-century masses. They conventionally dramatic portrayal of fervent hope was to be replaced with calm acceptance, or at any rate with Elysian calm. And certainly there was to be nothing of the horrors of death emphasised in Berlioz.

Despite this apparent certainty of purpose, Fauré's Requiem exists in three different states, and the complex tale of the many re-visions cannot be told here. The version employed today, and in this recording, was not printed until 1901. It was, indeed, his publisher Hamelle who wanted the initial conception expanded, apparently believing thus the work's greater permanence. Perhaps he did, but Fauré was still able to give us, as he wished, a Mass for the Dead without a Last Judgment.

Many years later his friend Louis Aguettant related that Fauré had in 1902 remarked to him: "It has been said that my Requiem does not express the fear of death, and someone has called it a lullaby of death. But I see death—as a happy deliverance, an aspiration towards happiness above, rather than as a painful experience" ("Comœdia," March 1934). Thus the word "requiem" is prominent in five of the seven movements, and this small statistic might well explain this choosing of the "Paradisum" with which to end the work. Likewise the final two lines of the "Pie Jesu," "Sempiternam requiem," "dona eis," and the phrase "dona eis requiem," and, again, the word "dona" set over and over again, mean "Give them the Dead to the Order of Burial, with the "requiem aeternam." We move from the Introitus for the Dead to the Order of Burial, with the responsory "Libera me" and antiphon "In Paradisum," and these bring to a close this briefest personal version of the Requiem Mass.

The approach was, of course, one that suited Fauré and his strengths as a composer, and that is very evident here. These include long, serene melodies and simple harmonies. The textures are clear and spacious structures—surprisingly Bach-like virtuos. The appeal of this music also arises, however, from the composer's links with Mediterranean culture, whence come his modality, elliptical originality, and enharmonic transitions. It should be added that Fauré's harmonic originality, and it is, as well demonstrates, consists not in the chords being new but in their being used freshly.

All these characteristic come together in the works of his maturity. In this instance conveying a purity, a sense of mystery, that transcends mundane reality and draws close to expressing the inexpressible.

It might seem a long way from a rarified work like the Requiem to the exigencies of the professional musical world. Fauré's career are just as apparent in "Pelléas et Mélisande," which is indeed Fauré's best and most substantial orchestral work. Maeterlinck's symbolist play had first been seen in Paris in 1893, and at its timeless setting and rapid succession of brief scenes that leave so much unsaid virtually ask for music.

Fauré's was in fact the first such response, predating Debussy's opera, Schoenberg's symphonic poem, Cyril Scott's overture and incidental music, and Poulenc's incidental music. He was at the request of the celebrated actress Mrs. Patrick Campbell for the first British production, using Jack Macdall's translation, in London, June 1898. Only six weeks were available for the task, but he produced some 17 numbers. This, however, involved the adaptation of a certain amount of existing material from the Sicilienne, Op. 78 for cello and piano and part of the Shylock music. The complete Pelléas score, these now being the fourth and fifth movements of the present suite.

As he composed the music, chiefly in April 1898, he passed it to his pupil Charles Koechlin for orchestration. Fauré conducted at the first performance in London, May 1898, being solely concerned with overall unity of effect, and therefore with broad matters of tempo, dynamics. There are other cross-references, too, and thematic ideas from Mélisande's song, "Les eaux de la mer," first heard at the beginning of Act III and now the third movement of the suite, provide the basis of the Molto adagio—Death of Mélisande in Act V. (There is a further echo in "Crépuscule," the ninth song of his 1906-10 song cycle, "La chanson d'Ève.")

Many of the pieces that Fauré wrote for theatrical use were not suitable for independent performance, but later in 1898 he reorchestrated three of them, using Koechlin's version as a basis, thus becoming the first version of the concert suite. They were the "Prélude," the Andantino quasi allegretto known as "La Fileuse," and the "Death of Mélisande." The remaining numbers were added at various later dates, resulting in the suite in the form in which it is presented here.

There is more unobtrusively effective orchestral writing in the "Pavane" Op. 50, one of Fauré's most familiar works. It was, in fact, originally designed for orchestra alone, in 1886, the choral section of the 1887-1900 orchestra's frankly unimpressive verses being added the following year. The choral parts are very light, in keeping with the graceful delicacy of music that is most effective when not taken too slowly. Rather more dramatic is the middle section with its series of four-bar sequences over bass pedal points that descend by whole tones, as in the Requiem, where the opening "Requiem aeternam" reappears towards the end of the Agnus Dei.
