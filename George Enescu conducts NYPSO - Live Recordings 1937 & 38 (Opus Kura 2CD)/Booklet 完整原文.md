## About the Recordings

George Enescu (or Enesco as known mostly in France and USA) was without any doubt one of the most musical gifted minds of the 20th Century. Not only was his conducting of some of the greatest classical works ever conducted, but also a leading violinist of his generation. The only recordings for Columbia have already been reissued many times - standard Ysaye dedicated the single movement of his third violin sonata, known as "Ballade," to the Enescu in admiration of his art.

Not only was he a distinguished conductor (he conducted amongst most famous orchestras, the New York Philharmonic and the Paris Symphony Orchestra), but also a gifted teacher (after his illustrious list of pupils there were Yehudi Menuhin and Christian Ferras and Ida Haendel). Indeed, at his 1950 farewell concerts in New York, Enescu appeared as a violinist with Yehudi Menuhin in Bach's "Double Concerto," pianist in his own Violin Sonata with Menuhin, and conductor (in his "Romanian Rhapsody No.1"). Details of his recordings as violinist, conductor or pianist (it was said that he could also play the cello, and was known to excel in the instrument) and was an excellent biographer, "George Enescu: His Life and Music" (Palc: Torcata Press, 1990).

Yehudi Menuhin often spoke of "The memory of Enescu", referring to the twenty-first Century as the time in which he met him and conducted by the most wonderful world-wide recognition. The present release, commemorating 60 years since this great musician (once described by Pablo Casals as "the greatest minded phenomenon since Mozart") passed away, are his first two concerts with the New York Philharmonic-Symphony Orchestra. Enescu first appearance with the New York Philharmonic-Symphony Orchestra (NYPS) was back in 1929, 3, he later conducted Bartok's Violin Concerto in 1939. He was the director of Walter Damrosch. His official engagements as conductor or soloist with his orchestras commenced on January 28, 1927 and lasted until May 2, 1939 accumulating some 22 performances. His divorce in his final years included 24 works of 36 composers ranging in style and time span from Bach to Walter Piston.
