# Béla Bartók: The Piano Concertos

Béla Bartók wrote three piano concertos spanning twenty years, from his First in 1926 to his Third, which was completed just before his death in 1945. Between them stands the Second Piano Concerto of 1930-31. Together they form a trilogy that charts the development of Bartók's mature style, from the angry modernism of the First, through the finely contrasted and expansive invention of the Second, to the eloquent and glowing synthesis of the Third, written when he knew he was dying.

## Page 2

### Piano Concerto No. 1 (1926)

Bartók's First Piano Concerto came as a shock to the system when it was first performed in Frankfurt on 1 July 1927, with the composer as soloist and Wilhelm Furtwängler conducting. The work is scored for an unusual orchestra with a very prominent percussion section – Bartók was fascinated by rhythm at this period – and the piano writing is uncompromisingly percussive, aggressive and dissonant. It is in one continuous movement, divided into three main sections: Moderato – Allegro, Andante – attacca, Allegro. The work reflects Bartók's interest in barbaric rhythmic energy, a direction that had been evident in his music since the *Allegro barbaro* of 1911. The opening subject is announced by timpani and percussion, taken up by the piano with massive chordal writing. The Andante provides a stark contrast, with eerie piano sonorities against quiet percussion. The final movement returns to the barbaric energy of the opening, with a conclusion that is typical of this period of Bartók's music – uncompromising and final.

### Piano Concerto No. 2 (1930-31)

By the time Bartók came to write his Second Piano Concerto, his style had matured and mellowed. The work is in three traditional movements: Allegro non troppo, Adagio, Allegro di bravura. The opening movement is characterised by brilliant piano writing and a strong rhythmic impulse. The slow movement is a beautiful example of Bartók's night music style, with evocative and atmospheric scoring. The finale is brilliant and witty, with a wonderful sense of rhythmic momentum. Bartók himself described the finale as "a kind of joyful race". The orchestration is more expansive than the First Concerto, with a larger string section and more balanced woodwinds and brass. The piano writing remains demanding, but is more varied and colouristic than the First.

## Page 3

### Piano Concerto No. 3 (1945)

Bartók wrote his Third Piano Concerto in 1945, during his final illness in New York, where he had emigrated in 1940. He completed the work just before he died, and it was orchestrated by his friend Zoltán Kósa from the detailed sketches Bartók left. The work is in three movements: Allegretto scherzando, Adagio religioso, Allegro vivace. It reflects a very different mood from the earlier concertos – there is a warmth and lyricism that is not always present in the earlier works, and the harmonies are generally more diatonic, with a glowing tonal orientation. The slow movement is particularly beautiful, with a chorale-like middle section that reflects Bartók's deep religious feeling, despite his general agnosticism. The finale is brilliant and cheerful, ending with a typically Bartókian explosion of rhythmic energy, but one that is more relaxed and humorous than the conclusion of the First Concerto. It is a fitting farewell from a great composer to his art.

### Jean-Efflam Bavouzet

Jean-Efflam Bavouzet was born in 1962 in Niort, France. He studied at the Paris Conservatoire with Pierre Sancan, Geneviève Joy-Dutilleux and, subsequently, with Vlado Perlemuter. He won First Prize at the Concours Géza Anda in Zurich in 1986, and has since gone on to enjoy an international career, appearing with many major orchestras and at major festivals throughout Europe. He has a particular affinity with twentieth-century French music and with Bartók. This is his first recording for Chandos.

### Péter Csaba

Péter Csaba was born in 1937 in Cluj, Romania (then Kolozsvár, Hungary). He studied conducting at the Franz Liszt Academy in Budapest with János Ferencsik. He has been Principal Conductor of the Budapest Symphony Orchestra since 1976, and has made many recordings for Hungaroton and other labels, focusing particularly on Hungarian music. He has toured throughout Europe and Japan.

### Budapest Symphony Orchestra

The Budapest Symphony Orchestra was founded in 1976, and has established itself as one of Hungary's leading orchestras under the direction of Péter Csaba. It has toured widely and made many recordings, concentrating on the Hungarian repertoire.

### Bartók and the Piano Concerto

Bartók's three piano concertos occupy a special place in the twentieth-century repertoire. They chart the composer's journey from the aggressive modernism of the 1920s to the more mellow, synthesized style of his final years, written in exile in America. Each concerto is a masterpiece in its own right, and together they represent one of the greatest contributions to the genre of the twentieth century. This recording presents all three concertos on a single CD, totaling 77 minutes 21 seconds.
