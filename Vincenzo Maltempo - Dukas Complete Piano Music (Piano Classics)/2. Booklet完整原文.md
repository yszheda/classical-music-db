Aside from *The Sorcerer's Apprentice*, his symphonic scherzo immortalized in 1940 by Walt Disney in *Fantasia*, Paul Dukas can hardly be considered a well-known composer. 'This man should be better known,' Gabriel Fauré once wrote to his wife. 'He is a "serious" type, gifted with a true philosophical spirit and a well-stocked mind. He is not an amiable character at first. But you will like him, even though his wildness is incurable.'

Dukas entered the Paris Conservatoire as a 16-year-old student in 1881. Over the course of the next eight years, he took piano lessons with George Mathias, harmony classes with Théodore Dubois and composition classes with Ernest Guiraud. As the intricate workings of the Sonata comprehensively demonstrate, Dukas received a thorough grounding in counterpoint as the fundamental driving force of musical argument. However, he was deeply discouraged by his repeatedly unsuccessful attempts to win the prestigious Prix de Rome (he was only awarded second prize in 1888).

Dukas's talent suffered throughout his career at the hands of his perfectionism: literally so, when one considers the number of his scores which found their eventual destiny not in a concert hall but in the fire. 'It is a family trait,' Dukas once confessed. 'I combine an anarchic spirit with a punctual and meticulous conscience.' He would pass on the fruits of that 'conscience' to many young students at the École Normale de Musique and later the Conservatoire, most renowned among them Olivier Messiaen.

We might think of the two protagonists of Goethe's ballad *Die Zauberlehrling* (the apprentice and the sorcerer) as embodying the contrasting facets of the composer's character, the 'serious type' and the 'anarchic spirit' - as well as dramatizing the teacher-pupil relationship.

and making a parable of the dangers of over-reach and ill-grasped supernatural forces (forming thus a comic pendant to *Faust*), Dukas's inner sorcerer was cursed with a rigorous self-criticism which allowed only 13 compositions to be published in his lifetime.

While Dukas was an enthusiastic supporter of Debussy and appreciated the new, rebellious strains in younger composers such as Stravinsky, his own music and teaching sprang from a veneration for Wagner and the classical tradition (Bach and Beethoven in particular). Amid the fevered artistic politics of the Belle Époque, Dukas promoted conservative figures such as Franck, Saint-Saëns and d'Indy. This Franco-German openness of spirit was decisively narrowed by the onset of World War I: 'The art of the Germans,' he wrote in March 1915, 'is the centuries-old curtain behind which they were preparing their coups. After Parsifal, which we all followed with enraptured eyes, the bombing of Reims, Soissons, Louvain...! I do not want to deal with that any longer!! And the Germany of Goethe and Schiller, that was nothing but a lie...'

Paradoxically enough, Dukas immersed himself in a study of Beethoven and a new edition of the complete piano works (published by Durand). His own well of creativity dried up almost completely, and he felt ill at ease in the 'harlequin years' of 1920s Paris. '[Poetry, dance, and music] are now but an echo from Beyond of sacred things that are dead. Art no longer presents to man a mirror of the future but rather a mirror of the past. [...] This is undoubtedly the role that future art will have to resign itself to, becoming the mirror of a past in which the future will lovingly treasure the most beautiful human images.'

Dukas died on 17 May 1935, and the non-religious obsequies included neither flowers nor speeches, according to his wishes, just some organ chorales of Bach. In his obituary for *The Musical Times*, Ravel's friend and fellow 'Apache' M-D Calvocoressi remarked that 'After d'Indy's death, Dukas, although not the eldest of survivors, had naturally and almost inevitably fallen into place as the senior, the "doyen" of the French school, as the only one worthy of this honorary rank... He belonged to no group. He kept thoroughly aloof not only from the surrounding turmoil and party tactics, but from all that might resemble publicity or self-revelation. He held that his person, private life, and views were nobody's concern but his own.'

As it comes down to us, his 'original' output for the piano includes just five works, dominated by the Sonata and the Variations, each of them a homage to a past master, to Beethoven and (more explicitly) to Rameau. The Sonata can be considered as a sort of French *Hammerklavier* for its colossal dimensions, its structural complexities and its tightrope virtuoso writing. However, unlike Alkan, Dukas never developed a super-virtuoso technique or an inclination to write for one. The considerable difficulties of the Sonata are concentrated instead on its motivic complexity, resulting in slightly awkward piano writing which prompted its dedicatee Saint-Saëns to consider making a more 'playable' transcription for two pianos.

Édouard Risler gave the premiere of the Sonata to generally positive reviews at the Salle Pleyel in Paris on 10 May 1901. Debussy's estimation is worth quoting: Dukas 'is master of his own emotions and knows how to avoid unnecessary outbursts... If you look at the third part of this Sonata, you'll find, beneath the apparently picturesque exterior, a powerful force that controls, almost imperceptibly, the rhythmic tension as if by a steel

spring. A similar force is to be found in the finale, which shows the art of controlling emotional content at its best. You could say that the emotions themselves become a structural force, for the piece evokes a beauty comparable to the perfect lines found in architecture – lines based on natural forms that blend so well with the open spaces of the sky that is all a perfect and total harmony.'

Fourteen years later, in 1914, Guy Ropartz chose to feature the Sonata as a competition piece at the Conservatoire in Nancy, which elicited a more psychologically insightful perspective, made with the benefit of hindsight, from the composer. Dukas discussed 'the analogy between victory over the beast within which I tried to put into music, and the other victory, the great one, the one that comes from every corner of the horizon, helping us trample underfoot the beast without, so real and almost as ugly (shall we say dirty), of which we had no inkling in those days' – remember that Europe is now mired in war. 'You may see it,' Dukas continued, 'as no more than a symbolic correspondence, one for musicians that only they can understand, going back to a time when our distant ancestors gave a moral meaning to their symphonic poems, describing the triumph of Apollo over the Pythian serpent! There's something of that here.'

In 1903, the Rameau Variations followed as a Diabelli-like pendant to his French 'Hammerklavier'. After 11 variations on Rameau's unpretentious minuet, transforming the theme beyond all recognition, a rhapsodic interlude introduces the long variation finale. Despite the 'neoclassical' character imparted by the Baroque elements in some variations and the interlude, the set as a whole sounds more in tune with the temper of the time than its immediate 'neoromantic' predecessor.

Curiously enough, Debussy was among the few critics who did not welcome the Rameau Variations, premiered in Brussels by Blanche Selva in March 1903. As pupils of Guiraud, Debussy and Dukas had met for the first time in 1885, after Debussy had just won the Prix de Rome. In 1893 their paths diverged, but their old friendship returned a few years later and lasted until Debussy's death in 1918.

Of all the composers asked by *La revue musicale* to write a piece in memory of Debussy, Dukas was the one who had known him longest and most intimately. A special commemorative issue of *La Revue musicale* appeared in October 1920 and included works by Ravel, Roussel, Schmitt, Satie, de Falla, Stravinsky, Bartók, Goosens, Malipiero, and a piece by Dukas, *La plainte, au loin, du Faune...* Surrounded by the tolling of a funereal bell, Debussy's faun plays a plaintive chromatic motif over and over until it unfolds in an open quotation of the *Prélude à l'après-midi*. But the theme has lost its sensuous allure: it sounds like a desolate memory.

In 1909, the first centennial of Haydn's death, the director of *La Revue S.I.M.* Jules Écorcheville, had likewise invited contemporary French composers to write a commemorative piece based on Haydn's name (corresponding to the notes B-A-D-G). Six composers, Dukas, Debussy, Ravel, d'Indy, Hahn, and Widor, accepted his invitation. Aside from Ravel's *Menuet sur le nom de Haydn*, the other works are rarely heard in concert. The *Prélude élégiaque* of Dukas is based on thematic juxtapositions that freely flow and eventually dissolve.

### VINCENZO MALTEMPO

Born in 1985, the Italian pianist Vincenzo Maltempo has been praised as a 'thrillingly resourceful musician with formidable technique and intelligence' (Jeremy Lee), with a sound 'carved in marble' (PianoNews), and 'phrasing so elegant, without ostentatious virtuosity, total mastery of form and musical sense' (L'Osservatore Romano)

He is counted among the most authoritative modern exponents of the music of Alkan. His recordings on Piano Classics have attracted glowing reviews in magazines such as *Diapason*, *The Guardian*, *PianoNews*, *Gramophone* and others. Andrew Clements (*The Guardian*): 'Exhilarating, demonic, a real revelation!' Robert Nemececk (PianoNews) 'one of the greatest contemporary interpreters of this composer [Alkan], no other pianist as Maltempo has managed to dominate in a so well thought-out and orchestral way the enormous difficulties [of Alkan's works]'. Having both performed and recorded Alkan's most important works, Maltempo is one of very few pianists to play all 12 of the notoriously demanding *Etudes dans toutes les tons mineurs* Op.39 in a single recital (Yokohama, November 2013). He has been awarded honorary membership of the Alkan Society in London.

Having begun to study the piano at an early age, Vincenzo Maltempo experienced a formative influence from the pianist and teacher Salvatore Orlando, himself a pupil of Sergio Fiorentino, and continued his studies with Riccardo Risaliti at the International Piano Academy 'Incontri col Maestro' in Imola. His performing career has included concerts in prestigious venues and piano festivals throughout Europe, America and

Asia, including La Fenice in Venice, the Festival of Two Worlds in Spoleto, the Rarities of Piano Music in Husum and the Miami International Piano Festival.

The pianist Francesco Libetta praises Maltempo as 'a glorious example among Mediterranean pianists' and Alexander Lonquich writes about him as 'a pianist/musician out of the ordinary who is able to use his natural virtuosity in the quest to uncover a true understanding of 19th-century music [...] He is also one of the few who can do justice to Alkan's music [...] for him the instrument is a means and not an end for itself'.

In *Gramophone*, Bryce Morrison compared his flamboyant and colourful playing to Vladimir Horowitz, and the critic Lorenzo Arruga acclaims his 'free and speaking phrasing, his warm touch and his loving attitude for the composers and the music.'

Besides the works of Alkan, Vincenzo Maltempo's discography embraces Liszt (including a set of the Hungarian Rhapsodies awarded 'Editor's Choice' in the February 2017 issue of *Gramophone*), Schumann (Piano Classics 2014), Michele Esposito (violin sonatas, recorded with Carmelo Andriani for Brilliant Classics, 2014) and Lyapunov, with the 12 *Études d'exécution transcendante*, Op.11 (Piano Classics, 2017). He lives in Turin and teaches at the Conservatory "A. Vivaldi" in Alessandria.
