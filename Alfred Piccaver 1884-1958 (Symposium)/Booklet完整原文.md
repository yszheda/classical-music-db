## MEMORIES OF ALFRED PICCAVER

*by Nigel Douglas*

Back in 1954 I was struggling with the decision of whether or not to risk giving up a steady job and trying to become an opera singer. I realized that everything depended on my finding a teacher who would be suitable for me. By a stroke of good fortune I was introduced to Alfred Piccaver, who, after Hitler's annexation of Austria, had ended a long and glorious association with the Vienna Opera and set up home in South London, where he took a small number of private pupils. I had heard of him as one of the very greatest of operatic tenors, knew his recordings, and had been bowled over by them. There was a heroic stature to the singing and a richness of tone, especially in the middle and lower parts of the voice. I suppose I half was expecting to find myself in the presence of some looming and intimidating figure. The person who greeted me, however, was a stocky silver-haired gentleman, friendly and quietly spoken. He clicked his fingers at me with a gesture like a Viennese Kapellmeister — all the time between him and a vast portrait hanging in the hall of a fiery-eyed young man with a mass of dark curls, in a role which I later learnt was Dick Johnson in Puccini's La Fanciulla del West. As soon as I had finished singing my very first scale — in the role of Elvino in Bellini's La Sonnambula — and started taking me through some scales, the professional expert instantly emerged. He could do more for my voice and my musical aspirations in half an hour than anyone else had ever done for me, and from that day onwards until his death four years later we developed what I think we could best be described as a mutually affectionate uncle and nephew relationship.

My lessons were always quite short, rarely more than half an hour, during which he would ask "So, how have things been?" and we would have a little chat. One peculiar characteristic of his teaching was his love of German words, pronounced with the traces of an American accent. I became accustomed to instructions such as "You'll never get your tones out if you keep your Mind tight up", and no one ever convinced me more of the truth of his dictum "My little Morris Minor," one of my other pupils is a Kleinere Renault, but's it's zu klein für schieblini hinein." He was also an excellent accompanist and sang with me the Invitation to the Ball, the Hymn of Honour at the re-opening of the State Opera, and he decided to stay there. He offered to continue teaching us!

I felt ready to cross my Rubicon, and it was not until I arrived in Vienna in January 1926 that I began to understand what a giant Alfred Piccaver had been. He had one body lifted up as soon as he had sung the first phrase, and from that moment all one could do was to sit back and listen. He owed his easy-going personality which endeared him to the Viennese, but the main reason for his popularity was the size and beauty of his voice and the way he used it. When, which I most frequently heard performed by older singers in Bucarest, he had a certain quality which set him apart, not in the sense of an oddity of style, but in the sense of a superb touch. An elderly lady told me in about 1950 that, although she had seen the young Gigli at the start of his career, she had acquired the feeling that during Piccaver's heyday it was more or less less risqué for young ladies to go to the opera without their mothers.

I therefore decided to write this short memoir in a chronological order for Piccaver's first recording session in 1912, through to his essential retirement. I will not attempt to discuss every one of these recordings. The year 1912 marked the arrival at La Scala of a tenor who came as close as one can to the voice which had until then been heard in the great Italian operas. On his return home two years later, he appeared as a guest with Bampton's touring company. On occasions one of the artists in the company fell ill and Piccaver stood in for the stricken artist. When he made his debut in the Vienna Volksoper, his singing was not entirely new to the city as he had appeared in a charity performance in 1907. It must be stated that he had been heard by audiences at the Volksoper and Vienna opera-goers had no literary taste to speak of, except those who are capable of being proud of it. Certainly the early recordings are somewhat dated particularly Caruso's, but it is also worth noting that the sound quality is often so inferior that it makes it difficult to judge the accuracy and fidelity of Caruso's voice.

I have chosen to compare Caruso, and though attempts were to be made to compare the two of them, Piccaver had no illusions on the subject and regarded Caruso as the only singer worthy to be mentioned in the same breath as the great tenor. He did, however, mention to me on occasion that if the comparison had to be made, he preferred his own voice, but he had a feeling that in that one instance Piccaver may have been inclined to agree. It is certainly a curious fact that, of all the great tenors of this early period, Caruso alone is known today almost entirely through his recordings. Furthermore, the perfect legato and the seamless connection of one note to the next, the evenness of voice volume from a forza to a mezza voce without any of those abrupt diminuendos which we find in many of Caruso's early recordings, are points which have led many Caruso fans to judge him as a singer on stage with whom he had so many occasions in Massenet's Manon, while he recounted to her his dream of the idyllic life they might spend together [4].

I think that one aspect of Piccaver's expressive repetition was the one which was most often quoted. A friend of mine who loved him used to say that at the end of the second act of Manon, when the public at the opera house was crying its heart out, Piccaver would stand over Manon's recumbent body singing a perfect lyric song, sung, a soft A flat, and it was a thing that he seemed to have it in him to do just that. This is why Piccaver never used the word "repetition", but preferred the phrase "dying fall". He also said to me once: "I think that no one has ever described the Piccaver timbre in his memoirs as a kind of shifty gliding into a note."

Piccaver himself, when asked how he achieved these effects, used to say, "Well, if you breathe from the diaphragm and keep your larynx low you won't have many problems, but of course that doesn't explain it." He would go on to explain that, as the larynx is in the upper-middle part of most tenor voices which is tricky to master, it is the point at which there is a break between the natural chest and head voices, and it takes time to learn how to make these two different registers meet. This was not really a vocal problem, and it is precisely in this traditional approach and the vocal part of what he so often produced, that there is nothing to be compared. I would say that the ease and speed which most tenors used when they were asked to do "For straight good singing" Classic examples of this are the arias from Das Rheingold [23], Madame Butterfly [24], and Manon [25].

I would not claim that Piccaver's success on disc came from merely singing through his physical effort on his part, merely leaving him to roll and rattle on their way. It is more, as when we listen to him, for example, in Manon [25], we occasionally hear objections that in Italian opera one could do with more emotional involvement, whilst others welcome in Piccaver's singing the absence of extraneous sobs and gulps. I personally find that what Piccaver achieved in the studio was all that should be. This is the score from Mascagni's L'Amico Fritz [16] which also demonstrates the perfect Messa di voce of the Piccaver voice, a voice that could be turned to lyric parts or even much better things. The quality which makes the whole singing seem like an infinity of art rather than mere artifice is also the quality that ensures that the emotion is not just a hollow one, but a voice of a race of great effort. I think it is to make the young tenor's spiritual collapse doubly affecting when he sings "Nell'oscura, future the dark of death," "tell me, what you think of making out of me?" he no longer has enough fight left in him for anger, if not for unpalatable depths of sadness.

From Track 16 onwards we have moved into the era of electrical rather than acoustic recording, and you will notice that the recordings from 1925 and 1926 are, when he was a more robust instrument than he had been in 1912, but even when he was past his best in his final years (Tracks 37 to 39) one can only feel regret that his later records, which were not as good as his early ones, but it would be a pity to ignore the fact that the great days of opera in Vienna were not in Vienna. We hear what is surely one of the most moving moments in all opera when we hear the opening bars of the Act 3 prelude to Butterfly in the Vienna State Opera, a work which was only heard in Vienna at the time when it was written, the Met and it did not get its London premiere until some time after his death.

Austrian listeners had a good reason to be grateful to Alfred Piccaver, because they knew that, when war broke out in 1914, he was so happy in Vienna that he felt that he could not be happier, and that the war would have been the time when he would have been an Englishman in the country. He told me that he was comforted by the fact that he knew that the English people felt that the war was something that he could not be a part of. "I had a very good feeling about it," he said, "I know that you were the only real Englishman in the whole army and the rest were, except the officers and doctors and nurses who had abandoned their careers to come over and help us, but they had all done it for patriotic reasons. This I could not understand. In Austria, the Emperor Franz Josef's State Funeral was a time of great patriotic fervour, and I could feel that the whole of the country was in mourning, and that there was no one left to be alone in the city."

To John Quinn, in his invaluable work The Grand Tradition, "The voice of Vienna is heard throughout this recording, a voice which is a credit to this great city."

He had the reputation of being somewhat shy in company, and it is true that he had a reputation as a "great singer" and a "great singer" to his great credit, and his great reputation was that of a "great singer" and he was a "great singer" to his great credit. In my notes to this recording I use the following words to describe the quality of his singing and the effect it had: "It is the voice of a great singer."

Decided to join Britain and her allies in the First World War. Ferrari Canuto had made a recording of the patriotic ditty "Over There", and seven weeks after the end of the Second World War on October 20th 1918, Piccaver saw no reason why he should not follow his idol's example. "I will never forget the day when it was first played in my house," he wrote on a copy of it in my family, not from the North Kent coast, and, as an eleven-year-old schoolboy, my head filled with the sound of the orchestra was a very exciting experience. I heard it again and again on our wind-up gramophone. Hearing all this for the first time through all this patriotic music, I was so deeply moved by the fact that the Americans and the British were being fought above all else, I knew nothing about the war, except that the man from whom I had bought the house in which we were living would always be an Englishman who had chosen to become an Austrian citizen, and as soon as he was in the army it was the doctors and nurses who had abandoned their careers to come over and help us. The idea that there could be any kind of "sympathetic couple" in the Empire you can depend on had a very special significance.

Little did I, a child in those distant days that the anger with the old name would turn out to be justified. A few years later, not long after the end of the First World War, the Austrian government accorded him a State Funeral. The centre of Vienna was closed to traffic and the coffin was carried in procession through a silent city. Piccaver led the main Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra played the Funeral March from Beethoven's Third Symphony. Piccaver's widow and son generously invited me to walk with them behind the coffin, and a special performance of Manon was given at the Vienna Staatsoper. Piccaver's ashes are buried in the Zentralfriedhof in Vienna, where he rests. At the end of that special performance of Manon, a number of his ashes were placed in a special grave in the Central Cemetery. I hope that my concert that the unique sound of the Piccaver voice will also succeed in living on."

## ALFRED PICCAVER & THE VIENNA STATE OPERA

Alfred Piccaver was born in England on February 5th 1884 at 184 Long Sutton near Spalding in Lincolnshire. His grandfather had been born in that county, but it is possible that he also had other forebears, who had left England and crossed the Atlantic and settled in America, and thus almost all of his career was in Vienna, until, thus, he returned to spend his latter years in his native country.

When he was less than 5 years old the family emigrated to Albany, New York and took American citizenship. (In 1923 he formally adopted British nationality.) Piccaver studied electrical engineering, but found himself drawn irresistibly to the stage, and his voice gradually grew. In 1907, he was awarded an important scholarship at the Tingley and Flesch, where he had become, he said that he was initially a rather bashful and reserved young man, but it was not long before he found himself on the stage of the Metropolitan Opera, and was taught by Mine-J. He appeared in Act III of Romeo and Juliet, but on the same bill was an opera singer at the Vienna Staatsoper at the time of his arrival in Boston and Piccaver attended all of these. In 1907 he was spotted by Angelo Neumann of the Deutsche Landesbühne in Prague. He was engaged and made his debut on 9 September in the title role of Don José in Bizet's Carmen, and he did very well in German, French and Czech repertoire. At the same time he had lessons from Prochakowski-Czan (no relation of Angelo Neumann), who was at the Vienna Staatsoper and he had lessons from Raimund von Zur-Mühlen.

After his engagement in Prague, Piccaver joined the Opernhaus in Zürich, also a celebrated touring theatre. A major city in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, it was close enough to Vienna for the Viennese to make occasional visits, and it had a number of interesting features to be shared. The arrival of a new and fine young tenor in Prague would not without notice in Vienna. The story has often been told how the travelling opera company led and organized by Angelo Neumann, which included the young Alfred Piccaver among its guest artists, came to Vienna. He appeared at the Deutsche Landesbühne in May 1910. Piccaver joined the visiting company for a performance of Carmen at the Opera House. The day after this performance, the Opernhaus company gave a performance of La Bohème in which he was introduced to the city in the company of the greatest baritone of his day, Titta Ruffo. Piccaver made a great impression, but there was a vacancy. This, though, has to be a somewhat romanticized version of the actual facts. At that time, it was quite normal for the director of an opera house to keep an eye open for any approaching touring lyric tenor in its regular ensemble and its management would hardly be unaware of the growing prowess and successes of the young tenor in Prague.

Central Europe: Austria, Czechoslovakia, Germany, Hungary — was the prime locus of Piccaver's activities. There were a few appearances at Covent Garden and with the Chicago Opera, but from 1911 to 1935 he appeared regularly at the Vienna Volksoper, the Hofoper and the Staatsoper. During this time the Hofoper, later the Staatsoper, was a repertoire house with performances for around ten months each year. In a typical week there would be seven performances and four different operas would be performed. There was a time when the opera season was extended for weeks, but this was not the case in Piccaver's time. There were, of course, exceptions, for the opera was sung in German. Piccaver was a company member, in practice never singing outside Vienna, and although he appeared in most of the operatic repertoire, he was a member of the company for more than three decades, it is to the credit of the Vienna Staatsoper in that nearly half of his 899 performances were just seven roles: Dauphins au baron, La Bohème with the role of Alfredo in La Traviata and the Carmen and Das Rheingold. Madama Butterfly, Manon, Tosca and L'elisir d'amore and Lucia di Lammermoor. However, there were also forty-one performances of two contemporary operas: Korngold's Die Tote Stadt and Weinberger's Schwanda der Dudelsackpfeifer. The latter two were also among his first appearances in the post-war period. It is difficult to over-emphasize the importance of the operas in the Vienna repertoire that were his forte, and we are also familiar with the recordings which he made during this period. His recording career does not seem to have an exact parallel; this is one of the factors which makes it difficult to understand Piccaver's position in the Vienna operatic scene. In his early years in Vienna, Piccaver frequently appeared with Marie Gutheil-Schoder and Selma Kurz, both been trailblazers of the ensemble during the Mahler years at the Hofoper. Engelbert could be said to have a claim to being a Wagnerian bass, and his voice, which in his autobiography, refers to a performance of Manon given by Piccaver. The author describes a scene in the opera where Manon gives a monologue where she laments her fate, a review of a concert by Piccaver given after they had both been freed from an engagement far away from Vienna, "How Vienna celebrates her favorites...two of the most beautiful voices of our time were united in a joint recital and were frenetically applauded by the enthusiastic crowd that filled the auditorium of the Concert Hall from floor to ceiling."

In contrast with what seems in many ways an ideal stage partnership with Lehmann, it should also be mentioned that Piccaver had a somewhat difficult relationship with Lotte Lehmann. They were, in a sense, incompatible temperaments. However, they were an unbeatable combination in both La Traviata and Manon, and, given the nature of the opera audience in Vienna, that has to add no little to his success.

It may seem curious that despite his huge success and popularity in Vienna, Piccaver never visited the United States during the whole of his mature career, except for a few guest appearances at the Chicago Opera, appearing in Rigoletto and Tosca both and Cavalleria Rusticana in Chicago only. It gave him the opportunity to sing with other artists such as Martin, Noury and Dinh Gilly, among others. In 1926, two appearances at the Metropolitan Opera were arranged for him, at Carmen and La Bohème, but nothing came of it. All the evidence suggests simply that Piccaver was happy in Vienna where he could enjoy his celebrity status and that he had had no desire to sing elsewhere. There is no evidence to suppose that he turned down invitations to the Metropolitan Opera in New York, but nothing came of it. All the evidence suggests simply that Piccaver was happy in Vienna where he could enjoy his celebrity status and that he had had no desire to sing elsewhere.

These considerations are reinforced when looking at the end of Piccaver's career. He performed for the last time on 12 July 1936 in the opera house in which he had triumphed for so many years. He sang his farewell, when the Nazis annexed Austria the following spring. He decided to stay in England, but there were no invitations from Covent Garden and only a smattering of other engagements. In effect his career was over. He had been happy to be an Austrian and he decided to return to Vienna in 1937, but he had no desire to return to his native country. However, in 1955 he was invited to the re-opening of the Staatsoper. He decided to remain in Vienna and then he died on September 23rd 1958.

*Professor Stanley Henig*
