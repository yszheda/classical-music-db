### MEMORIES OF ALFRED PICCAVER

by Nigel Douglas

Back in 1954 I was struggling with the decision of whether or not to risk giving up a steady job and trying to become an opera singer. I realised that everything depended on my finding a teacher who would be willing and able to give me reliable advice and a happy coincidence led me to Alfred Piccaver, who, after Hitler's annexation of Austria, had ended a long and glorious association with the Vienna Opera and set up home in South London, where he took a small number of pupils. Before presenting myself there for the first of many visits I had already bought a couple of his recordings, and I had been bowled over by them. There was a heroic stature to the singing and a richness and individuality of timbre quite unlike any other that I had heard, and on that first visit I was expecting to find myself in the presence of some charismatic superman. The person who greeted me, however, was a stocky silver-haired gentleman, friendly and quietly spoken. He chatted to me about his garden and about Butch, his boxer dog, and I could see no connection at all between him and a vast portrait hanging in the hall of a fiery-eyed young man with a mass of dark raven curls, in a role which I later learnt was Dick Johnson in Puccini's La Fanciulla del West.

As soon as he sat down at the piano, however – a Bechstein grand, autographed under the lid by Enrico Caruso – and started taking me through some scales, the professional expert instantly emerged. He declared himself happy to take me on as a pupil, and I immediately felt that this was the right advice for me, and from that day onwards until his death four years later we developed what I think could best be described as a mutually affectionate uncle and nephew relationship.

My lessons would last for an hour, with a brief break in the middle, during which he would ask "So, how have things been?" and we would have a little chat. One peculiar characteristic of his was that he virtually spoke a language all of his own, a higgledy-piggledy mixture of English and German words, pronounced with the traces of an American accent. I became accustomed to instructions such as "Und I vant to get your voices out to the people in der leckest (leckest = gallery)", and on one occasion he came out with the memorable utterance, while looking approvingly at my elderly Morris Minor, "one of my other pupils has a kleiner Renault, but it's zu klein for sochkleppen hinen" – by which he meant that it was too small for an elderly gentleman to slide into with comfort. When I had been with him for a little over a year he was invited back to Vienna as a Guest of Honour at the re-opening of the State Opera, and he decided to stay there. He offered to continue teaching me if I felt ready to cross my Rubicon, and it was not until I arrived in Vienna in January 1958 that I really began to understand what a giant Alfred Piccaver had been. One old lady summed it up when she said to me "Nobody in Britain could possibly understand how the public felt about Piccaver. They didn't just love him and admire him – they adored him". There was clearly something about his singing which went far beyond mere technical excellence, and the quality I think most frequently heard praised by older opera-goers was his "Wärkchait", or softness – not in the sense of softness as opposed to loud, but in the sense of soft to the touch, as another old lady put it "His voice was like a great velvet glove. He was one of the greatest artists of the century". I gradually acquired the feeling that during Piccaver's heyday it was more or less de rigueur for young ladies to sleep with his picture beneath their pillows.

I have tried as far as possible to set out in chronological order Piccaver's first recording session in 1912, through to their eventual retirement. I will not attempt to discuss every one of these performances individually, but in opening tracks from Rigoletto to what we are hearing it is worth making a few general observations about Piccaver's Vienna years. In 1907 or 1908, two years earlier, when he appeared there as a guest with Battistini's touring company. On this occasion all of the critics expressed the opinion he "stands head and shoulders above all the German colleagues, and the Vienna Opera has no lyric tenor in its disposal today who is capable of singing like this".

Certainty the two arts are despatched with splendidly carefree bravura, and it is also worth noting that Piccaver has no illusions on the subject, and regarded Battistini as the only singer worthy to be mentioned in the same breath as the great tenor. It may seem curious that despite his huge success and popularity in Vienna, Piccaver never really had much of an international career. During 1923 and 1924 he performed at Covent Garden and the Chicago Opera, appearing in Rigoletto and Tosca in both at Covent Garden and Cavalleria Rusticana in Chicago only. It gave him the opportunity to sing with other artists such as Muzio, Norena and Raisa. He seems to have made little impact in Chicago, but received good reviews in London. However, there is no evidence of any repeat invitation from Covent Garden even when he was living in London during the 1938 and 1939 seasons. At some point he seems to have been offered a contract by the Metropolitan Opera in New York, but nothing came of it. All the evidence suggests simply that Piccaver was happier in Vienna where he could enjoy his celebrity status and that he had little desire to tour the world.

These considerations are reinforced when looking at the end of Piccaver's career. He performed extensively in Vienna during the first part of 1937, but was in London when Germany annexed Austria the following spring. He decided to stay in England, but there were few invitations from Covent Garden and only a smattering of other engagements. In effect his career was over. The greatest of English born lyric tenors had returned to spend what became his retirement years in his native country. However, in 1955 he was invited to the re-opening of the Staatsoper. He decided to remain in Vienna and then he died on September 23rd 1958.

## 内页 4 (HIPPO_20241230_0006.jpg)

To quote John Steane, in his definitive work The Grand Tradition, "Taken slowly, the phrases beautifully moulded, the tone varied, gentle in the right places, yet fervent too – it has all the ease which the singer of his generation could command." The diminuendos at "pace deus" and "stem deisti" are observed and are exceedingly beautiful. All this is the work of a genuinely distinguished singer.

To move from the consideration of such exalted music to the humbler realm of the drawing-room ballad may seem somewhat abrupt, but no apology is required for the two examples of the gems in our current selection. I may perhaps be prejudiced about "Pale Hands I Loved So Well" [18], as it was the first Piccaver disc which I owned, but I am in total agreement with the late Alan Blyth, who described it as "a million miles away from the drawing-room ballad. The voice is a wonderful example of the real Piccaver timbre, call it velvety, call it what you will, the second ballad, "I Hear You Calling Me" [22], is by no means as simple to sing as Piccaver makes it sound, and he passes the major poser presented by the climactic phrase with all the "break in the voice" to which I have referred above, and somehow I do not think that many of the great singers of this number in those Edwardian drawing-rooms would have dreamt how evocative the final "pianissimo" high A can be made to sound if you happen to possess the voice and the technique required.

And so to Track 23, which may come as a surprise to anyone who connects the name of Alfred Piccaver exclusively with composers of classic stature. However, when the United States decided to join Britain and its allies in the First World War, Enrico Caruso had made a recording of the patriotic ditty "Over There" and seven weeks after the outbreak of the Second World War, on October 27th 1939, Piccaver saw no reason why he should not follow his idol's example. I will candidly admit that I find it difficult to listen to this recording without a tear in my eye. I had a copy of it in my family home, not far from the North Kent coast, and as an eleven-year old schoolboy, I used to play it endlessly on our wind-up gramophone. Hearing it again after all these years carries me straight back to the summer of 1940, with our troops straggling home from Dunkirk and the Battle of Britain being fought above our heads. I knew nothing about the singer, except that he had a rather odd name, but I drew comfort from being so confidently assured by him that there would always be an England. Moreover, as we had the New Zealand Medical Corps stationed all around us, including doctors and surgeons who had abandoned their careers to come over and help us in our hour of need, even the somewhat simplistic couplet "The Empire too, we can depend on you" held a very specific significance.

Well, little did I know in those distant days that the singer with the odd name would turn out to be someone who changed the whole course of my life. When he died on September 23rd 1958 the whole of Vienna went into mourning. The streets were lined with people from dawn onwards, the funeral procession halted in front of the Opera House, while from the balcony above the main entrance the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra played the Funeral March from Beethoven's "Eroica" Symphony. Piccaver's widow was so generous as to invite me to walk with them behind the coffin, and what this was an honour for an Englishman who had not sung a note in Vienna for twenty years, and whose death would have passed virtually unnoticed had he died in his native land. In Vienna his ashes are accorded a special place of honour in the Central Cemetery. I hope that our current selection of his recordings will play its part in ensuring that the unique sound of the Piccaver voice will also succeed in living on.

Professor Stanley Henig

### ALFRED PICCAVER & THE VIENNA STATE OPERA

Alfred Piccaver was born in England on February 15th 1884 in Long Sutton near Spalding in Lincolnshire. His grandfather owned an inn in that county, but it is possible that he also had Spanish antecedents. However, his boyhood and youth were passed in the United States of America and almost his entire career was in Vienna. Until retirement he spent little time in this native country.

When he was 1½ years old the family emigrated to Albany, New York and took American citizenship. (In 1923 he formally adopted British nationality.) Piccaver studied electrical engineering at the University of Albany, but by the time he was twenty he had decided to make singing his career. From about 1902 he sang various songs and shorter works at public concerts. It was that singing was initially a hobby rather than an intended vocation. Then, in 1905, he won a scholarship to the Metropolitan School of Opera run by Heinrich Conried, Director of the Metropolitan Opera in New York. In 1905 he also went to Milan to study with the Antonio Bernucci. He made a complete performance of "Der Freischütz". Miss Jaeger also ran a summer school at her home in Austria and Piccaver attended there in 1907. Whilst there he was heard by Angelo Neumann, Director of the Deutsche Landestheater in Prague and made his debut on 9 September 1907 as Faust in "Die Lustige Weibe von Windsor". There he learned his trade and developed his repertoire. At the same time he had lessons from Prochaska-Nezamunn (no relation of Angelo Neumann) in Prague. Later he also had lessons from Rosario in Milan.

It was while he was singing in Prague that he received the offer of a permanent ensemble, but also attracting visiting stars. A major city in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, it was close enough to the capital Vienna – home to what was then perhaps the world's finest operatic ensemble – for artists from the Vienna Opera to travel to Prague to make guest appearances, or to try their luck in the capital. The story has often been told how the travelling opera company organized and organized by the baritone Mattia Battistini was looking a tenor who arrived in Prague by performance at the Deutsches Landestheater in May 1910. Piccaver joined the touring company for a performance of "Die Lustige Weibe" and was immediately engaged and made his debut on 23 September was introduced to the city in the company of the greatest baritone of the age and a moment when there was a tenor vacancy. This has, though, to be a somewhat romanticized version of the actual facts. What seems clear is that Piccaver made a big impression on the Vienna public. It was a ranking lyric tenor of his day in its regular ensemble and its management would hardly have been unaware of the time were united in a joint recital and were frantically applauded by the enthusiastic crowd that filled the auditorium of the Concert House up to its ceiling".

In contrast with what seems in many ways an ideal stage partnership with Lehmann, Piccaver's relationship with Maria Jeritza brought together two very different and almost incompatible temperaments. However, they were an unbeatable combination in both "La Fanciulla del West" and "Tosca", and, given the nature of the opera audience in Vienna, their clashes no doubt added to box office appeal.
