# Rachmaninoff's orchestral works

This 2-CD set contains some of the lesser-known distinct Russian pieces by Rachmaninoff starting with the Scherzo, Rachmaninoff's first orchestral work, and finishing with his last, the Symphonic Dances. Also included is the overwhelming choral symphony 'The Bells'.

The Scherzo (1887 or 1888) was written while Rachmaninoff was still a student. It is his first surviving orchestral work which has a rather light-hearted tone and not yet the typical Rachmaninoff sound. It was originally intended to become a movement of a symphony, but both Arensky and Taneyev, his teachers in counterpoint and harmony respectively, did not like the Scherzo. So it remained a single piece.

The next pieces for orchestra, *Prince Rostislav* (1891) and *The Rock* (1893) begin to show the composer's skill for writing program music and incorporating tone painting which would culminate in *The Isle of the Dead* (1909). *Prince Rostislav* is based on the same ballad by Tolstoy which inspired Borodin for his opera *Prince Igor*. When crossing the river Stugna after being defeated by the Polovtsians in 1903 the Grand-Prince of Kiev and his brother Rostislav are drowned. The music already shows Rachmaninoff as an excellent melodist, but the piece also draws heavily on Tchaikovsky (*The Tempest*) and to a lesser extent on Rimsky-Korsakov (*Sadko*). The premieres of both the Scherzo and *Prince Rostislav* took place as late as 1945, when the USSR State Symphony Orchestra played them in Moscow.

His next piece for orchestra, *The Rock*, opus 7, once again has a program, an extra-musical subject, and was inspired by no less than two literary sources. Lermontov's poem *The Rock* and a short story by Chekhov, *On the Road* which in turn had been inspired by Lermontov. It is a the story of a young woman and an older man who meet at an inn on Christmas Eve. Tchaikovsky liked the composition and was to conduct it soon after its completion. But his death prevented him from doing so. Eventually it was premiered in April 1894 under Vassily Safonov.

In the same year Rachmaninoff also completed a Capriccio on gypsy themes, *Caprice bohémien*, opus 12. This was dedicated to Pyotr, gypsy singer Anna Lodizhenskaya's husband. It is a vibrant piece with rhythmic vitality and ingenious harmonics, pre-echoing the composer's later development in this field.

From the mid 1890's, more or less starting with his *First symphony*, Rachmaninoff's work began more and more to have an individual style and tone. Not yet a master piece this symphony shows Rachmaninoff's strong, expressive and lyrical writing. He wrote his most famous symphonic poem *The Isle of the Dead*, opus 29 in 1907 while living in Dresden. It was directly inspired by Arnold Böcklin's painting 'Toteninsel'. The music is Rachmaninoff all over: mysterious, beautifully and most effectively orchestrated, melodious and sombre. It all ends with the *Dies Irae*.

## Page 3 (scans/HIPPO_20230521_0003.jpg)

Rachmaninoff composed both his mature operas *Francesca da Rimini* and *The miserly Knight*, his second and third piano concertos and his second symphony before he started working on *The Bells*. It is often said that this four-part choral symphony was a favourite of the composer Rachmaninoff. He was truly fascinated by the sound of bells and incorporated it in his music more than once, for instance in the finale of his *First Suite for two pianos* (1893) and in the first Prelude for piano *'The bells of Moscow*. And apparently the poem he based his texts on moved him deeply in 1912 the words had been sent to him anonymously. They are a translation or rather interpretation or even reworking by the Russian symbolist poet Konstantin Balmont (1867-1942) of a poem originally by Edgar Allan Poe (1849). Balmont's version disregards some of the main characteristics of Poe's piece. For instance it lacks the rather echo-like repetitions (bells, bells, bells, bells) and does not use the same metre. So Rachmaninoff's piece is an impression based on Balmont's 'version' rather than a true setting of the original text.

To make matters even more complicated, *The Bells* is often performed in English. Since Poe's original words do not fit Rachmaninoff's music most often the translation by Fanny S. Copeland is used: a translation in English of a German translation (!) of Balmont's text. The performance on this record is sung in (Balmont's) Russian.

*The Bells* depict four aspects in life: birth, marriage, terror and death. But in fact a threat of death looms over the entire work. It is written for orchestra, chorus and three vocal soloists: soprano, tenor and bass. These solo singers however do never sing together or even appear in the same movement.

The first movement, subtitled *The Silver Sleigh Bells*, is a joyful piece with a solo tenor. The chorus, partly humming, mainly serves as an accompaniment. In the text the sleigh bells are seen as a symbol of new life and dreams, but also of 'oblivion' to come.

## Page 4 (scans/HIPPO_20230521_0004.jpg)

The slow, second movement has a soprano solo part and is called *The Mellow Wedding Bells*. The text (and the music) focuses on the more tender aspects of marriage. This is a rather intimate and romantic movement with a sensuous soprano and just one outburst of emotion. The golden wedding bells are seen as representing harmony.

The third movement, *The Loud Alarm Bells*, depicts the terror and danger caused by an all consuming fire. This time the bells are brazen. The panic-stricken chorus is almost shouting, the well-known *Dies Irae*-melody surfaces.

The fourth and final movement is subtitled *The Mournful Iron Bells*. This Lento lugubre is mournful, gloomy and lugubrious indeed: funeral bells sound. There is also a free quotation from Tchaikovsky's final and equally sombre work, his sixth symphony. In this part of *The Bells* the sound of the death knoll is heard continuously, going back and forth from C sharp (becoming D flat near the end) to G sharp.

Rachmaninoff wrote his last orchestral work, in fact his last work, the *Symphonic Dances*, opus 45, in 1940. It was premiered by and dedicated to Eugene Ormandy and the Philharmonic Orchestra. The work was originally named *Fantastic Dances* and scored for two pianos, but today is more often played in its orchestral version. This 'three-movement' symphony may have been intended to be choreographed, but Michel Fokine with whom Rachmaninoff had previously collaborated on the story of *Paganini* – on the *Variations on a theme by Paganini* – died in 1942 and the composer never saw this music being used on the stage.

The *Symphonic Dances* (once again) feature the *Dies Irae* theme and several other religious quotations. From the first movement of his first symphony, based on motifs for Russian church music; and from his *All-night Vigil* ('Blessed be the Lord'). Since the first symphony's premiere had been a failure and the work had not been published yet very few listeners will have recognized the quotation. The composer unusually chose an alto saxophone as a solo instrument. The Dances are a combination of the composer's typical lyrical melodies and almost Prokofiev-like energetic rhythmic sections.

Pesko Frank, 2007
