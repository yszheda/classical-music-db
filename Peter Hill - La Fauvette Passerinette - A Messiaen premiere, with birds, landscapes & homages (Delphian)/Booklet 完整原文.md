## A Messiaen premiere, with birds, landscapes and homages
### By Peter Hill

In 2012, while working on Messiaen's sketches, I came across several pages of what seemed to be finished music, in rapid and decisive handwriting, though very hard to decipher, prefaced with the Latin word *fin* (end) and signed with the date. Looking closely, I realised with growing excitement that this was a draft score of an unknown composition, that although written in bird-song was very much Messiaen's music: harmonic progressions in a major key and gestures that are unique in Messiaen's output. The manuscript appeared to be in an advanced state of completion, and would, I was sure, be considered so by Messiaen, who, at one point, seriously hoped to make a fair copy. (An annotation in the margin dates composition to August 1961, for Pâquier, Messiaen's summer retreat in the French Alps.)

Until this discovery it has been puzzling that Messiaen composed nothing in 1961. It now seems certain he used this time to plan the earlier *Catalogue d'oiseaux* (1956–8) – the earlier *Catalogue*'s layout of piano pieces to follow the earlier *Catalogue* of piano pieces (1956–8) and to plan the *subalpine warbler*, *Sylvia cantillans* shown in the cover photograph, *La Fauvette passerinette*. *Sylvia cantillans* is the *Catalogue*'s harmonic structure of each piece and the evocations of the sea, the mountains, trees reflected in a river – all elements of a new approach to more abstract, working methods in bird-song and emerging from a few background colour chords. Since the birdsong has to create its own harmony, the writing is richer than in the *Catalogue*, a stepping stone to the birdsong style in *La Fauvette des jardins* (1970) and to the fabulous refinement of *Petites esquisses d'oiseaux* (1985).

Another consequence of creating this structure from Messiaen's notes is that it allows the birds to be heard as one another, in a kaleidoscopic juggling of their characteristics: buoyantly, similar to Debussy's way, for example. The most prominent innovation is a new kind of bird development, a *fauvette passerinette* that absorbs motifs from the songs of other birds and that are layered with increasingly hard-edged, developing in a purposeful way that absorbs motifs from the songs of other birds and that are layered with increasingly hard-edged, that is, the final section is both a virtuosic finale and a synthesis of the catalogue's principal bird – and a recapitulation that sums up the bird-song heard in the piece.

The main difficulty in realising Messiaen's sketch proved, however, to be not in the manuscript itself – which feature the song of the soloist – are entirely finished, down to details such as pedal-marks and even fingerings. The middle is more fragmentary and required a certain amount of detective work. The order of events was determined by following Messiaen's alphabetically laid-out scheme, and was able to supply missing dynamics or marks of articulation by consulting Messiaen's birdsong notebooks. The work, once I knew that the music is closely based: these were on my only additions to Messiaen's text.

The disappearance of *La Fauvette passerinette* is understandable. By the end of 1961 Messiaen was occupied with plans for an orchestral work, the *Couleurs de la cité céleste* for the following year, and soon afterwards three more large-scale works were completed: *Sept Haïkaï* for piano and orchestra (1962), *Et exspecto resurrectionem mortuorum* for wind and brass (1964), and *La Transfiguration de Notre Seigneur Jésus-Christ* (1965–9). The second 'catalogue' of birds was never fulfilled, and *La Fauvette passerinette* was put away and forgotten.

I wanted to give a context to the newly-discovered piece by recording it alongside other works by Messiaen showing developments in his music up to 1961. These are further developed in the later pieces – a survey of the musical landscape that inspired him and inspired his music with nurture through his teaching. All the composers have something in common with Messiaen, or owe something to his example. Three were at different times students in Messiaen's class (Stockhausen, Tristan Murail and George Benjamin), while Takemitsu studied with him informally. Only very rarely does the music sound like an homage: George Benjamin's *Fantasy on Iambic Rhythm* is clearly such. If a student had imitated his style, he would have wanted only that our own voices should strengthen their own gifts. In fact he would probably have put their works down saying 'it's too strong a work! I'll say you were over-influenced by his musical world'.

I met the same generosity when I worked with Messiaen in Paris in the 1970s while I was preparing my recording of his piano music. He was encouraging, though all mildness disappeared when it came to his own music, as I recorded in an article about our sessions:

> Altogether an absolutely passionate, intense musician, everything has to be 100 per cent, and really love the birds, the sun, the music.

Messiaen set the highest standards, but he encouraged everyone around him to love his music, while keeping his own. The revelations came all at once. It was not unusual for him to play a few chords on the piano, scrutinising the effect of altering the balance to change the colour – literally so for Messiaen, who saw and experienced harmonies as colours.

## PRELUDE

This sensitivity to sound is apparent in Messiaen's earliest compositions, absorbed from the music he got to know in his childhood and teens. Messiaen played Ravel's piano music (which he played again in concerts with his students). 'He had musical information to give away.' So he wrote *Miroirs* in *La Colombe* (the first piece in the set of *Huit Préludes*), the final piece is a love song to the dove, setting its flight – curved flight, trills – against wildly spaced chords that recall the sombre F minor chords in Ravel's *Oiseaux tristes*. *Oiseaux tristes* invokes birds lost in an oppressive heat, very dark tones, evoking the hottest hours of summer. The inspiration was a real bird that I absorbed in 1928 during a visit to the forest of Fontainebleau, its song 'an vehement, melancholy arabesque'.

By his late teens Messiaen had found a personal and absolutely his own. Partly this stems from the interaction between tonality and modes and modes that have no tonal centre. La Colombe (The Dove) begins with a texture in three layers. The lowest sustains the bass part E major, while the central texture derives from the octatonic scale that Messiaen used as often as Bach used a pattern of alternating semitones and tones (E, F, G, G sharp, etc.). In between, the melody outlines E major, inflected expressively by notes foreign to the key but found in the mode F natural, A sharp, D natural. The coda returns in an E major tonality that is coloured by a softer parallel line, two octaves above the melody.

*La Colombe* was Messiaen's first evocation of a bird, composed while still a student of Paul Dukas at the Paris Conservatoire. First published in *La Revue musicale* in August 1936, a memorial collection (or 'tombeau') for Dukas, who had died the previous October. Messiaen's contribution, suggested by Falla, Rodolfo, Schmitt and others. Messiaen's arts graphic and spiritual message, seeming to react at the call of the light. The powerful harmonies include a chord he called the 'chord of resonance', a rich chord showing over a common bass note like the 'accords dans la renversement transposés'. I based my first CD on the piano music of Le Traquet stapazin, and again as a solemn pattern in *La Fauvette passerinette*.

## ÉTUDE

It was possibly through the influence of the students that Messiaen had been urged by his teacher to take an interest in serialism. As early as 1945 an entry in his diary speculates on applying serial organisation to tempo, later he planned a ballet on Taine, in which all the musical parameters were derived from a single twelve-note series. The catalyst for putting these ideas into effect came in 1949 with the sharp decline in health of Messiaen's colleague Olivier Messiaen, and the opera were shelved, and Messiaen embarked on a series of piano pieces in which the piano itself played, piano and organ. The piece was also a part of a project that he had planned for many years, 'Look for melodic motifs, chords, rhythmic figures from beyond my language, may accept a total freedom'.

The first of the *Quatre Études de rythme* to be composed was *Mode de valeurs et d'intensités* (1949). This was based not only pitch but also duration, dynamic, and even types of attack (staccato, legato, etc.) through serial methods. *Île de feu 1* was the last of the studies to be written, in the summer of 1950. The 'isle of fire' is a reference to the Gospel, and the piece is structured around a succession of dance-like bars with a pulsating accompaniment.

The *Quatre Études* were also an essential influence on a whole generation of younger composers who came to the fore in the 1950s. *Klavierstück VII* and *VIII* were written in May 1951 and played at the summer courses for new music at Darmstadt the same year, where it created a sensation. Among the audience was Karlheinz Stockhausen, then aged 22. The effect on Stockhausen's music was immediate, and from the following year he became one of Messiaen's star pupils in his time for Messiaen's class at the Paris Conservatoire. He had already met both Schaeffer and Radio France, where he made his first electronic music in 1952–3. Stockhausen then applied his researches to the piano, soon adding a second and third set of *Klavierstücke* to his growing output. *Klavierstück VII* is a single large piece, *Klavierstück XI* to *XVIII* (1954–5), but Stockhausen's first and last composed while studying with Messiaen.

The middle two pieces of the second set were intended by Stockhausen to be played together. The piece's opening, a chord of great harmonic and rhythmic complexity, is one of the most daring in all of Stockhausen's piano music. The piece is constructed around a central pitch-class set, and the music is organized around a single harmonic progression. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. The piece ends with a return to the opening material, but transformed and transfigured.

Julian Anderson's *Etude No 1* was written in 1995. His first *Etude* was written for a collection of *Variations* in 2001, in 1996. It is a brief but intense exploration of rhythmic complexity. The piece is constructed around a single rhythmic cell, which is repeated and varied throughout. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. The piece ends with a return to the opening material, but transformed and transfigured.

George Benjamin studied in the class at the Conservatoire from 1976 until Messiaen's retirement in the summer of 1978. He attended classes in composition, harmony and counterpoint, and also studied Messiaen's *Technique de mon langage musical*. Benjamin's *Fantasy on Iambic Rhythm* is a large-scale work that explores the possibilities of rhythmic variation and development. The piece is constructed around a single rhythmic cell, which is repeated and varied throughout. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. The piece ends with a return to the opening material, but transformed and transfigured.

## BIRDS & LANDSCAPES

In his student compositions, the birdsong in Messiaen's early music is stylised, for example the blackbird and nightingale at the opening of the *Quartet for the End of Time* (1940–41). Messiaen signalled a change of direction when he went public for the first time about his birdsong researches in a newspaper interview in 1948. Three years later, he set off to the Alps to listen to birds at first hand. *Le Traquet stapazin* (Black-eared wheatear) began in June 1962. It is the first piece in the second book of *Catalogue d'oiseaux*, and is dedicated to the memory of Messiaen's first wife, Claire Delbos. The piece is a portrait of the black-eared wheatear, a small bird that lives in the mountains of southern Europe. The piece is constructed around the bird's song, which is repeated and varied throughout. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. The piece ends with a return to the opening material, but transformed and transfigured.

Henri Dutilleux studied at the Paris Conservatoire with Jean Galon, who earlier had taught harmony to Messiaen. D'ombre et de silence (1973–88) was dedicated in memory of a friend, a pianist in Ravel, close in spirit to Messiaen. Dutilleux called his piece of the piano's own, dispersed by the release of successive keys like Stockhausen's *Klavierstücke* (above). In second half, the line ascends in layers of melody, the height of which hovers bird-like over the abyss.

Peter Sculthorpe and Douglas Young share a fascination with landscape. Peter Sculthorpe runs deep in French music since the Javanese gamelan at the Paris Exposition of 1889, through Debussy, Ravel and Satie, Messiaen himself encountered a Balinese gamelan first-hand at the Exposition universelle in 1931. Messiaen's interest in the Indonesian ensemble also influenced his writing: in *Ile de feu 1*, for instance, the bell he read as a young man, and the influence of gamelan can be heard as far away as Balinese composer's *Sun Music* (1967) and *Tabuh tabuhan* (1968), the latter title borrowed from McPhee's seminal study of gamelan music. The elegant but minute detail of Sculthorpe's music reflects his immersion in the specialised literature.

In the darkening skies the three notes of the antipodean burhin, harsh songbird of the night, ring out over the dark plain. Silence… Ten o'clock. Nightfall. A faint echo of the night's first warbler.

Stars is the last of five miniatures for piano called *Night Pieces*; one can imagine in this music the vastness of the Australian desert, silent land of the country's ancient culture. The texture gradually gains momentum, and the move from darkness to light is paralleled by the shift of the final page parallels passages in Messiaen's birdsong works, as the focus moves from outer space to the composer's inner emotional world.

The development of Douglas Young's music in the 1970s was from the early influences of Britten, Sibelius and Stravinsky towards a growing mastery of the world of music from Ireland, Africa, Latin America and Australia, with particular attention made a study of the work of Colin McPhee. River is a pianist's landscape, after Australian artist Ceremonial Music. River (1977–8) begins seemingly in the sound-world of Ravel's *Jeux d'eau*, with patterns of arpeggios and the piece unfolds the chord is understood as a landscape, with a series of melodic fragments and contrasting intervals heard both melodically and in a variety of colours. The piece is an inversion of the first, afforded the upper half of the keyboard, with middle 'C' the 'horizon' line of the landscape; on the Sculthorpe, the effects of rigorous construction combined with a sense of improvisation. The piece ends with a limpid evocation of last note, like 'Le Mont Aoraki'.

## MEMORIAL

Tristan Murail studied with Messiaen from 1967 to 1972. With Gérard Grisey (another Messiaen pupil, who also studied with Dutilleux), Murail initiated the movement known as 'spectral music', which is based on the analysis of the harmonic spectrum of sounds. *Cloches d'adieu, et un sourire…* was written in 1992 as a tribute to Messiaen, who had died in April of that year. The piece is constructed around a single chord, which is repeated and varied throughout. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. The piece ends with a return to the opening material, but transformed and transfigured.

Takemitsu's *Rain Tree Sketch II* was written in 1992 as a tribute to Messiaen, who had died in April of that year. The piece is constructed around a single chord, which is repeated and varied throughout. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. ## POSTLUDE

*Morceau de lecture à vue* (Sight-reading Piece) was written in 1934 as a pedagogical piece for Messiaen's students. The piece is a simple exercise in sight-reading, but it contains many of the harmonic and rhythmic elements that would become characteristic of Messiaen's later music. The piece is constructed around a single chord, which is repeated and varied throughout. The piece is a study in contrasts: the opening is loud and aggressive, while the middle section is quiet and contemplative. The piece ends with a return to the opening material, but transformed and transfigured.

## PETER HILL — Biography

One of the leading British pianists of his generation, Peter Hill worked with Messiaen between 1968 and 1991 when the composer was awarded the Diemersen Prize by the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris. His research on Messiaen continues, and a book on *Catalogue d'oiseaux* is in preparation. As well as recitals, Peter Hill gives lectures and masterclasses around the world. He holds an honorary professorship at the University of Sheffield and is a Fellow of the Royal Northern College of Music.

Writings include a book on Stravinsky (*Stravinsky: The Rite of Spring*, Cambridge University Press) and three books on Messiaen, among them a groundbreaking biography (*Messiaen*, Yale University Press) which was awarded the Diemersen Prize by the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris.
