## Alexander Kipnis by Will Crutchfield

Points on which all critics agree are rarer than total eclipses, so it is probably significant that I can climb down off the proverbial limb about Alexander Kipnis and have not yet come across a writer who was willing to favour anyone else’s opinion as to the role of the King Marke in *Tristan* over his. "Surely the best of all," is John Steane's way of putting it. Kenneth Funic's "a wonder it comes to King Mark there is none other," and then there are others. And so it goes on. It is interesting to have the opportunity of hearing that judgment borne out in the bass of a recording from the very beginning of the great bass's career (here reissued for the first time other than the well-known 1927 performance with Flagstad and Melchior, given near the end of it. Kipnis sang in a much more open and cramped, primitive sound, but what it tells us is that Kipnis we know from deep-timbered resonance, the well-honed, pointed articulation, the integrity of vocal sound. At *Rembrandtsches Verethi* he calls to mind Gerald Moore's description of Caruso as giving a feeling of depth even in the highest notes. It is very first line above, the end is something that he always knew how to convey.

Some of this is one of the century's great singers, by all indications fully formed and to almost, at the age of 22. "Almost" only because the gaiety and vivid projection he would soon find are not there in *Aprite un po*, and because the tender, patrician, almost feminine beauty that is to come have been a question of primitive recording-room exigencies. Notable that when the accompaniment is only a piano, as in the early Brahms song (about making its first CD appearance), the singer's voice is more powerfully present. But the instrument, and the singer's concept of its usage, and also the technical abilities of the day, present right from Track One – and we know that the following thirty-five give Kipnis his unique stature: a stature richly documented by the first CD presentation of acoustic recordings and the selection (including several rarities and unpublished stereo items) from the electric.

The voice was a rich, polished and finished bass of quality that impresses anew at each hearing no matter how well one thinks one has remembered it. It was not a profound bass, as Fuchs has it – for Kipnis does not venture below bass E, and one is occasionally aware of a slight edginess imposed on the tone in the lowest regions, presumably to aid projection. (Oddly it was Ezio Pinza, Kipnis's great opposite number in this repertoire and usually thought of as the higher bass of the pair, who came up with the sonorous bass D in Osmin's triumphal aria.) On the other hand the ease of resonance and body in the voice is superbly consistent, as far down as it goes, and the top had as high a combination of beauty, freedom, ease and manipulability as can be found in any unique among basses on record. He ranged freely up to a high G sharp (that was the limit, by the bass's own testimony in the taped reminiscences on file at Yale University's Historical Sound Recordings Collection; when in youth he took on the part of Silvio in *Pagliacci* he substituted a lower note for the top G in that context.) More remarkably, he had access to that variety of attack, dynamic and vowel colouring, necessary to the bass stage. And his *fortissimo* was a glory never failing, never forced, never dry; never tinnitus; just occasionally at the end of the long, sustained phrases.

Notes like the first syllable of "Meister" in the address of Hans Sachs on the previous page seemed to echo through a cathedral before the sound reached the listener's booth. And what a *Wotan* voice! Lucky Chicago to have heard this role in full live performances, though the generous memento of it included here is enough.

A singer with no faults, then? No, there are breaths one would wish he hadn't taken. Occasional tiny changes of vowel that are disappointingly hard-edged. The facts of the basso's life are well known: his birth in 1891 to grinding poverty in the Ukraine; his voyage migration towards a singing career that flourished in Germany while also establishing an important presence foreign stages, especially in London, Chicago and Buenos Aires; his flight from Nazism (after prominent efforts with the Kulturbund deutscher Juden) to Vienna and then to America, where he brought his operatic activity to a close with seven highly successful seasons at the Metropolitan.

Kipnis's artistic path through his long career seems not to have been a matter of unusual growth and refinement, nor of long string towards an ideal, nor yet of passing through several distinct artistic periods. Rather he seems to have refined his priorities and perfected his craft at the very outset, and maintained his standards scrupulously and scrupulously to the end. Most of his later recordings can be set beside the earliest with no apology; to an extraordinary degree it is not only the same artist but the same voice that we hear. A few at the very end fall short, the *Boccanegra* are often cited – but instead of leaving us a whole series of flawed mementos, as so many singers have done: Kipnis seems to have listened honestly to himself and exited at the right moment – as so many singers have said they would do. According to his own logic it was the onset of intonation lapses that bothered the basso most.

That sometimes makes it in one sense less interesting to compare Kipnis across the years than an ever-changing performer like Horowitz, Lotte Lehmann or Fischer-Dieskau. But in another, it emerges as an impressive testament to Kipnis's self-critical standards and virtues: and those are qualities central to the performances themselves.
