## Inner booklet

### Kathleen Long by W S Meadmore
*The Gramophone, September 1950*

Kathleen Long lives but a few hundred yards from the Wigmore Hall in a quiet secluded street, where, she remarked, she could practise when she liked and not disturb or annoy anybody. I arrived at the door of her flat at the same time as a messenger with a glowing bunch of scarlet gladioli. The flowers had been sent to her by one of the members of the Loewenguth Quartet, the occasions when she and the Quartet had played together.

We sat in a room which had been newly decorated, and drank tea out of fragile and exquisite china. Of course, there was a piano, a Steinway grand, its top strewn with scores and at one side, a silent metronome. That piano had a history, between wars it had been kept for Rachmaninov's use when he was in this country, it had been offered to Miss Long in 1940 when her own piano had been destroyed. Then she had been living in South Kensington until the night when a German bomb dropped and cut the house in two "like a piece of cheese". Miss Long was sitting in a front room, blast or shock occasioned unconsciousness, when she recovered, she was okay. All the same, that made quite a lot of people shaken.

"You will find me difficult to talk to," Miss Long said. I didn't. She had quickly put me at ease and was sympathetically helping me with my awkward and stumbling questions. Now she was saying: "I have no ambition. The only things I like about the musical profession are practice and rehearsing. It seems to me that musicians can be divided into two classes, those of us who, when we were young, have too much time to practise and no concerts, and those with many concerts and no time to practise! Whatever time I can spare I give to practise, it is the greatest pleasure of my life."

She was born in the Suffolk town of Bury St Edmunds, beginning to learn the piano when she was six, her aunt, a music teacher, being her first instructor. There was no great musical background: her father was a doctor, was her aunt's father was the organist of Croydon Parish Church at the beginning of the last century. She made her first public appearance at the age of eight. "With trepidation?" I suggested. "With wedding with bravado," Miss Long said. At the age of nineteen she made her début at the Aeolian Hall.

One of her first concerts was with Eugene Goossens and that fine violinist, the late Arthur Beckwith, thus early in her career identify-ing herself with chamber music. During the next four years she played in every classical work of the chamber music repertory (with the sole exception of the Brahms Clarinet Trio). With Marjorie Hayward, Rebecca Clarke and May Mukle, the English Ensemble was formed and played together for the next twenty years, among other first performances in this country, giving that of the Ravel string quartet. Miss Long on many other occasions played piano duets with Bennett. He was a good reader and a great enthusiast, she said.

When, at the end of the 1914-18 war, Miss Long left the Piano Quintet, then, owing to the pressure of her solo engagements, for many years her career was difficult. Ten years elapsed before she got the chance to play with an orchestra. In those days, she earned a living as an accompanist and by playing in many places gave concerts with John Goss, who, like herself, had enthusiasm for the music of van Dieren, whose music they frequently performed. She was the first to play some of Ravel's music in this country. She described him as "a dry little man, intellectual and interesting, but not sympathetique". Her first introduction to modern music had been through Casals, who had sent her from Prades after their concerts such a great honour to receive such words of praise from such a great musician.

Two other treasures were handed to me to look at, original photographs of Brahms and Robert Schumann taken together. These had been given her by Edward Speyer (a friend of Schumann's), at the end of a whole day's playing of Schumann at Speyer's home at Ridgehurst, Shenley.

From her first recitals, Miss Long has invariably given rare and satisfying musical experiences.

During the last war, she played at more than sixty of the National Gallery concerts. In 1941, she travelled in convoy to Iceland to give five recitals to the people of Reykjavik. When concerts after the war were regularly resumed at the Albert Hall, she there played the Fauré Ballade with Charles Münch, now the conductor of the Boston SO. Besides frequently playing with the French flautist, René le Roy, and many other French artists, she has recently accepted invitations to play with that distinguished ensemble, the Loewengh Quartet, in Paris, and with the Schweelinc Quartet in Holland.

Since, she has recorded a great number of Fauré records, including the *Ballade* for piano and orchestra, the magnificent *Theme and Variations* (lately remade, together with some of the Barcarolles, Impromptus and Nocturnes, for long-playing records), the second book of Debussy *Préludes*, many Scarlatti sonatas, the Schumann G minor and the Schubert E flat sonatas, and the Mozart G minor concerto with the Concertgebouw of Amsterdam, conducted by Eduard van Beinum. Miss Long was reticent to say which of her recordings she preferred, nor would she admit that she was satisfied with any of them. "It would be presumptuous," she said, "to say that." "I do not think Miss Long would be satisfied with anything she did. She would always have the feeling that it was not perfect, that it could have been done better!"

French critics do not usually praise English musicians. The critic of the *Paris Combat* however has these remarks to offer on Miss Long's recording of Fauré's *Ballade* and as they are so much what I feel about these recordings I repeat them:

*"La Ballade pour piano de Fauré figure fois aux catalogues de disques Français, avec édition ancienne de Marguerite Long et celle récente de Gaby Casadesus. Ces deux versions, la première interprétée dans un esprit très fouréen, mais dans une grave que de brio que de sensibilité. Aussi, pour une fois, acceptera-t-ona dans peu pâle, la seconde jouée avec plus de brio que de sensibilité, acceptera-t-on dans peu préférable aux précédents enregistrements. La sonorité veloutée et liquide que Miss Long obtient chez Fauré, la Chaleur héritée des maîtres qu'il admirait profondément."*
