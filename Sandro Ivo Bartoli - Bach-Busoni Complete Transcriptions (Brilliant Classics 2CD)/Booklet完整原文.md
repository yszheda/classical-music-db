**The Bach-Busoni transcriptions: a tale of genius and destiny**

It was not long after I had begun my studies at the Royal Academy of Music in London that, one fateful morning, I was awoken by an early telephone call; a friend was ordering: 'Come to the Festival Hall at once! Shura Cherkassky was playing!' My musical erudition was a sketchy affair then, and I had no idea who Shura Cherkassky was, but my protestations of tiredness and indisposition fell on deaf ears: 'Come at once! I've got the tickets.' It was Sunday, 13 October 1991: at 3.45pm precisely the 82-year-old Shura Cherkassy came on stage with his elfin step, sat at the piano and began playing Bach-Busoni's Chaconne. Six short bars later a new world opened before my eyes and ears. I had never heard such ravishing sounds come out of a piano, and Shura's delivery was something akin to magic: each and every musical line came forth with absolute conviction, as if guided by a supernatural entity, and the overall impression was one of blessed sublimity. Transfixed, I wrote Maestro Cherkassky an admiring letter and, to my great fortune and delight, a few months later he became my mentor. Such was my fascination with his playing of the Bach-Busoni Chaconne that I pestered him many times about his mercurial way of performing it. At best, he would say in his typical matter-of-fact style: 'Yes, it is truly marvellous music'; or, if in a particularly helpful mood, 'You have to sound like a church organ.' As a child I had worn down a couple of LPs of Bach's organ music performed by Dr Albert Schweitzer, and his majestic, profoundly humane performances had left a lasting impression in my mind. It was the combination of Dr Schweitzer's august approach and Shura's bewitching renditions that spurred my interest in and helped to forge my interpretative ideal of the Bach-Busoni transcriptions. Research into the subject highlights a wonderful story that flirts with destiny and genius in equal measure.

Ferruccio Dante Michelangelo Benvenuto Busoni (Empoli, 1866 – Berlin, 1924) was an Italian pianist, composer, philosopher, pedagogue, bibliophile and aesthete whose work left a lasting impression on several areas of the music profession. Despite endorsements from none other than Liszt and Rubinstein, he was fundamentally self-taught and cultivated his multifarious activities with unprecedented freedom and liberal thinking. Preoccupied with art as a general sphere of expression, and intolerant of dogmatism, he made it his quest to push boundaries through searching for an ideal beauty of classical proportions that transcended limitations. As a pianist, he ranked amongst the greatest in history: 'His sense of infallibility – wrote Alfredo Casella – merged beautifully with the grandiosity of his style, which in turn did not allow weaknesses. This style was essentially epic, monumental, cyclopic, but also fantastic and surreal.'

Following Busoni's performance of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier in Helsinki on 3 November 1888, Adolf Paul famously titled his review 'Ein Bach-Spieler von Gottes Gnaden' ('A Bach-player by the Grace of God'). But although the combination of this labelling with an 'epic, monumental style' might have been a satisfactory result for many young pianists, this was not the case for Ferruccio – he saw his personal success as an inevitable consequence of his destiny, and, generous man that he was, set forth to share his knowledge and vision through a planned mammoth edition of the complete keyboard works of Bach. He moved to Leipzig and found lodgings in the Centralstrasse just in front of the Thomaskirche, where Johann Sebastian Bach had been Kantor, and it was there that he heard a performance of Bach's Prelude and Fugue in D major BWW532 (presumably played by Carl Hermann Wilhelm Pisti, then the church's principal organist) and 'was moved' to transcribe it for the pianoforte. At this time, his active repertoire included a number of piano transcriptions of Bach's organ works, notably Tausig's famed arrangement of the Toccata and Fugue in D minor BWV565, and it is perhaps natural that a young pianist-composer, ambitious and enthusiastic, felt the urge to try his hand at producing his own version of something he admired; he had no idea, I feel sure of it, of the consequences his actions were to have not only on his personal artistic trajectory, but on the very future of the genre. This was effectively the moment in which his preoccupation with the music of Bach 'merged beautifully with the grandiosity of his style'; such a complex and challenging 'transposition', to use Busoni's own description, was a novel affair and revealed a pianistic style that was modern and emancipated from the Romantic tradition. Although he respected Bach's text with fidelity, Busoni intervened heavily as regards the texture of the music, and concentrated on reproducing the sonorities of the organ with a formidable re-disposition of the musical matter throughout the keyboard. The result was shocking, with lines that seemed carved into the finest Carrara marble and highlighted contrasts, heightened the dramatic impact and often called for sophisticated pedal effects. Busoni moved fundamentally along the same path in his transcription of the Prelude and Fugue in E flat major BWV552. These were the opening and closing numbers of the third volume of Bach's Clavier-Übung, an important collection of Chorale Preludes that Bach had himself held in high regard (so much so that he actually published it privately). The Kantor had specified a registration pro organo pleno, 'for full organ', hinting at the

majestic conception of the Prelude and the enormous complexion of the triple Fugue. Busoni, with his 'epic, monumental, cyclopic' style, was in his element recreating on the piano such lush, awesome sonorities, and his transcription is a tour de force both physically and intellectually.

The best, however, was yet to come.

Encumbered by financial difficulties, in 1891 Ferruccio accepted the post of Piano Professor at the New England Conservatory of Music in Boston. In America he toured extensively and eventually produced his most ambitious Bach transcription yet: the mighty Ciaccona from the second Solo Violin Partita BWV1060. Busoni admitted that he had treated the musical material in much the same way as the organ works, although here he had expanded liberally a whirlwind of contrapuntal ideas that were only hinted at in the original. The effect was orchestral, and when Busoni premiered his transcription of the Chaconne at Boston's Union Hall on 30 January 1893, public success was unanimous and unequivocal: he had found an ideal balance between old and new, and through his innovative piano writing had created a sublime environment in which the same again.

Adventurous, bold moves towards the future have always met with a modicum of scepticism, and Busoni's Chaconne was no exception. He had dedicated the work to Eugen d'Albert, the famous Liszt pupil, who showed little enthusiasm for it and claimed that in his opinion the Chaconne could 'not tolerate a transcription for two hands' and that it was 'too modern'. One could not help feeling that d'Albert felt a certain amount of jealousy towards Busoni; he was himself the author of several Bach transcriptions that were steeped in the Romantic tradition and shared little of the Italian's pioneering, modernist approach. D'Albert's complaints, however, prompted Busoni to defend his position. When he licensed his edition of Bach's first volume of the Well-Tempered Clavier in 1894, Busoni completed it with an appendix titled 'On the transcription of Bach's Organ Works for the Pianoforte'. Indeed, convinced that the interpretation of Bach's organ pieces on the piano was 'essential to a complete pianistic study of Bach', he assembled a full compendium on the subject, outlining in detail his own procedures and offering insights into his reasoning – both technically and artistically. He maintained that 'the piano possesses certain characteristics that give it an advantage over the organ: rhythmic precision, emphatic exactness of entrance, greater impetuosity and distinctness in passage-playing, ability as regards varying the touch, clearness in involved situations, and, where required, rapidity [...]'. The great disadvantage, however, was to be found in the piano's inability to sustain a note indefinitely as can the organ, although Busoni hinted at mysterious possibilities he could achieve via judicious deployment of the third pedal. He was particularly engrossed in the subject of registration, recreating certain organ effects on the keyboard by means of an adequate imitation of the organ disposition, and offered his transcription of the Prelude and Fugue in E minor BWV533 as an exemplar of his theories. Many sceptics were thereafter silenced.

Back in Europe, Busoni expanded on his quest to spread the popularity of Bach's music. He prefaced the 1898 edition of his arrangement of 10 Chorale Preludes with a statement of intentions: he had not wished to display his capabilities as an arranger, but rather 'to interest a larger selection of the public in these compositions which are so rich in art, feeling and fantasy'. He added a quizzical caveat: that these arrangements were in a 'chamber music style' and only seldom made 'great demands upon the technical proficiency of the performer'. A cursory look at these scores, at least through the eyes of this writer, suggests that Busoni was a weak liar!

Two Toccatas BWV 564 & 565. These were famous pieces to start with: the great Toccata, Adagio and Fugue BWV564 – which apparently drew its inspiration from the popularity of the three-part Italian concertos of Vivaldi and Corelli – and that youthful romp, the Toccata and Fugue in D minor BWV565, which was destined to enter the pantheon of 'famous tunes' of the 20th century, heralding the entrance of Sir Christopher Lee in countless Dracula films! BWV564 is perhaps the more challenging of the two, presenting the pianist with a full array of technical and interpretative problems, not the least of which are to be found in the middle movement, Adagio. Here a long caressing has to sustain itself on seemingly endless curves and nuances, during the very spirit of the pianoforte and testing it to its utmost limits: it is a testament to Busoni's complete command of the instrument, and his lifelong, esoteric flirtation with achieving the impossible.

Busoni was often referred to as 'the last Renaissance man'. Thanks to his work as an editor and transcriber, his name became inextricably linked to that of Bach, to the extent that his widow Gerd was often introduced as 'Mrs Bach-Busoni'! He had both the vision and the fortitude to illuminate the most complex issues with crystalline clarity, and throughout his life endeavoured to share his personal conquests with anyone who would listen. In his seminal Sketch of a New Aesthetic of Music, he postulated that 'every form of musical notation is in itself a transcription of an abstract idea', and, in another well-known essay, that from Bach he had 'learnt that Good and Universal Music remains the same through whatever medium it is sounded'. For the time, these were innovative, visionary precepts indeed. He envisaged all his Bach transcriptions as an advanced school of pianoforte playing, and gave an ascending order of difficulty: Ten Chorale Preludes, Prelude and Fugue in D major, Prelude and Fugue in E flat major, Chaconne, Two Toccatas. This is music of the highest calibre, with soaring, majestic lines that seem to aim straight at the Heavens; poignant moments of the most sublime intimacy; and complex, intricate counterpoints thatmagically blend into one another as if guided by an inexorable destiny. To tackle it through the magnifying lenses of such a piano titan as Ferruccio Busoni is, for me at least, a great privilege and an act of enduring joy.

In a final analysis, in the 1923 epilogue to his Bach edition, Busoni paid a posthumous tribute to his father Ferdinando: 'I owe my father the good fortune of having been very severely pushed towards the study of Bach during my early years; this happened at a time during which, in Italy, the Maestro did not enjoy much more credit than Carl Czerny. Which same instinct should have brought my father, a simple clarinet virtuoso who enjoyed playing Fantasias and Paraphrases on Il Trovatore and the Carnival of Venice, [...] to guess the only right way, seems to me a mysterious order of Destiny'. Perhaps so, and the gain is posterity's.

**Sandro Ivo Bartoli**

Sandro Ivo Bartoli is one of the foremost interpreters of early 20th-century Italian piano music. Acclaimed internationally as "an outwordly virtuoso" (Svenska Dagbladet) and "a fantastic pianist" (Der Spiegel Kultur), he has played all over the world with orchestras such as the Philharmonia in London, the Deutsche Philharmonie, the Hallé, the Maggio Musicale Fiorentino and the Mozart Chamber Orchestra.

Bartoli studied at the Florence State Conservatory and at the Royal Academy of Music in London, but the most important influence on his artistic development came from his lengthy collaboration with the late Russian pianist Shura Cherkassky. His rediscovery of the concertos of Respighi, Malipiero and Casella led to the first modern performance in the USA of Ottorino Respighi's Toccata for piano and orchestra, under Lewis Dalvit; the concert was broadcast live by PBS and subsequently included in the station's 'Great Performances' series. As a result, Bartoli signed his first recording contract, with ASV, for an album dedicated to the piano works of Malipiero. His career developed rapidly with appearances in Sweden, Norway, Germany and France that cemented his international reputation. On a return visit to Germany, the German press defined Bartoli 'one of the most important musicians to come out of Italy in the last 30 years'. Sandro Ivo Bartoli is the Artistic Director of the Accademia de'Concerti, a music society devoted to the divulgation of Italian music.

A musician of varied interests, Sandro Ivo Bartoli appeared in the triple capacity of pianist, arranger and composer when he produced the scene music for Fernando Pessoa's Book of Disquiet, performed to great acclaim at the Avignon festival in an adapted version by Antonio Tabucchi. The same year he founded in London 'Opera Excetera', a series of concerts devoted to the non-vocal music of opera composers, and the Lyric Club Renato Bruson of Turin awarded him the Gina Rosso Prize for his work in the arts.

More significant engagements followed: Shostakovich's First Concerto in Stockholm, Rachmaninov's Third Concerto in London, Beethoven's Third Concerto in Bedford, and a cycle of five concerts where he performed various works by Malipiero, Busoni, Casella, Respighi, Pizzetti, and the new Sonata of Luciano Berio. In 2005 Sandro Ivo Bartoli took part in a grand benefit concert at Munich's Philharmonie with Martha Argerich and Rodion Shchedrin, and completed the world premiere recording of Malipiero's piano concertos with the Radio Symphony Orchestra of Saarbrücken under Michele Carulli for CPO. The double CD was received with enthusiasm by the international press, winning a Diapason D'Or/Découverte, the '10 de Classica-Répertoire', and numerous 5-star reviews from various magazines.

Most recently, Bartoli has embarked on a large-scale recording activity which has seen numerous releases on Brilliant Classics – the complete Liszt-Busoni transcriptions, Respighi's works for piano and orchestra, and Busoni's monumental Fantasia contrappuntistica have all met wide critical acclaim. On the concert platform, he has played Rachmaninov's Second Concerto in Dresden, Beethoven's Fourth and Mozart's 'Jeunehomme' in Munich. After his performance of Liszt's Malédiction Concerto in Bad Elster, the German press defined Bartoli 'one of the most important musicians to come out of Italy in the last 30 years'. Sandro Ivo Bartoli is the Artistic Director of the Accademia de'Concerti, a music society devoted to the divulgation of Italian music.
