## Liner Notes

### Magda Olivero and Adriana Lecouvreur

Testament offers this historic recording in tribute to Magda Olivero, who died in Milan on 8 September 2014 at the age of 104. She was born in 1910 at Saluzzo, near Turin in Italy, year 1914 as was often given during her career. She made her stage debut at Turin in 1933 as Lauretta in Giannino Schicchi and during the next two years quickly established herself as one of the foremost of the younger generation of Italian sopranos. In 1941, soon after adding the role of Adriana Lecouvreur to her repertoire, she withdrew from the stage after she married, but was soon persuaded by Cilea to come out of retirement and revise his masterpiece, which she did in 1951.

From then on she sang all over the world to a wide range of operas including Fedra, La bobème, La fanciulla del West, La traviata, La Wally, Madama Butterfly, Manon Lescaut, Montsalvatte and Tosca at La Scala; but Adriana remained her most famous role. The part fitted her like a second skin. Adriana became a talisman for her: wearing rouge written for the August 1963 Opera magazine (part of which is included in this booklet); prior to her appearance at the Edinburgh festival that year with the San Carlo company of Naples. She had sung in the opening of this production in Naples in 1959 when, despite miscarriage during surgery herself, she replaced Renata Tebaldi who cancelled after the dress rehearsal. The second recording of the 1959 performance with Tito Gobbi, Simionato and Barbieri has been widely circulated and will temper a video copy emerge of the first night, which was televised. Then in 1963, she joined the San Carlo company in Edinburgh with no less a tenor than Giuseppe di Stefano.

After Edinburgh, Olivero sang the role once more, in 1967 at La Scala with Giuseppe di Stefano and in 1972 appeared in the USA in Cherubini's Médée at the Dallas Opera in 1967 and at Kansas City in 1968, and made her belated farewells to Puccini in 1975. Her last performances on stage were in March 1981 in the one-woman opera, La voix humaine by Poulenc.

In 1983 she donated her concert wardrobe after losing her beloved husband. She continued to give an annual recital in the church at her birthplace of Saluzzo, and in 1993 she amazed the opera world by singing the role of Adriana for a Risorgimento recording which she described as her testament to her favourite role. I can testify to her recital for her concern the same year in Barcelona, celebrating 60 years since her debut. At the age of 83, she sang the two arias of Adriana and astonished all with her technique, even refining her interpretation by adding a perfect messa di voce: it crescendo followed by a diminuendo) on the final note of 'Io son l'umile ancella'.

I first had the good fortune to capture her interpretation at the optimum for vocal preservation and quality of sound. There are several other live recordings with different colleagues, including a finest extract from a 1939 broadcast from Rome Opera with Gigli, and also recorded performances in 1957. In this performance she is given excellent support from Juan Oncina especially from the Spanish tenor Juan Oncina, a late replacement for Flavisio Labo. The critics, predictably, dismissed the work while praising the singers. One critic, Philip Hope-Wallace, for Opera, wrote: 'The success of the evening was the assumption of the name part by handsome and dignified Magda Olivero, who was called out of retirement twelve years ago to study the part with the composer, of now slightly beyond her best. Dependent, concentrated efforts of glissantis, a fine simple and telling style put her, at least, in mind of Claudia Muzio at the final stages. The voice itself had not much liltson on it on the first night but the characterisation of the role and the understanding of what must be brought to it bespoke a real artist at work.'

*(Richard Capesani, 2014)*

### A Personal Reminiscence of Adriana Lecouvreur

The Edinburgh International Festival 1963. The company was the San Carlo from Naples and the opera was Cilea's Adriana Lecouvreur, a work that had not been seen in Britain since 1927. Its star, making her Festival debut, was Magda Olivero. In an operatic career spanning over half a century she sang innumerable performances in opera by Rossini, Donizetti and Verdi, but it was in verismo that she excelled. She was particularly associated with the role of Adriana, which became her signature role.

We should also remember that, after Lecouvreur's death and the almost clandestine burial in unconsecrated ground, Voltaire protested against the unjust decision of the Paris clergy and dedicated some moving poems to her memory. Among her biographers were such writers as Saint-Beuve and D'Argental. One of the aesthetic and psychological pictures of the character had been built with documentary faithfulness. Cilea could freely follow the romantic pattern set by the drama by Scribe and Legouvé, stressing the human side together in the suggestion of being on stage.

Extract from the article that appeared in *Opera* magazine in August 1963, reprinted here by courtesy of the editor.

*(Eileen Moller, 2014)*

Eileen Moller is author of *The Edinburgh International Festival 1947-1996* (Birlinn Press, 2007).

### Juan Oncina 1921-2009

Born in Barcelona, Ončina studied there with Mercedes Llopart and later with Ghirardini in Milan. He made his debut at the Gran Teatre del Liceu in Barcelona in 1946, in the same year he appeared in Bologna as Almaviva in Il barbiere di Siviglia. He appeared in the major Italian theatres (La Scala, La Fenice and Rome) and in 1952 he appeared in Barcelona as the Duke in Rigoletto and as Don Curzio in Le nozze di Figaro. In 1952 he sang in the opening of the San Carlo production of Adriana Lecouvreur, replacing Flaviano Labò who cancelled after the dress rehearsal. He continued to sing until 1961. He also appeared at the Vienna State Opera and Bayreuth. His last appearance was in 1974 at the Liceu in Barcelona as Monostatos in Die Zauberflöte.

*(adapted from Grove)*

### Sesto Bruscantini 1919-2003

Born in Civitanova Marche, Italy, Sesto Bruscantini was one of the most versatile singers of his generation, initially renowned as both a buffo bass and baritone in Mozart, Rossini and Donizetti. He later moved on to sing more lyrical and dramatic roles including Verdi parts such as Rigoletto, Germont in La traviata, Simon Boccanegra, Melitone in La forza del destino, Rodrigo in Don Carlo and Iago in Otello. He won a KOV award for Rigoletto in 1947 and made his debut at La Scala, Milan, in 1949 as Geronimo in Cimarosa's Il matrimonio segreto. A singer of amazing range and versatility, he was a regular guest at the Glyndebourne and Salzburg festivals, the Vienna State Opera and also appeared in Brussels, Monte Carlo, Paris and London. He sang virtually little outside Europe but did appear at the Lyric Opera in Chicago in 1961 and 1962. He retired from the stage in 1980, to work on establishing a singing school at his native town.

*(adapted from Grove)*

### Adriana Lazzarini born 1933

Born in Venice in 1933, Adriana Lazzarini was born in Istria. After studying at the Conservatory of Vienna and with Carlo Morelli in Milan, in 1954 she won the Votto Competition and in 1955 La Callas Competition at Asciano in Florentine. In 1957 she made her debut at La Scala in La forza del destino. Her career was primarily based in Italy, where she appeared at the major theatres in Bologna, Florence, Rome, Venice, Trieste and Palermo. She also appeared at the Vienna State Opera and the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden. She retired from the stage in 1975.

---

### Piero de Palma 1925-2013

Born in Molfetta in Southern Italy, Piero de Palma studied with his father and was an outstanding tenore di grazia. After studying in Florence, he specialized in comprimario parts, a genre at which he became one of the most acclaimed artists of the post-war era. During his career, he left recordings of over 130 of them, appearing at the major theatres throughout Italy. He made his debut at La Scala in 1952 as the Duke of Mantua in Rigoletto. He sang at all the leading Italian opera houses including Rome, Florence, Venice, Naples, and made his La Scala debut as Don Curzio in Le nozze di Figaro. He also appeared regularly in Opera and made his Met debut as Don Basilio in Il barbiere in 1972.

### Cilea and Adriana Lecouvreur

I must admit that, in the greatest gallery of all the characters whose lives I have re-lived and expressed through my singing with an unequalled emotional sincerity and equal affection, the character of Adriana Lecouvreur occupies a special, in my heart, almost sacred place.

This is not only because of those natural, understandable reasons of feminine psychology which are so easily understood when one thinks that I have lived this part intensely for over forty years, with a deep love that has never wavered.

The French tragic play by Scribe and Legouvé on which the opera is based, was given to me with the conviction that I would interpret it at its best. Thus, in 1941, shortly before my first retirement, I had taken my first steps into the part of Adriana with the great tenor Beniamino Gigli. After that, in 1951, when I was persuaded by Cilea to come out of retirement to revise his masterpiece, I sang it in Rome with Giacinto Prandelli, and again in 1959 in Naples with Giuseppe di Stefano. In 1963, I sang it at the Edinburgh Festival with the San Carlo Company of Naples, conducted by Oliviero de Fabritiis.

The spiritual link I feel with the character of the unfortunate, unhappy, passionate actress of the Comédie-Française is made even stronger by a personal event which occurred at the time of my second debut, in 1951, after my voluntary exile of almost ten years.

In 1941, shortly after I had taken my first steps into the part of Adriana with the great tenor Beniamino Gigli, I was to make my debut in this role at the Opera House in Rome. A few days before the opening night, I received a message from Cilea that asked me to suspend that I should go to his home in Varazze, where he was living. It was there that I was to have found the ideal suggestions. This determined change of plan brought me to meet Cilea's new ideas, and also of a sudden sharing of a personal experience that, in the intimacy of Cilea's home, took on a more romantic and totally strange interpretation was that I, in fact, had found the way to interpret Adriana's character – once and for all – the world of opera from my own life.

The story continued to sing but my spirit, as I had felt so strongly, had become a second skin.
