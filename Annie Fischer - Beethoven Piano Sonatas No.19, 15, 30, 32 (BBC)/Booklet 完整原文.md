```
scherzo. The Andante, with its gentle melody over a moving staccato bass, was said to be
Beethoven's favourite movement.
```

**第 4 页 (英文续):**

```
a few months later, Annie Fischer recorded the little G minor sonata Op.49 No.1, one of
two which Beethoven completed in the late 1790s. A serene Andante gives way to a
gently pastoral Rondo.

Beethoven's last sonata is in two contrasted movements. The first has a double-dotted
introduction, followed by a faster section with a three-note motif: a second theme of six
bars leads to a further dramatic episode and a forceful coda. The second movement is a set
of variations of great complexity and grandeur; a long coda summarises the mood of the
principal theme.

Because of her strong dislike of the physical process of recording, Annie Fischer made
relatively few records. Of those discs that were issued, her Mozart concertos with Boult
and Szwajkoll are best known, though her Schumann Concerto with Klemperer and a
public performance with Fricsay of the Bartók Third (with which she had made her
Salzburg début with Solti in 1964) have attracted much praise. She recorded all the
Beethoven piano sonatas in Hungary but steadfastly refused to approve them for release;
in this, she was never released.

Her last appearance on the concert platform was in a recital at the Royal Festival Hall in
June 1992, when she took the place of the indisposed Alfred Brendel. Like so many of her
concerts, this was a special occasion: as an encore, she played the fugue from the
Hammerklavier sonata. Annie Fischer died in April 1995, shortly after being taken ill at
a concert in Budapest. She had been listening to a broadcast performance of Bach's St John
Passion and was in her eighty-first year.
```

```
Annie Fischers Bühnenkarriere begann mit Beethoven: mit knapp acht Jahren gab sie ihre
erste Darbietung seines Ersten Klavierkonzerts. Mit jugendlicher Zuversicht begann sie
ihr Studium im heimatlichen Budapest, wo Dohnanyi einer ihrer Lehrer war. Zu ihren
```
