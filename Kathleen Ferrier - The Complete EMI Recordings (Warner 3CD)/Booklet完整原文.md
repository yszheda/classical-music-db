# Kathleen Ferrier

Kathleen Ferrier had many sides to her personality. She was gregarious, intense, hardworking, and had a ribald sense of humour, yet – as you can hear on these recordings – they all came together into a radiant whole when she opened her mouth to sing. It wasn't just for the beauty of her contralto voice, a remarkably low and rich instrument with dazzling lights shimmering over its surface, that she was loved, however. It was also for the immediacy and directness of her powers of communication, the sense that she wanted to share her joy of living with each one of her audience members. When she sang – anything from sophisticated Mahler to simple folk songs – she could reduce audiences to tears with the generosity, directness and warmth of her performances.

Her early death from cancer in 1953, aged just 41, robbed the musical world of one of its giants and, as with all great artists who die young, has led to a tantalising set of what ifs. What if she'd recorded Elgar's *The Dream of Gerontius*, in which she was by all accounts sublime? What if she'd sung Wagner? What if she'd sung more opera? Painful thoughts: so we must let it suffice that what remains of her recorded legacy still contains some of the most appealing and moving singing ever committed to disc. 'She was a tall, elegant woman, with a tremendous sense of Lancashire warmth,' said the great mezzo Janet Baker in tribute. 'People could connect with her.' And connect they did, whether in her sold-out concerts or via her best-selling discs.

To celebrate the centenary of her birth, EMI is releasing together for the first time this series of recordings from early and late in her career. The first few tracks, recorded in June 1944 but not issued until 1978, are tests supervised by legendary producer Walter Legge, made to find out how the microphone responded to her voice, and how the result appeared when projected through speakers. (There are plenty of great singers who are at their worst in recordings and, conversely, there are mediocre singers who blossom in front of a microphone: it's one of the mysteries of the art.) As we can tell, Ferrier sounded terrific, and she was immediately offered a one-year contract, which she signed in September 1944. The rest of the early recordings of short songs and duets here were made in fulfilment of that deal.

Legge later wrote (in his memoir of Maria Callas) that jealous colleagues of Ferrier had tried to put him off hearing her, and had described her to him as 'not your type of singer'. But he knew talent when he spotted it, and was thrilled to have Ferrier at EMI. Unfortunately his delight wasn't reciprocated. Legge was indisputably a genius as a record producer, but he could be demanding and dictatorial too. Ferrier disliked him and felt unhappy working with him. She jumped ship and went to EMI's rival Decca, where she remained on contract for the rest of her career.

This didn't prevent her coming back to EMI later in 1949 'on loan', however, when there was a chance to record a work very close to her heart, Mahler's *Kindertotenlieder* ('Songs on the Death of Children') with the Vienna Philharmonic. The temptation of working with her friend and mentor Bruno Walter, a conductor who had known Mahler personally, and of singing with an orchestra which had Mahler's music in its blood (as much as any orchestra did at a time when Mahler's music was relatively unknown), proved too great to resist, Legge or no Legge. And thank goodness it did.

It was with Bruno Walter, a colleague whom she came almost to regard as a second father, that she came closest as a singer after Mahler – perhaps her most heartbreaking recording of all, Mahler's *Das Lied von der Erde* (The Song of the Earth) for Decca in 1952. She was already very ill by this time, and both she and Walter understood that she was in some mysterious manner celebrating her own life and forthcoming death through Mahler's music.

As a bonus, at the end of this collection, there are two extra 'reserve takes' from Ferrier and Walter's *Kindertotenlieder* issued here for the first time. As was EMI's practice in 1949, the recording was made on 12-inch waxes, with a tape machine running as a safety back-up. For the original 78-rpm release, masters from the waxes were used; for the later re-release of the work on CD, the tapes (but not these reserves) were used. Here they see the light of day for the first time.

The Bach items have an unusual history. They were recorded at a rehearsal in the Musikvereinssaal concert hall in Vienna in June 1950. EMI engineers were setting up their equipment in the hall for another project, and decided to turn on their machines to check that they were working properly. In this rather accidental manner, for which heaven be praised, they captured these tracks including two duets with Walter Legge's wife, the soprano Elisabeth Schwarzkopf.

Ferrier didn't perform much staged opera during her career, for although she worked hard at her stagecraft, she wasn't a natural actor. 'I couldn't believe how difficult it was to make even the simplest arm movements without looking like a broken windmill,' she once wrote,

wondering on another occasion, 'Could I even walk on stage without falling over my own rather large feet?' But her two chosen roles still brought out the very best in her: those were the title role of Benjamin Britten's *The Rape of Lucretia* (which had been composed specifically for her), and Orfeo in Gluck's *Orfeo ed Euridice*. The recording presented here of the latter opera is a live radio broadcast from Amsterdam, a city where Ferrier felt particularly at home, in July 1951.

This role also came to play a part in the Ferrier legend, for it was during a performance of *Orfeo ed Euridice* in 1953 at Covent Garden that Ferrier's femur in her left leg broke: it been eaten away by cancer, and crumbled while she was on stage. Though immobilised, she completed the performance before being taken to hospital. That she was singing about the power of love to conquer death, while so close to death herself, caused even greater general respect after her passing for her power to touch on the 'great immensities' through her art.

It was all a far cry from her beginnings. Ferrier was born on 22 April 1912 into an industrious lower-middle-class Lancashire family, of the solid but circumscribed type celebrated in the novels of Arnold Bennett. Her parents were both very musical, and both teachers – although, as was customary, her mother gave up her professional life to look after the family once married. Her father became headmaster of a school in Blackburn, where the family moved in 1914. Ferrier's was a cosy and safe life, and it cost her a great deal of heart-searching, when the time came later, to throw over her routine of small concerts and provincial choral societies and make the necessary move to London.

Her musical talents blossomed early, although it was as a pianist, not a singer, that she initially saw herself. In her early teens, she began to do well in local competitions. But her dream of pursuing a higher musical education came to nothing, and her father's impending retirement meant that there was little money to support much hope. Ferrier left school at 14, and began to work as a trainee telephonist at the local exchange. She continued to perform occasionally, but her life was too tangled with the wires of the GPO to permit a full commitment to her art. When she was 19 she began singing lessons, and took occasional solos in local concerts. At first her voice was not considered remarkable, but slowly her powers began to reveal themselves. In 1937, aged 25, she entered a singing competition for a bet in Carlisle and, to her surprise, she won. After this offers of work began to materialise, and during the war she successfully auditioned for the Council for the Encouragement of the Arts (CEMA), an enterprise which took concerts and other entertainments to canteens and workplaces to boost morale. It was here she first began to fraternise with professional singers (such as Isobel Baillie, heard in the duets on CD1) and they encouraged her to turn her thoughts to London, and give her career the chance it deserved. When the great conductor Malcolm Sargent heard her, he suggested her as a client to Ibbs and Tillett, a London-based concert management agency. They took her on immediately and so, in 1942, Ferrier took the plunge and moved with her sister and retired father to Hampstead in London.

Her first London concert was at the National Gallery on December 28. 'Oh boy, did my knees knock!' she wrote cheerfully in a letter. 'There was a huge crowd, and it was a bit of a facer, so I was glad when it was safely over.' Success bred success: soon she was making her first recordings, and appearing on light entertainment programmes such as *Housewives' Choice*. Her Proms debut was in 1945. The *Lucretia* premiere at Glyndebourne took place in 1946. Her first trip abroad, to perform Britten's opera in Holland in 1947, was greeted rapturously.

It was in 1947 too that she met the great conductor Bruno Walter (heard here on the *Kindertotenlieder* tracks), who was to play a major role in introducing her to the music of Gustav Mahler. 'The greatest thing in music in my life has been to have known Kathleen Ferrier and Gustav Mahler – in that order,' he wrote after her death.

European and American tours followed. Work piled up. Her recordings flew off the shelves. In a few short years she had gone from being a Lancashire telephonist with a dream of playing the piano, to one of the world's most celebrated singers. But her success was to prove short-lived. In 1951 she was diagnosed with the cancer which was ultimately to kill her. An immediate mastectomy followed, and then treatment by radiation. She bore it cheerfully, even noting that she was merely 'laid a bit low' when others undergoing the same treatment seemed to be 'knocked flat'.

After the treatment was completed, she continued to work and booked in a heavy schedule of oratorio concerts, recitals and recordings for 1952. Her final performance on stage, in the production of *Orfeo* already mentioned, took place in February 1953, and she died on 8 October of that year. Such was her popularity that some critics noted at the time that her passing had even dampened the

euphoric aftermath of the coronation of Elizabeth II.

Little is known of Ferrier's private life, beyond the fact that she was enormously gregarious and loved the company of friends. There was a short, unsuccessful marriage, which ended in an amicable divorce in 1947, and thereafter she seems to have lived for her work, even describing herself on one occasion as a 'lone she-wolf'. John Drummond, former controller of Radio 3 and producer of a film about Ferrier, writes in his book *Tainted by* Experience 'My own feeling is that there was no private life. Once she started singing professionally, at nearly thirty years of age, her work was her life. It was as if she then had to run fast to make up for lost time.'

That seems a likely proposition. As one can hear on these discs, she was a singer who gave her all in performance, and who held nothing back. She lived most fully when singing: and we can only rejoice that we have recordings like these to keep the memory of such a great talent alive.
