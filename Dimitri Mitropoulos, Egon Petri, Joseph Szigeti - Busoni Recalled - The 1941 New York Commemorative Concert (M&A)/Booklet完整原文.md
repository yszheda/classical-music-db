# Ferruccio Busoni
## Works for Piano and Orchestra

# Busoni the Composer
by Doriana Attili

**"... that extraordinary cold white fire of intellectualized and sublimated emotion, emotion so great, so intense, and at once so intellectualized and sublimated ..."**
— K. S. Sorabji (Around Music, 1932)

Ferruccio Busoni was born in Empoli (near Florence) in 1866. A musical prodigy, he was already touring as a pianist at a very young age. He met Brahms in Vienna, Grieg, Mahler, and Tchaikovsky in Leipzig, and Sibelius in Helsinki, where he also met Gerda Sjöstrund, whom he married in Moscow, where he turned down a professorship at the Moscow Conservatory in order to travel to America. He taught at the New England Conservatory in Boston from 1891 to 1894, then returned to Europe and settled in Berlin. Excepting the war years 1915-20, which he spent in Switzerland, Berlin remained his home until his death in 1924.

Through the years, Busoni's compositional style underwent many changes; one can find elements of late Romanticism, Impressionism, and even the "matter-of-factness" characteristic of twentieth-century music. In his later works, his musical language suggests a highly integrated synthesis of these different styles, in keeping with his concept of the "Unity of Music." Busoni's aesthetic positions have often been judged inconsistent: for example, his interest in the atonal music of Arnold Schoenberg, which influenced such works as the seven *Elegien* (1907-9) and *Sonatina seconda* (1912) for piano, and the *Nocturne symphonique*, Op. 43 (1912), seems to contradict his objection to the "novelty for novelty's sake" of early Expressionism. Busoni's work as a composer might be interpreted as a "reaction," as the self-defense of a hyper-sensitive artist who has had to wait too long for the universal recognition he deserved. Though he was to some extent influenced by the music of his day, he remained an artist who retained a purity and dignity of style in the face of contemporary trends.

Neglecting to keep in mind the apparent contradictions in Busoni's life and works can lead to misleading interpretations of his works—as, indeed, was the case with many of his contemporaries, who were confused by the heterogeneity of his ideas. Schoenberg, for example, agreed with many of Busoni's convictions about the future of music, yet at the same time recognized a tension between Busoni's "modern" convictions and his "traditional" compositional means. Busoni, in turn, believed that Schoenberg had failed to make advances in terms of piano writing, and so he did not hesitate to "improve" the second of Schoenberg's *Drei Klavierstücke*, Op. 11 (1909), by making an effective concert transcription of it, in 1910. Busoni opposed the use of new compositional practices merely for the sake of novelty, and in realizing his own goal of enriching the musical language, especially for the piano, he often adapted musical techniques of the past. From childhood he had absorbed a vast legacy of musical knowledge and experience, and was heir to a musical heritage on which, he felt, it was his right to draw.

The first evidence of Busoni's interest in American Indians is found in a letter to his wife dated March 22, 1910, in which he mentions one of his former American pupils, the New York-born ethnomusicologist Natalie Curtis, who had compiled a standard anthology of Indian music, *The Indians' Book*. Busoni, for his part, referred to American Indians as "the only cultured people who will have nothing to do with money, and who dress the most everyday things in beautiful words." His first completed compositions on this subject were the two books of the *Indianisches Tagebuch* [Indian Diary]—the first a set of four studies for piano, the second a study for small orchestra titled *Gesang vom Reigen der Geister* [Song of the Spirits' Dance]. Both books were composed in 1915 and published the following year.

Drawing on Indian melodies taken directly from Curtis's book, Busoni began the *Indianische Fantasie* für Klavier mit Orchestra in the summer of 1913, but did not complete it until 1915; he gave the first performance himself, in Zurich, in 1916, and achieved greater success with it in London, in 1920. He had difficulties developing the themes and organizing the structure of this work, difficulties reflected in his indecision about naming it: he first chose the title *Concerto Secondo*—(*Fantasia, Canzone e Finale su dei motivi delle Pelli-Rosse*)—per Pianoforte con Orchestra, then considered the title *Indianische Suite*, and even the English title *Indian Rhapsody*, before making up his mind.

The *Indianische Fantasie* marks a significant stylistic departure from the *Piano Concerto*, Op. 39, or the *Concertstück*. Though Busoni was preparing his edition of Book II of Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* when he composed it, he rejected a highly contrapuntal style, and instead searched for a form in which harmony and rhythm were subservient to melody, as in his *Berceuse élégiaque* for orchestra, Op. 42 (1909), and his *Nocturne symphonique*. In 1912, at a time when many musicians favored

Expressionistic as opposed to melodic writing, Busoni wrote that he was concentrating on the role of "pure melody, a sequence of ascending and descending intervals that are organized and move rhythmically. The sequence has harmony latent within itself, and reflects a kind of feeling. And all this can exist without expressive guide-lines drawn from a text, and without vocal accompaniment. ... The melody, at first independent, eventually unites with the accompanying harmony and later becomes inseparably linked with it. Recently the aim of polyphonic music (which is always in progress) has been to free itself from this unity. In contradiction to deep-rooted convictions, melody, within polyphony, should be permitted to continuously expand, to grow in size and capacity of expression, and so become the most powerful element in the composition."

In the *Indianische Fantasie*, dance rhythms alternate with slow laments, grandiose themes with barbaric passages. The orchestral instruments are often treated sectionally, thus avoiding the mixing and merging of individual timbres. Busoni introduces several themes in succession, with highly original, polytonal harmonies, then elaborates them while conserving their original character. The piano part is almost symphonic in character, with cadenzas used as the structural means for introducing new musical material. Busoni wrote in 1913 that "I have arrived at a critical moment in my work, where many ideas have need of form"; in the case of the *Indianische Fantasie*, that form divides into three principal sections that are linked by piano cadenzas:

(I) The introduction (Andante con moto, quasi marcia) is built up from a two-bar fragment in a march style, characterized by a pedal-point. The fantasy proper begins with the entrance of the piano, with a syncopated and clearly articulated theme. An Adagio fantastico section, with a new four-note theme supported by heavy chords, is followed by a contrasting Allegretto affettuoso section, then a Piu mosso, with a bellicose seven-bar theme accompanied by hammered-out octaves in the piano. The end of this theme is transformed into a lyrical fragment for the orchestra, leading to the cadenza, which develops the material of the Allegretto affettuoso.

(II) The middle section of the *Indianische Fantasie* is the most conventional. The piano soloist presents a song in G major (Andante, quasi lento), alternating with the orchestra. The Allegro sostenuto serves as a bridge to the Andantino maestoso, which is built on an orchestral theme enriched by arpeggios in the piano part.

(III) In the Vivamente, a barbaric dance rhythm, used as an ostinato, is blared out by the horns, then taken up by the winds, while the piano plays full chords. In the Animato section, the various themes intermingle, in a savage rhythm, leading to the conclusion in C major.

According to his biographer, Anthony Beaumont, Busoni discovered Mozart's opera *Idomeneo* only in 1918. Busoni found that *Idomeneo* presented an entirely different kind of music drama than *Figaro*, with which he had long been familiar, and he studied these differences with great interest. Also, the libretto of *Idomeneo* aroused his curiosity, as it reminded him of the Biblical story of Jephtha whose symbolic meanings had attracted him. In 1918 Busoni arranged a concert suite from *Idomeneo* consisting of the Overture, the Sacrificial Scene and the Festal March from the opera, and it is this arrangement of the Overture we hear on the present recording. The work was published in 1919, dedicated to Othmar Schoeck.

The *Sarabande and Cortège* Op. 51 (*Two studies for Doktor Faust*), also known as the 5th and 6th Elegies for Orchestra, were composed between December 1918 and January 1919 and dedicated to Volkmar Andreae, who gave the first performance at the Zürich Tonhalle on 31 March 1919, conducting the Tonhalle Orchestra.

As Anthony Beaumont points out in his excellent *Busoni the Composer*, Busoni wrote his masterpiece *Doktor Faust* piecemeal, with the earliest musical ideas dating back to 1912. Passages in such piano works as *Fantasia Contrappuntistica*, the *Sonatina seconda*, the fifth *Sonatina* and other piano works later found their way into the opera. By the autumn of 1918 large portions of the score of the opera were completed, including the last entrance of Faust. Busoni's imagination was now directed to the closing scene in which Faust is victorious over the devil and magically transfers his spirit to another body. The *Sarabande* is the musical embodiment of the culmination of Faust's spiritual quest. The score is headed with a mysterious symbol of the extremes of our dualistic world which Faust transcends in the final scene. Beaumont calls the *Sarabande* "one of Busoni's most sublime compositions. Like the *Berceuse élégiaque*, it is a mediation on death and rebirth. The traditional rhythm of the Sarabande, two slow beats and one fast...marches relentlessly through the piece at a stately tempo, leaving no doubt in the listener's mind — whatever the inner symbolism of the score — that a profound and solemn moment is being unfolded."

The language of the Sarabande, compared to the pre-war works, is less dynamic and lacking in the extremes of dissonance or rhythmic freedom found in such earlier works as *Sonatina seconda* and *Nocturne symphonique*. Busoni himself characterized the sound he produced during this period as "abstract, 'inevitable,'... but not 'spasmodic': thus generally reflective and reserved."

As a companion piece to the Sarabande Busoni gathered from the score of the opera a number of passages into a *Cortège* during the month of January 1919.

The *Cortège*, which uses a full orchestra, begins with a Polonaise which is followed by a solemn trio in two sections. In the words of Beaumont, Busoni "chose to seek harmonies which themselves are ambiguous, whose content can be sensed as a mixture of joy and sorrow...By maintaining a careful control of the harmony (as well as the orchestration), he breathes a sense of uneasiness into the outwardly festive character of the music."

Busoni himself thought that these two sketches represented the pinnacle of his art, a view not shared by either his critics or his public, which remained on the whole unsympathetic and unmoved by these pieces.

The *Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 35a* was composed in 1896-97 and was first performed on 8 October 1897 in Berlin at the Singakademie, with Henri Petri, violin and the Berlin Philharmonic conducted by Busoni.

Anthony Beaumont provides the following analysis of the work: "The three movements of the Violin Concerto correspond to classical convention. Their inner structures, however, which are almost bereft of literal recapitulations, are more complex. Themes are transformed from movement to movement in ingeniously secretive ways — no blatant cyclic methods, no inflation of motto themes into glorious apotheoses, but a much more subtle art...A mixture of Latin and Nordic elements, a tendency to secretive handling of thematic material, a certain fleetingness, an elegiac, melancholy tone alternating with positively plebeian humor — all these facets of the later Busoni are jumbled happily together in the Violin Concerto, but not yet integrated."

The pianist Egon Petri, son of the violinist Henri Petri, first met Busoni as a child in the mid-1880's when the composer had moved to Leipzig and became a friend of his father (as well as of Mahler, Delius, and Adolf Brodsky). Eventually Petri took a few piano lessons from Busoni. More than a pupil, he became a lifelong exponent of Busoni's works, as well as a friend and confidante. In 1910 Busoni heard and was greatly impressed by a performance in Basel of his monumental piano concerto by Petri (a broadcast performance of this work was recorded in 1936 by RRG, the German radio system; unfortunately the discs have not survived, although the recording of the Finale only from an earlier broadcast is still extant). Petri gave the first performance, with Busoni, in 1921 of the latter's *Fantasia Contrappuntistica* for two pianos and arranged or edited a number of works by Busoni for publication. The composer's correspondence with Petri is an invaluable resource for insights into Busoni's creative processes.

Violinist Joseph Szigeti devotes quite a few pages in his autobiography, *With Strings Attached*, to the profound influence Busoni exercised on his artistic and cultural development. Around 1912 Szigeti played the Violin Concerto to Busoni who by that time had come to regard it as an immature creation; Szigeti's interpretation convinced him that the work was still viable. Thereafter Szigeti performed the work often and in fact this Concerto has remained one of Busoni's most popular works. Szigeti also frequently programmed Busoni's second sonata for violin and piano.

Conductor Dimitri Mitropoulos was a young composer flirting with serialism in Berlin in 1920 when a friend introduced him to the pianist Egon Petri, to whom Mitropoulos played his newly composed *Greek Sonata*, a rhapsodic, passionate work. Petri then introduced him to Busoni. Mitropoulos quickly became a member of Busoni's inner circle and frequented Busoni's house where he met some of the outstanding luminaries of the day such as Kurt Weill, Vladimir Vogel, Phillip Jarnach and many others over the next few years. The late American composer Otto Luening recalls: "Busoni was more than a mentor — he was an *animator*...he would size you up and then give you support as an artist whether you were a pianist or a composer. He was interested in your personality, and if there was a seed in your personality that could be developed, that's what he went for. He did that with everybody who came into contact with him and he did, I'm sure, have a terrific influence on Mitropoulos." (Quoted in *Priest of Music: the Life of Dimitri Mitropoulos*, by William R. Trotter, © 1995 Amadeus Press, p.46)
