## LINER NOTES by Ingrid Tray Plonzo

### EARL WILD

*"When Earl Wild performs, the Golden Age of the keyboard suddenly reappears."* — TIME Magazine

Earl Wild is a pianist in the grand Romantic tradition. His legendary career, as distinguished and long, has continued for well over 70 years. Born in 1915, in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, Earl Wild's technical accomplishments are often likened to what those of Liszt himself must have had. Born with absolute pitch he started playing the piano at three. Having studied with great teachers such as Egon Petri, his lineage can be traced back to Schvarotka, Busoni, Ravel, d'Albert and Liszt himself.

Earl Wild's career is dotted with musical legends. As a young pianist he was soloist with Arturo Toscanini and the NBC Symphony. Since then he has performed with virtually every major conductor and symphony orchestra in the world. Rachmaninov was an important idol to him. It's been said of Earl Wild, "He's the incarnation of Rachmaninov, Livecque and Rosenthal rolled into one!" In 1986 after hearing him play three sold-out Carnegie Hall concerts, devoted to Liszt, honoring the centenary of that composer's death, one critic said, "I find it impossible to believe that he played those million notes with 70-year-old fingers, so fresh-sounding and precise were they. Perhaps he has a worn-out lip up in his attic, à la Oscar Wilde's Picture of Dorian Gray."

He's one of the few American pianists to have achieved international as well as domestic celebrity. He has performed for six Presidents of the United States, beginning with Herbert Hoover, and in 1939, was the first classical pianist to give a recital on the new medium of television. At fourteen he was performing in the Pittsburgh Symphony with Otto Klemperer as well as working at radio station KDKA, where he played many of his own compositions. As a virtuoso pianist, composer, transcriber, conductor, critic and teacher, Mr. Wild continues in the style of the legendary great artists of the past.

In addition to his distinguished concert career, which encompasses performances with conductors such as Stokowski, Reiner, Munch, Szell and Mitropoulos, and artists like Callas, Tourel, Pons, Melchior, Peerce and Rumbay, Wild successfully shines as both a conductor and composer. His Easter oratorio, Resurrexit, was broadcast by the ABC network in 1962 and again in 1964. Wild's recent composition, Variations on a Theme of Stephen Foster for piano and orchestra ("The-Fah"), premiered with Wild as soloist with the Des Moines Symphony Orchestra in 1992.

In 1986 Mr. Wild was awarded the Liszt Medal by the People's Republic of Hungary in recognition of his long and devoted association with the music of Franz Liszt. Also in 1986, a TV documentary entitled Wild About Liszt, filmed at Wynyard, the Marquess of Londonderry's family home, received the British Television Award for best musical documentary.

Liszt is a composer who has been closely associated with Mr. Wild throughout his long career. His first performing all Liszt recitals for over forty years, in New York City in 1961, he gave a monumental all Liszt recital celebrating the 150th anniversary of Liszt's birth. In 1986, honoring the 100th year of Liszt's death, he gave a series of three recitals entitled "Liszt the Poet," "Liszt the Transcriber," and "Liszt the Virtuoso," in New York's Carnegie Hall and other recital halls of the world. Championing composers such as Liszt long before they were fashionable, is part of Mr. Wild's tradition on which Mr. Wild has built his long and successful career.

Mr. Wild is one of today's most recorded pianists, having made his first disc in 1938 for RCA. In 1958, he has recorded with 21 of the world's record labels, resulting in a discography that includes more than 33 piano concertos, 14 chamber works, and 600 solo piano pieces.

Today at 85, Mr. Wild continues to record and perform concerts throughout the world. He has been called "the finest transcriber of our time," and his many piano transcriptions are widely known and respected. In 1997, he was a Grammy® Award for his disc, "The Romantic Master" - Virtuoso Piano Transcriptions. Praised by critics and music lovers around the world for his "stunning document of musical sensitivity and virtuosity" and "a tribute to America's greatest pianistic treasure" - this CD is once again available in its original HDCD state-of-the-art audiophile sound on Ivory Classics [70097].

Along with the release of over 20 other CDs in the last 10 years, when he was 79, Earl Wild recorded a well received Beethoven disc which included the monumental Hammerklavier Sonata, as well as another disc comprised of Rachmaninov's Preludes and the Second Piano Sonata. Earl Wild is an exclusive Ivory Classics® artist. In celebration of his 85th birthday, Ivory Classics released 20th and 21st Century Piano Sonatas [71005], which includes Mr. Wild's own Piano Sonata (2000).

### FRANZ LISZT

*"In Franz Liszt we have not only the most important figure among pianists in the nineteenth century, but a universal genius, who summed up in himself the whole development of piano playing since the invention of the instrument."* — Edward Burlingame Hill (1872-1960), American composer and teacher.

Franz Liszt's influence on the 19th century was overwhelming and through his numerous students, his strong musical presence influenced piano playing into the 20th century. He developed piano playing not only through his own compositions, but also through his teaching. His compositions influenced many other composers. Additionally, Liszt helped promote the works of many composers during his years as a touring virtuoso. Later, in his position as head of the Weimar orchestra and opera house, he was an important influence on contemporary tastes, tirelessly promoting the works of his contemporaries. In his later years, Liszt did his best to champion the works of the newer Russian school of composers, including Borodin and Mussorgsky. Numerous other composers — Grieg, Smetana, Chaminade, and of course Wagner, have copiously written about the value they placed on Liszt's moral encouragement as an important aid in their careers. As one of Liszt's most illustrious students, Moriz Rosenthal (1862-1946) once wrote, "When one was with Liszt, one felt the power of his overwhelming personality..."

Franz Liszt (1811-1886) was a graceful subject for biographers, factual and fictional. He possessed every feature of a romantic personage, as we of the 21st century are apt to picture the great personalities of the 19th century. He had a brilliant beginning as a child prodigy; he was kissed on his brow by Beethoven and received the composer's greatest students. At Czerny as a youth he was the prince of pianists and the leading artistic figure in European capitals at the time when Europeans were, according to our dearest beliefs, pious and preoccupied with glamorous rather work or war; he flew flowing hair and had a wild appearance about him; he loved women, and women loved him; and in his middle age, became an Abbé, as some singers do in romantic novels. Liszt wrote music with expressive and meaningful titles, often with a poem for an epigraph; and he was unquestionably, with Wagner, the co-author of the "music of the future," as designated by the despairing conservatives for its quality of hugeness of design and grandiloquence of idiom.

As a pianist, Liszt was unique (in the correct use of the word). According to Felix Mendelssohn (1809-1847), "Liszt possesses a sort of velocity and complete command of fingers, and a thoroughly musical feeling, which cannot be called. In a word, I have heard no performer whose ideas are capable of being projected to the listener and that he could illustrate such imaginary without words. This was his lifelong aesthetic. Liszt transcribed about 150 songs. In addition, he was transcribing for piano the complete symphonies of Beethoven, numerous works by Berlioz, Wagner, Rossini, Weber, and others. Virtually every musician of that era wrote gratefully for Liszt's transcription skill. This set an incredibly high standard that was the lifeline for virtuosos' transcription, and it is this standard that was the influencing factor and guideline for virtuoso pianists who were and continue to follow.

The lied is, poetically as well as musically, an intrinsic product of the Germanic Music." wrote Liszt's student Moritz von Beckerath on Robert Franz. Liszt's own song settings prove his own hypothesis. There are 57 settings of German texts, 11 French songs, 5 Italian, 3 Hungarian, one English and one Russian. **Die Loreley** (S532/R289) [Disc 2, 5] was originally cast in November 1841. Liszt at a text by Heinrich Heine (1797-1856). It is in a posturing of the original German folksong/rhapsody/ballad, reminiscent of Rheinmaid voices strengthen the personal experience, beginning with the lines:

> I know not what it means
> That I should be so sad;
> A legend of long ago
> Gives me no peace of mind.

The piano transcription by Liszt of this song was published in 1861 and another version for voices and orchestra was written between 1859 and 1873 and orchestrated posthumously, these are compositions of a lifetime — the product of continuing inspiration, and the reflection of Chopin's very soul. Liszt first met Chopin in 1831, immediately after Chopin's arrival in Paris. Their association was unhappy at best, and often flawed by misunderstandings and jealousy. It was Liszt who introduced George Sand to Chopin, resulting in a questionable and difficult relationship. When Chopin died, Liszt was by chance on an assignment. Thus he was in the perfect position to have discovered later and of which he did not approve. After Chopin's death, Liszt showed abominable plans in publishing a terrible biography of Chopin. The result was scandalous and led to digressions and misinformation. Today most musicologists agree that the book was the handiwork of Liszt's mistress, Carolyne Wittgenstein. Be that as it may, among Liszt's song transcriptions are by Chopin. They are some of Liszt's most popular and enduring compositions. The songs in this recital were created by Liszt during a period of thirteen years, from 1847 to 1860 and dedicated to Princess Marie von Hatzfeldt-Schillingsfürst (also known as Princess Marie von Sayn-Wittgenstein, daughter of Princess Caroline von Sayn-Wittgenstein). The fifth song transcription, **"Meine Freuden" ["Mine Joys"] [Disc 2, 7]**, is the only one in Polish. This gentle expression of love not only is the most beautiful, but a look from her is enough to set one alight, and her sweet caress raises the pleasure of taking her in his arms and wildly kissing her... in a mazurka rhythm.

Liszt's favorite works of literature, he let it be known, were Goethe's Faust and Dante's Divine Comedy. It is therefore not surprising that Franz Liszt had a profound love for the diabolical. In addition to his Totentanz, his Faust Symphony, his transcription for piano of Berlioz's Symphonie Fantastique, he composed also three pieces with the devil as a focal point. The first of these are the five **Mephisto Waltzes** (the fifth is actually entitled Bagatelle sans tonalité) and the **Mephisto Polka** (S217/R39) [Disc 2, 8]. Composed in 1883, the year before Liszt's death, this one is a tonal regent. Highly ascetic, in chromaticism at times border on the polytonal. This work is not a picture of a bellicose demon, capering dancing to the macabre strains of ghastly music, but rather an old composer being wry, sarcastic, full of irony and devilish.

The six short pieces which Liszt published in 1850 under the title **Consolations** had a literary inspiration. According to scholars, Liszt was familiar with a collection of poetry called Consolation by historian and poet Joseph Delorme (pseudonym by Charles Sainte-Beuve (1804-1869), which Liszt read shortly after its publication in 1830. Although Liszt began the set in the first half of the 1840s, it was not until the first half of 1849 that the ideas had been worked out. The three Consolations Nos. 4 and 5 were composed in Weimar, No. 6 in Rome. When the complete set of six Consolations was published in 1850, Liszt sent a copy to Carolyne Wittgenstein, with these words: "Here are six consolations, which I hope will bring you some comfort. They are the expression of my feelings for you."

The **Dante Sonata** [Disc 1, 1] is not a transcription but an original work. Liszt started working on it in 1837 and completed it in 1849. It is one of his most ambitious works. The Sonata is dedicated to Liszt's friend and future son-in-law, Hans von Bülow. The work is a musical journey through Dante's Inferno and Paradiso. The opening theme represents the gates of Hell, while the lyrical second theme represents Beatrice. The work culminates in a fugue representing the divine light of Paradise.

The **Sonetto 47 del Petrarca** [Disc 1, 2] is based on Petrarch's Sonnet 47 ("Benedetto sia il giorno"). The music captures the poet's adoration of his beloved Laura. The **Sonetto 104 del Petrarca** [Disc 1, 3] expresses the poet's anguish and despair. The **Sonetto 123 del Petrarca** [Disc 1, 4] is a meditation on love and death.

The **Ballade No.2 in B minor** (S171/R44) [Disc 1, 6] was composed in 1853. It is a work of epic proportions, with dramatic contrasts and technical demands. The ballade has been compared to a tone poem for piano, with its narrative structure and emotional depth.

The **Liebesträume** (Dreams of Love) are three solo piano compositions published in 1850. Each piece is based on a poem by a different German poet. No. 2 is based on "Seliger Tod" (Blessed Death) by Ferdinand Freiligrath. No. 3 is based on "O lieb, so lang du lieben kannst!" (Love as long as you can love!) by Ferdinand Freiligrath.

**Un Sospiro** (A Sigh) is the third of Liszt's Three Concert Etudes (S144). It is one of Liszt's most popular and beloved works, known for its arpeggiated accompaniment that flows like water.

The **Bach/Liszt: Fantasia and Fugue in G minor** is a transcription of Bach's organ work. Liszt was a great admirer of Bach's music and made numerous transcriptions of his works.

The **Sonata in B minor** (S178/R21) is one of Liszt's greatest achievements. Completed in 1853, it is a work of extraordinary depth and complexity. The sonata is in one continuous movement, with themes that are transformed throughout. It is considered one of the greatest piano sonatas ever written.

**Die Loreley** is based on the German legend of the Lorelei, a siren who lures sailors to their deaths. Liszt's transcription captures the haunting beauty of the original song.

**Funérailles** (Funeral rites) is the seventh piece in Liszt's collection Harmonies poétiques et religieuses. It was composed in 1849 as a tribute to Liszt's compatriots who died during the Hungarian Revolution. The middle section is a recreation of a funeral march.

The **Mephisto Polka** is one of Liszt's later works, showing his wit and humor. It is a virtuosic showpiece that requires considerable technical skill.

**Consolation No.3 in D flat Major** is the most famous of the six Consolations. It is a simple, song-like piece with a beautiful melody.

### Après une lecture du Dante (Fantasia quasi Sonata) [Disc 1, 1]

Liszt's *Après une lecture du Dante* (Fantasia quasi Sonata), which is often entitled *Dante Sonata*, was begun in 1837 and completed twelve years later in 1849. The final work is the seventh book (Italy) of the *Années de Pèlerinage* (Years of Pilgrimage), the complete edition of which was published in 1858. According to Liszt biographer Peter Raabe, and collector Hans Bunge, the *Dante Sonata* was originally intended as the first movement of a three-movement work that Liszt abandoned. The *Dante Sonata* [Disc 1, 1] has no programmatic text, but it is clear that his reactions to the "strange tongues, terrible words, tones of pain, and accents of anger" which Dante described in his *Inferno*. Liszt first sketched his *Après une lecture du Dante* ("After Reading Dante") in 1839, conceiving the work soon after reading the *Inferno* section of Dante's *Divine Comedy* (1307-1321). The work was inspired by Dante's "Divine Comedy." So impressed with Dante's writings was Liszt that he would later name his illegitimate son Daniel after the great Florentine poet.

Beethoven's influence: Ingeniously based on three basic ideas, the *Dante Sonata* begins with jarring, descending octave octaves (known as *thema diabolica*), and we hear these tritones in a less-dissonant interval as Liszt introduces the "Pater noster" (the Lord's Prayer). The second basic idea heard in the work is a grim, chromatic descending figure played in groups of two repeated octaves in the right hand that alternates with dark-colored chords in the left hand. For the final basic idea, Liszt introduces the subject in form of a sharp motive counterbalancing the subject. Held with the low bell is evoked. The rising figures bring us to the end and "Paradiso."

Liszt's *Three Petrarch Sonnets* (S161/R10b) began life as songs composed in 1838-9 before their magical transcription into ardent piano pieces in 1858 and inclusion in the second book (Italy) of the *Années de Pèlerinage* ("Years of Pilgrimage"). Based on Francesco Petrarca's (1304-1374) love poems, "Pace non trovo" [I find no peace], the *Sonetto 47 del Petrarca* [Disc 1, 2] is a triumph of Romanza-aria traits (peace and war, hate and love, grief and laughter, and death and life); *Sonetto 104 del Petrarca* [Disc 1, 3] is based on the Petrarchan sonnet "Amor, che mi fai tu?" [Love, what do you do to me?], an ardent lyric written by Petrarch to his beloved Laura (much-adored during the artist's lifetime); and the final *Petrarchan Sonata* [Disc 1, 4], based on "I' vidi in terra angelici costumi" [I beheld on earth angelic grace]. In this piece we hear Liszt and his passionate emotions for Italy and Italian art, more of all Liszt and his passionate emotions with genuine poetic feeling.

The years 1835 to 1837 are generally recognized as Liszt's period of transcendental emotion, during which time he gave well over a thousand concerts throughout most of Western Europe, Turkey, Poland and Russia. The *First Year (Suisse)* of the three collections entitled *Années de Pèlerinage* (Years of Pilgrimage) was begun in 1835. During the early 1840s Liszt traveled as he was the first flowering of a more introspective approach to composition, moving gradually away from the scintillating pyrotechnics of his previous compositions. The second year was completed in 1858. Between 1867 and 1877 he completed his third set in the series, a more austere and impersonal group of works reflective of the manner in which Liszt was changing. Published in 1883 and four of the seven pieces are music of mourning or lamentation. *Les jeux d'eau à la Villa d'Este* (Fountains of the Villa d'Este) [Disc 1, 5] was one of the first of the fountained glory and gaiety of a young man from Liszt's time, of life itself preferred the music with a fragment from the Gospel of St. John: "But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst."

### Ballade No.2 in B minor (S171/R16) [Disc 1, 6]

The *Ballade No.2 in B minor* (S171/R16) [Disc 1, 6] was composed in 1853. It is a work of epic proportions, with dramatic contrasts and technical demands. The work is one of Liszt's most challenging works for the pianist. In his famous book on Liszt, Ernest Newman makes this statement concerning the ballades: "No works of Liszt's are more characteristic of him, none show more clearly the two sides of his nature — the reflective, philosophic side and the side that rejoices in the purely musical, in the sensuous, in the color and pageantry of sound."

### Die Loreley [Disc 2, 5]

The title *Loreley* — impassioned love songs without words. Yet, they began their existence as songs with words. Liszt published them in 1850 with the title *Drei Lieder für eine Singstimme*. All this leads to a preternatural finale of startling splendor. There is *nothing* more exciting in the literature of the piano. It is brilliantly captivating, and Liszt the Magnificent is stamped on every bar!

Franz Liszt was born a Roman Catholic and all his life, life never wavered in that commitment. In a letter written to Joseph d'Ortigue (1802-1866), a scholar specializing in the history and praxis of the Catholic Church, Liszt wrote, "In the depths of my being I feel myself a Christian and I pray joyfully (avec allégresse) I arise under the benevolent gaze of our master Jesus Christ, our Savior, at a trumpet to call the children of God... I love with my church out of love demands of a man... I shall not put that which God has joined, so shall I never agree to sever the union between the arts; and at the time with the essence of eternity, art in its highest manifestation — I shall not cease to be a musician as I increasingly become a Christian. Quiet certain though I hope just thus to be to my music-conscience and so to fulfill my artistic task with increasing power..." Liszt continues: "I am fully resolved to lead a life that is becoming to a Christian... I admit that he felt 'a mysterious feeling which has pierced my entire life as with a sacred wound.' Yes, Jesus Christ, God made man, yearning longing after the Cross and the raising of the Cross... Yes, this was my true inner calling..."

Throughout his life, Liszt's central struggle of his being was fought on religious lines. Since before he was a boy he had hoped to enter the priesthood, but this was not to be. And it is his music that gives proof of the great spiritual aspirations. Liszt created an astonishing quantity of religious works, not only for chorus but also for the piano. His most famous piano cycle is a set of ten pieces entitled *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses*. The title of this cycle was taken from a group of Alphonse de Lamartine (1790-1869) poems published in 1830. Liszt sketched only one of the piano pieces (which he eventually became the third in the set) in 1845. The rest of the pieces took form between 1847 and 1852. He published the collection in 1853.

Liszt had a very close friendship with his former student, Princess Carolyne von Sayn-Wittgenstein. They developed a deep friendship with Jeanne-Elisabeth Carolyne (Princess Sayn-Wittgenstein (1819-1887)) during his last year of touring in 1848. She followed Liszt from Russia to Weimar, eventually becoming his final partner in Rome. The Princess was instrumental in getting Liszt to concentrate on the *Eternal Wisdom of the Church*. Liszt's *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses* is dedicated to her.

### Funérailles [Disc 2, 6]

The *Funérailles* (Funeral rites) [Disc 2, 6] is the seventh piece in Liszt's *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses*. The title of the first sketch of the work (located at the Goethe und Schiller Archiv in Weimar) is dated October 1849. The title of the first sketch of the work is in reference by Liszt to the tragic events following the failure of 1848-1849 Hungarian War of Independence. The Austrian imperial and military courts put to death Count Louis Batthyány (the president of the first free Hungarian government) and sixteen officers from the lead-in Hungarian War of Independence. At this time Liszt lost some of his best and dearest friends. The sad events in his homeland tormented him deeply. He wrote: "I take hold of that strong and determined mind that has so often supported my spirit, which is certainly destined still for better days to come. Oh Lord, how distant fainter the sounds! By friends unbroken I lay great, what are you doing? What has become me close to you... Why does a harsh destiny keep me far away?" Swiss-German composer Joachim Raff (1822-1882), who befriended Liszt, helped the widowed Princess Carolyne to raise her son from her first marriage.

Liszt has been deeply moved by the loss of his best Hungarian friends... For many years many musicians and critics insisted that *Funérailles* had been occasioned by Chopin's death (who died only a few days after Carl Czerny's death) but this was not the case. Although Liszt was doubtless unequivocally refused the suggestion that this work had anything to do with Chopin's death, and the work is not an homage to Chopin, it has been a Chopin-inspired composition in a sense, it is a patriotic homage to heroes (or perhaps, in a larger sense, a people) who go beyond his personal loss. The middle section is a recreation of a funeral march, and the work ends quietly rather than this has rarely been pointed.

Franz Liszt was also a master transcriber. Whether the melody was a simple folk song, a complex symphonic work, a lengthy chamber piece, an operatic aria, or a beautiful art-song, Liszt could not resist the urge to transcribe a piano version into a piano work. More than half of his compositions are transcriptions, arrangements, fantasies and paraphrases on other composers' works.

### Liebesträume No.3 in E flat Major [Disc 1, 8]

In the most celebrated of the three works, it is a musical picture of wonderful beauty and exquisite use of light and shadow, it paints a musical picture of love's highest fulfillment — in death, awakened by a kiss!

**Liebesträume No.3 in A flat Major** [Disc 1, 8] is the most celebrated of the three works. It is a musical picture of wonderful beauty and exquisite use of light and shadow, it paints a musical picture of love's highest fulfillment — in death, awakened by a kiss!

The *Ständchen* poem by Ferdinand Freiligrath (1810-1876) inspired the poem. Liszt provides the complete texts of the two poems of the Freiligrath's poem before the piano pieces: Liszt called his *Liebesträume nocturnes*. *Nocturne 1*, *music* full of warm evening colors.

**Liebesträume No.2 in E flat Major** [Disc 1, 7] is a setting of Uhland's *Seliger Tod* ("Blessed Death"):

> Gestorben war ich in Liebe wonne;
> Begraben lag ich in ihren Armen;
> Erwecket ward ich von ihrem Kuss;
> Von ihren Himmels sich In ihren Augen.

> I died in Love's bliss;
> I lay buried in her arms;
> I was awakened by her kisses;
> I saw heaven in her eyes.

Twice in the course of this impassioned work the melody is interrupted by a brief interlude between the verses, as it would seem, giving us a fleeting glimpse of the summer night with its subtle perfumes and vague whisperings. The work closes with a passage of soft, sweet, ecstatic harmonies, a sigh of content in the final fruition of love's desire.

### Concert Étude No.3 in D flat Major (S144/R5) [Disc 1, 9]

**Un Sospiro** [Disc 1, 9] is subtitled *Un sospiro* ("A Sigh"). Published in 1848 as part of Liszt's *Trois Études de Concert*, this piece is the most often performed of the set. This mood piece is beautifully melodious and soothing, containing no difficulty except in the flowing arpeggiated passages described in as a "ripple of sweet sadness that seems as if it might roll on long after it has passed by."

Notes about Franz Liszt's keyboard wizardry have been so numerous and frequently of such unbelievable nature, that it is always interesting to note what his contemporaries thought of his playing. English pianist Oscar Beringer (1844-1922) heard Liszt in 1870 and set down his impressions: "What cannot those fingers do on the piano! What incredible and unapproachable feats, those who know of his audience, men and women alike, affected to tears, when he chose to be pathetic; and what messages he had for them all from the pianissimo to the highest forte. Through the medium of his instrument he played upon every human emotion."

In musical respects Liszt was far in advance of his day. He, more than any of his contemporaries, understood the possibilities of the piano. Many people, dazzled by his European celebrity, saw him as a piano wizard rather than the brilliant composer, arranger, and conductor somewhat superficial and tainted by the same flamboyance. Whether this judgment was correct or not, Liszt had an extraordinary gift of advocacy. It was because of this, that he was widely regarded as a symbol of Romanticism.

His many interests, and particularly his passion for literature is quotation from a letter to his friend: "My mind and my fingers are working like two lost spirits the Bible. Locke, Plato, Byron, Hugo, Lamartine, Chateaubriand, Bach, Beethoven, Goethe are my daily companions, whom I meditate on them, devour them avidly. Beside this I practice the piano 6 to 8 hours... Liszt studied Bach, in fact knew most of Bach's keyboard works by heart by the time he was sixteen.

Liszt also learned to play the organ and became famous as proficient and virtuoso on the organ as the piano. He composed eleven organ works during his life time, including the *Bach: Fantasia and Fugue in G minor* (S463/R128). This work was originally composed by Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750) for organ. Liszt transcribed it for piano in 1852.

### Sonata in B minor (S178/R21) [Disc 2, 3-4]

One critic called the *Sonata in B minor* "Liszt's boldest experiment in original music for the piano alone." Why call it an experiment? Because, before the time of Liszt the composition, a sonata. It is in one continuous movement: its themes are not formally treated in the sonata-manner. It is, in effect, a symphonic tone poem, reduced in scale to the measure of the piano resources. Yet within this single movement one can discover the elements of the sonata, the leading characteristics of that form are fully maintained within the scope of the single movement, and, as one analyst pointed out, "Liszt's Sonata contains a more comprehensive and complex whole than could be obtained by these distinct and independent movements."

Richard Wagner (1813-1913), a student of Chopin's works, and professor of piano at the National Conservatory in New York, stated that the Sonata "is one of those compositions that does justice to the most beautiful of all arts." There is no doubt that the work is one of the most extreme transformations. The drama, the pathos, the sensuousness and rhetoric abound in a greater measure than Liszt could possibly put into a composition. But it hardly plays itself! The Sonata is virtuoso music at its best — a work that requires careful study and prodigious technique.

The *Sonata in B minor* [Disc 2, 3-4] opens in a portentous atmosphere with a motif which is encountered later on, from the opening a tonality which occurs several times. We have inspired Wagner's *leitmotif* for Wagner. This heavy descending scale passage is followed by a lyrical Lisztian melody of descending octaves passage leading to a third subject, a theme of an inspired and passionate nature. The work is developed from three themes. Konrad Wolff comments: "The development and recapitulation of the sonata with life itself," with all its highlights in its moments of repose and detachment, its emotional and spiritual involvement, ending in death and resurrection. Perhaps, Liszt had in mind German philosopher, theologian Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel's generally accepted view of the three stages of human thought, or in Hegel's words, "unfolds itself in the form of being different." It is certainly a useful way of examining a work that has a structure unlike any other written before or since [1848-9]. A grand theme of broad chord-like characterizes the *Andante sostenuto*. It is developed by transformation, with the grandiose and lyrical themes. Liszt uses many unusual techniques that builds up into a grandiose finale. For the famous critic and Liszt enthusiast, James Huneker, "The Sonata in B minor is the grandest specimen of pianoforte composition... a tremendous dramatic work; in it it sits the blood. It is intense. It is complex. The operatic is truly Lisztian... Power there is, sardonic power... Is there a composer who paints the infernal, the diabolic, the satanic, with such suggestion and power as Liszt? In the opening of the Sonata the question, now appears and proclaims the religious belief of the composer in dogmatic accents, and our convictions are swept away until after that outburst in C major... But the souls of siken alike is back of every bar: sensuous imagery, a plaint flame of infinity lurks in every cadence and the coda."

The piano transcription by Liszt of this song was published in 1861 and another version for voices and orchestra was written between 1859 and 1873 and orchestrated posthumously. These are compositions of a lifetime — the product of continuing inspiration, and the reflection of Chopin's very soul.

**Mephisto Polka** (S217/R39) [Disc 2, 8] is one of Liszt's later works, showing his wit and humor. It is a virtuosic showpiece that requires considerable technical skill.

**Consolation No.3 in D flat Major** (S172/R12) [Disc 2, 9] is the most famous of the six Consolations. It is a simple, song-like piece with a beautiful melody.

These pieces, Liszt achieved a precious cultivated elegance, choosing to write music simply to serve the melodic material. The six pieces were conceived as a unit (in fact, some to be played attaca) as suggested by the sequence of keys. The *Consolation No.3 in D flat* [Disc 2, 9] has long enjoyed great popularity as a piece on its own.
