## Andrew Rangell 介绍

**ANDREW RANGELL**

Long recognized as among our most elegant and insightful interpreters of the major keyboard works of Bach and Beethoven, pianist Andrew Rangell has drawn acclaim for a variety of recordings, ranging from the music of Sweelinck, Farnaby, and Gibbons to that of Janacek, Enescu, Nielsen, Bartók, Valen, Christian Wolff, and many others. The project *Fanciful Music* (Centaur), devoted to music of Froberger, Frescobaldi, and Forqueray, adds to a long list of distinctive performances that includes the Goldberg Variations, French Suites, Partitas, and Well-Tempered Clavier, Books 1 and 2, as well as the French Overture, Inventions, Sinfonias, and many shorter works.

Andrew Rangell made his New York debut as winner of the Merkin Award of the Concert Artists Guild, and has since performed and lectured throughout the United States, and in Europe and Israel. He has also taught on the faculties of Dartmouth, Heidelberg, and Tufts University. In 1976, already recognized as a distinctive recitalist and collaborative pianist, Mr. Rangell served on the Steinway-Bechstein, and the award of an Avery Fisher Career Grant – for his vivid traversal of the complete Beethoven sonatas, cycle in New York, Boston, Cleveland, Rochester, Denver, and other U.S. cities.

A harpsichord summit in 1991 forced Mr. Rangell gradually after the discovery of his career, and eventually to place his highest priority on recording. His recent previous releases: Bach 640, combines a short Bach recital with a DVD voice of Rangell's own chitchat, his book, Peter Pejansen. (Art and narration by Andy, soundtrack by Bach.)

## English Suites, BWV 806-811 — Andrew Rangell

Bach's English Suites were the first of three major collections of keyboard suites produced in succession – each set containing six works. The other sets are the French Suites (c.1725-22) and the Partitas (c.1725-31). A final suite, the so-called B minor French Overture, was added in 1735, completing Bach's unsurpassed contribution to the genre.

The designation "English Suites" was not Bach's. Lacking an autograph manuscript for these works, we have neither their original title nor the date of their composition. However, the English Suites were surely written sometime between 1715 and 1720. J.J. Frohlich wrote in 1802 that they were called "English" having been commissioned by an English nobleman. Even if this is somewhat fabled, given Bach's eminently admiral fusion of French, Italian, and German compositional elements in these suites, that they should be called "English" all six demonstrate the same succession of movements: prelude, allemande, courante, sarabande, and gigue – the group preceded by a pair of dances, in da capo arrangement, whose type varies from suite to suite. (Examples of these dances: bourrée, gavotte, minuet, passepied.) The first suite was probably written some time earlier than the others, and differs from them in two main respects. It lacks a large allegro prelude, instead offering a modest three-voice invention to begin with. Also, it presents an unusual constellation of courantes: two successive ones, immediately followed by two variations of the second. A documentary detail: the prelude's short opening flourish, a direct quotation from a suite of Charles Dieupart, whose set of six (published 1701) had earlier been copied out by Bach. A shout-out, in today's parlance!

By far the most conspicuous feature of the English Suites is their opening concerto-style allegro: imposing, virtuosic, and presenting an alternation of ritornello (tutti) and soloistic materials. It is worth noting that Bach had, not long before, made an immersive study of Italian, and especially Vivaldian, concerto form – with the consequence that ritornello structure became a recurrent feature in many different genres of his keyboard work. The grandeur and sweep of the English Suite preludes sets the tone for subsequent movements, imparting a sense of high seriousness, which is further promoted by contrapuntal, and other complexities. Many of the gigues exhibit fugal expositions, with the second section employing an inverted subject. Most of the allemandes feature exchanges of melodic material between hands, in imitative counterpoint. Several sarabandes include alternatively embellished versions, which one (the D minor's) includes a fully written out, and transformative, variation (called a "double"). Even the so-called galanteries (the optional movements inserted between the sarabande and gigue) often exhibit sophisticated contrapuntal writing. The overall feeling of carefully-achieved rightness and proportion in the distribution of each component to the whole unity – even at the level of individual phrase – is such that no single element seems "locally" fixed, and "seamed" procedural. Two ambitions, the E minor and E minor. The latter, in its enormous scope and harmonic daring, stands with Bach's greatest achievements in this form.

— Andrew Rangell

This photo, taken on the first day of the session, has particular meaning for me. We began late in the afternoon. My plan was to first record one complete suite per day, at whatever pace my mind and body allowed, the most intimate movements, including innumerable spontaneous decisions.
