## ENGLISH SUITES, BWV 806-811

Bach's English Suites were the first of three major collections of keyboard suites produced in succession – each set containing six suites. The other sets are the French Suites (c. 1720-22) and the Partitas (c. 1725-31). A final suite, the masterful B minor French Overture, was added in 1735, completing Bach's unsurpassed contribution to the genre.

The designation of "English" was not Bach's. Lacking an autograph manuscript for these works, we have neither their original title nor the date of their completion. However, the English Suites were likely written sometime between 1715 and 1720. J.N. Forkel wrote in 1802 that they were called "English" having been commissioned by an English nobleman. Even if so, it is somewhat ironic, given Bach's uniquely alchemical fusion of French, Italian, and German compositional elements in these suites, that they should be called "English."

All six demonstrate the same succession of movements: prelude, allemande, courante, sarabande, and gigue – the gigue preceded by a pair of dances, in da capo arrangement, whose type varies from suite to suite. (Examples of these dances: bourrée, gavotte, minuet, passepied.) The first suite was probably written some time earlier than the others, and differs from them in two main respects. It lacks a large allegro prelude, instead offering a modest three-voice sinfonia to begin with. Also, it presents an unusual concentration of courantes: two successive ones, immediately followed by two variations of the second. A tantalizing detail is the prelude's short opening flourish, a direct quotation from a suite of Charles Dieupart, whose set of six (published 1701) had earlier been copied out by Bach. A shout-out, in today's parlance!

By far the most conspicuous feature of the English Suites is their opening concerto-style allegro: imposing, virtuosic, and presenting an alternation of ritornello (tutti) and soloistic material. It is worth noting that Bach had, not long before, made an immersive study of Italian, and especially Vivaldian, concerto form – with the consequence that ritornello structure became a recurrent feature in many different genres of his subsequent work. The grandeur and weight of the English Suite preludes sets the tone for subsequent movements, imparting a sense of high seriousness, which is further promoted by contrapuntal, and other, complexities. Many of the gigues exhibit fugal expositions, with the second section employing an inverted subject. Most of the allemandes feature exchanges of melodic material between the hands, in imitative counterpoint. Several sarabandes include alternatively embellished versions, while one (the D minor) includes a fully written out, and transformative, variation (called a "double"). Even the so-called galanteries (the lighter optional movements inserted between sarabande and gigue) seem to have special weight. Lastly, there is present a feeling of carefully-achieved rightness and proportion in the contribution of each component to the whole unity – even if the later suites do possess a greater variety of dance forms. Two of the English Suites stand out for their special ambition, the E minor and D minor. The latter, in its enormous scope and harmonic daring, stands with Bach's greatest achievements in this form.

— Andrew Rangell

## Recording Note

This photo, taken on the first day of the session, has particular meaning for me. We began late in the afternoon. My plan was to first record all allemandes and sarabandes, the most intimate movements, involving innumerable spontaneous decisions. A favorable zone of expression, inspiration, and exploration was soon found, and seemed providential. Two hours in, I stopped and looked out the window. Tom Stephenson, the recording engineering, took the picture, very much an affirmation.
