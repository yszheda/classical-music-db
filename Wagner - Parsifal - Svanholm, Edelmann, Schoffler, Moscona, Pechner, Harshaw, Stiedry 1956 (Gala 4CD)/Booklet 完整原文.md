### 第 5 页 - HIPPO_20250107_0003.jpg (内页展开左)

Stiedry, she was born in Philadelphia in 1909. This American mezzo-soprano, later soprano, studied at the Juilliard School with Anna Schoen-René. After winning the Metropolitan Opera Auditions of the Air in 1941, she made her Metropolitan debut as the Second Norn in *Götterdämmerung* and subsequent seasons sang such roles as Azucena, Amneris and Mistress Quickly. At San Francisco (1944-57) her roles included Ulrica, Brangäne and Delbissy's *Geneviève*. During the 1950-51 season she changed to soprano parts, succeeding Helen Traubel in the heroic Wagnerian repertory (Isolde, Senta, Kundry and Brünnhilde) and remaining with the Metropolitan until the close of the 1963-64 season. During this period she also fulfilled engagements at Covent Garden (1955-6), Glyndebourne (notably as Donna Anna in 1954) and elsewhere. She was a convincing actress and possessed a good, though by no means great, Wagnerian voice; her tone was evenly produced through its wide range she came to be heard throughout the USA.

Kundry was sung by Margaret Harshaw.

### 第 8 页 - HIPPO_20250107_0006.jpg (文章页)

*Parsifal's history is inextricably bound up with Wagner's stipulation that for his last theatrical work, was only to be performed in his own custom-built Festspielhaus at Bayreuth. As time went on, the performance monopoly that Wagner had imposed, began to be questioned and after his death in 1883, a year after Parsifal's premiere, increasingly pressing questions were put to Cosima von Wagner concerning this question. (The composer himself had, in fact, led the way by selling the score to a publisher – for what use was so such an expensive score to a publisher if no one was allowed to print it!)*

An "illegal" first performance at the Metropolitan Opera on 24 December 1903 caused quite a stir at Bayreuth, but the problem was resolved once and for all by the argument that Bayreuth – particularly at that time – was a long way away for any American. This argument is of course completely contrary to that performances in America would not adversely affect attendances at Bayreuth's "own" Festspielhaus, and Wagner's heirs would not lose audience income.

European attempts to break the Bayreuth monopoly, however, met with a different response. In 1911 Bologna suddenly proposed all such attempts, and even made repeated petitions to the German parliament to grant an extension to the exclusive performing rights, that was to expire thirty years after the composer's death that, but at that moment – and much to Cosima's displeasure – already several other performances had been mounted, the first of these in 1905 by the Dutch Wagners Society in the Amsterdam Stadsschouwburg.

Cosima's further attempts to ban all the performances from any future appearances at Bayreuth which, given the strength of public opinion, the Wagners singers at that time, was an admission of defeat, and could not be long upheld.

But the question remains: why did Wagner want to restrict Parsifal exclusively to Bayreuth. Was it a financial matter, or did he regard this "Bühnenweihfestspiel" (Sacred Stage Festival Play) as a truly sacred work, that should only be performed in his own "temple". Many Wagnerians still believe the latter was the case, but would also prefer for the audience did not applaud, as is common in other countries with performances of Parsifal.

Matthew Passion. (In response to the uproar on this subject the Bayreuth Festival direction decided that – in accordance with the composer's instructions – there should be no applause after the first act, but that audiences should be free to applaud after the other two acts. The result is at Bayreuth a spontaneous outburst of applause at the end of Act I is invariably taken as a clear sign of disapproval, that spoils the atmosphere more than any applause would have done.)

There is, however, another possible answer. Wagner was always well aware of the strengths and weaknesses of his own works, and no amount of ideological, philosophical and psychological profundity can conceal the fact that Parsifal is a difficult opera to say in uneven "opera", particularly by nineteenth-century standards. For all the beauty of the music and the nobility of its aspirations, the work poses many dramatic problems. Kundry, a leading role, has hardly anything to sing during the first and third acts, but all that time she has very few words to sing, while Gurnemanz sings for a total of nearly 4 hours during the first and third acts, and does practically nothing – apart from narrating and sometimes commenting upon the deeds of others. Indeed very little happens on the stage during the entire opera, and this, coupled with the fact that the atmosphere is mostly determined by the orchestral music, makes it essential that the listener is not frightened of performances of the work falling under the general heading of "concert". Opera houses with mediocre orchestras, that are unable to produce a truly great Wagnerian sound. The public response to many of the performances given after 1913, mostly under abominable conducting, corroborates this view – and it is perfectly possible that, for that very reason, Wagner wished this work only to be performed under the best possible conditions. No one knew the conditions in Bayreuth better than he, and Parsifal is the stage: care and love for the work, and above all a good orchestra!

And indeed the conditions in Bayreuth are ideal for Parsifal: an orchestra that is made up of the best German musicians, a magnificent chorus, a generally unequalled team of

soloists and a dedicated public, all brought together there in an acoustic that is absolutely unique. Anyone who has sat in the darkened auditorium of the Festspielhaus and has felt themselves almost magically drawn into the first bars of the Prelude, has undergone a musical experience that is beyond compare. With its long melodic arches and countless orchestral moments, Parsifal is also the pre-eminent conductor's opera, and in that respect post-war Bayreuth enjoyed a golden age.

That performance Parsifal was sung by Set (Karl Viktor) Svanholm. This Swedish tenor was born in Västerås in 1904. At first he was a church organist and singer, and in 1939 he became a pupil of John Forsell at the Stockholm Conservatory opera school. In 1930 he had made his debut with the Swedish Royal Opera in the baritone roles of Silvio and Rossini's Figaro, and in 1937 he was engaged by the company. In 1936 he had made his tenor debut as Radames, and he subsequently took on such heavy tenor parts as Otello, Siegmund, Parsifal and Tristan. He sang at Salzburg and Vienna (1938), Berlin, Budapest and Milan (1941-42) and Bayreuth (1942). In Sweden his repertory included Manrico, Canio, Florestan, Bacchus, Isouren, Tristan and the Ring tenor roles; in 1946 he sang Peter Grimes in the Swedish première of Britten's opera. In the same year he visited North and South America, singing Siegfried at the Metropolitan. At Covent Garden he sang regularly from 1948 to 1957.

Svanholm was always reliable, and though his voice had neither the warmth nor the splendor to match that of Flagstad (with whom he often sang), his performances were admired for intelligence, musicianship and stamina. He was director of the Swedish Royal Opera, 1956-63, introducing several contemporary operas. Among them The Turn of the Screw, Mathis der Maler and The Rake's Progress. He died at the age of 60.

Otto Edelmann sang the part of Gurnemanz. The Austrian bass-baritone was born in 1917. He studied in Vienna with Liebenheimer and Graner, making his debut in 1937 as Gora in Mozart's Figaro. From 1938 to 1940 he was engaged at Nuremberg. In 1947 he joined the Vienna State Opera, where he made his debut as Hermit (Der Freischütz). At the first two post war Bayreuth Festivals (1951, 1952) he sang Hans Sachs, repeating the role at the Edinburgh Festival (1952) and at his Metropolitan debut (1954).
