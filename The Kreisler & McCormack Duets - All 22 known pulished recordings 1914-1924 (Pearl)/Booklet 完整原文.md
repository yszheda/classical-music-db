## 内页说明

### THE KREISLER/McCORMACK DUETS

On this record are collected together for the very first time all the known published recordings made in duet by two of the greatest and best-loved artists of this century - Fritz Kreisler and John McCormack.

That these two men should have been such good friends and have made music together so often is not entirely surprising. Both were in the very front rank of their respective spheres of artistic activity; both were able to perform not only established major masterpieces but also considerable trifles - to which they lent such distinction that these pieces seemed greater than they were; both eschewed vulgarity in any form; both belonged firmly to the mainstream romantic tradition and ignored largely the 'modern' musical developments of this century; and neither degraded themselves by jockeying for position in the musical firmament, but then of course they didn't need to - they were there by virtue of their own inherent greatness.

The backgrounds of the two men however could scarcely have been more dissimilar. Kreisler, some ten years McCormack's senior, was born on 2nd February, 1875, to a prosperous Viennese Jewish family. A child prodigy, he was at the age of ten awarded a gold medal by the Paris Conservatoire and two years later toured America with the pianist Moriz Rosenthal. This was not a great success and, disillusioned, Kreisler abandoned music altogether for a while; during this break in his career he qualified as a Doctor of Medicine and was commissioned officer in a regiment of Uhlans in the Austrian army. His return to the world of music was marked by a brilliant début in Berlin (with Nikisch); his reputation rose rapidly thereafter.

By contrast McCormack was born to a relatively humble family in Athlone, Ireland, on 14th June 1884. (Entertainingly, this future emblem of Ireland and eventual Papal Count was the scion of a migrant Scottish protestant!). McCormack received little parental encouragement - it is reported that his father once said that in his opinion John "would never amount to much" - and did not evince any interest in singing until he was twelve. Assuredly no child prodigy, McCormack achieved no notable distinction until May 1903, when he won the Gold Medal in the Dublin Feis Ceoil.

McCormack went to London the following year, but only as a member of the Dublin Choir, for an appearance at the St. Louis Festival; after a rather humiliating experience, he walked out on the choir and began a slow ascent to a happier side to this otherwise bleak period of his career. He became engaged to a fellow singer, Lily Foley but she was not pleased to see him parlay briefly, in adapted form, because he sang a few Italian songs and an aria by Gounod.

At this point the careers of McCormack and Kreisler begin to converge. The reader may recall that both men made their first début in 1904. But in 1904 Kreisler was becoming an established artist and could hardly have felt a threat from McCormack. Indeed, the tenor felt that his own voice had not yet received training without proper tuition and this he determined in 1905 to obtain in Milan.

Sabatini, one of the last great teachers of the bel canto tradition, became McCormack's tutor. McCormack returned to England in November 1906 and the following year, at the age of twenty-three, the youngest prizewinner ever to be appointed at Covent Garden.

The two great artists first met in 1912. The following year they toured England together, forging a friendship which was to last until McCormack's death. They first duetted together made in New York a few months later, on 25th March 1914: the promotional photograph taken by Victor at the session depicts a young McCormack looking somewhat solemnicious in their first four pieces.

Between 1914 and 1924 twenty-six recordings of McCormack accompanied by his friend were made but only two were published, and all of these are reissued here. It is known that three further items of this first recording session in 1914 were made, at the expense of the catalogues reproductions of his voice yet to be published. McCormack paid the expense had been spared to achieve the most faithful rendition of their artistry. Enough though they knew that later recordings as soloists, it would seem that they had no objection to the 'new process' being used for their joint recordings. The continuing of their duo series is documented by Victor. The partnership on disc were continued until 1938, but without success.

The cessation of recording activities as a duet partner the two continued to appear in public. For many concert-goers of the two great artists, McCormack had retired - on 27th September 1944 he gave a farewell recital at the Royal Albert Hall. He was however prevailed upon to emerge from retirement to make broadcast and public appearances in 1950 and 1951. Both his 1945 emphysema set in. He made his last recording in 1950 and retired from all public activities. John McCormack, by now a very sick man, died of pneumonia at his home, Villa Salude, on Dublin on 16th September 1945.

As for Kreisler, he preferred to pass the war in America: indeed, his wife was Austrian and was declared an enemy alien. In 1941, when he was aged 66, he was injured in an unfortunate automobile accident and this eventually affected both his hearing and his memory. He gave his farewell concert - in New York, the city of his adoption - on 1st November 1947, after which he lived in retirement until his death on 29th January 1962.

The partnership of John McCormack and Fritz Kreisler was unique; how fortunate that this rich legacy of twenty-two recordings has survived to preserve it.
