## Listening guide

### David Nice on the pianistic brilliance of three Russian greats

#### Rachmaninov Preludes

All three composers on this recording were master pianists in the Russian-school tradition, with Richter and now Leonskaja following on. Rachmaninov also happened to be a teenage genius as a composer much like Mendelssohn, Schubert and Shostakovich. His C sharp minor Prelude (track 17), with its tolling mournful bells joining a fateful left-hand descent, was composed in the autumn of 1872, when Rachmaninov was 19. The most famous not only of the five pieces which make up his Op. 2 but also of all his works, it alerted Tchaikovsky shortly before his untimely death to the next genius on the scene.

The two books of Preludes came later: Op. 23 in 1903, between the Second and Third Piano Concertos, Op. 32 in 1910. Op. 23 No. 1 in F sharp minor (track 1) sets the tone for the predominantly dark first sequence here. In his notebooks Richter calls it 'a sombre and poetical piece. Neuhaus [the great piano pedagogue] thought it was the best of the whole collection. In it you feel the freshness of a Russian evening beside a pond from which mist rises up.' Dancing dotted rhythms are tied to a sense of the obsessive in Op. 32 Nos 10 (track 3) and 2 (track 7), companion pieces even though separated by seven other pieces. A triplet figure kicks off Op. 32 No. 6 (track 8), *Allegro appassionato*, with plenty of the demonism which was to overshadow works to come.

Brightness predominates in the three Op. 23 Preludes of the Aldeburgh recital, among the best-known of both sets. Rachmaninov offers a very individual triumphalism in the B flat major Prelude (track 10) with its tumultuous chord sequences and a typically original conclusion, taking flight before the emphatic and thrilling last two bars. The D major *Andante cantabile* (track 11) is the height of tranquil poetry, the equivalent in Rachmaninov's opus to Chopin's E major Prelude among his Op. 10. Op. 23 No. 5 in G minor (track 12) is one of the few works to fit the cliché, beloved by the American press during Rachmaninov's touring years, of Cossacks galloping across a Russian plain. Certainly it seems to have a ballad-like tale to tell.

#### Scriabin Sonata No. 9

The harmonies which Rachmaninov stretched to breaking point – though his typingcasting as reactionary late romantic has often ignored the fact – dissolve tonality in the ninth of Scriabin's compact sonatas, the whole body forming a journey into strangeness unrivalled in the piano repertoire. By 1912, when he began this repertoire, ominously titled 'Black Mass' (track 13). Scriabin's combination of the Messianic and the occult was at its peak, though the level of dissonance and complexity in previous sonatas was, as one cautious admirer pointed out, thankfully reduced. The admirer revisited the work in 1925 and noted how the Ninth 'portrays a fascinating image of a certain mystical striving mixed with eroticism and a kind of shadowy, dark power, a striving that at times erupts in convulsive spasms followed by the melancholy of impotence and a dreamy, exhausted resignation'.

#### Prokofiev Sonata No. 4

The commenter mentioned above was none other than Sergei Prokofiev, who had at one point considered making an orchestration of Scriabin's Fifth Piano Sonata. The process was reversed in the slow movement of his own Fourth Piano Sonata (track 15), completed in Kislovodsk, a Caucasian spa resort, at the end of the tumultuous year 1917; it starts with a reworking of the lugubrious theme which began the Andante of a 1908 Symphony, eventually returned to its orchestral form in 1934. Scriabinesque mystery, though allied to clarity rather than obscurity of thought, is the essence of the opening movement (track 14), unique in Prokofiev output; while there are diatonic, 'white note' C major themes here and in the Andante, it takes the finale (track 16) to raise the veil with a brilliant display of fireworks.

## Artist biographies

### SVIATOSLAV RICHTER (piano)

Richter was born in the Ukraine to a musical family. He began learning piano and composition at seven, and in his teens worked as an accompanist, giving his first recital in Odessa at 19. In the late 1930s, he entered the Moscow Conservatory. Despite being expelled three times for refusing to take exams, he was reinstated on each occasion on the insistence of his teacher, Heinrich Neuhaus. In 1940 he made his official debut with works including Prokofiev's Sixth Sonata – the composer later dedicated his Ninth Sonata to him. After WWII, Richter enjoyed a major career in Russia and the West. Until 1989, he toured prolifically, playing an average of 70 concerts a year. He died in 1997.

### ELISABETH LEONSKAJA (piano)

For decades, Elisabeth Leonskaja has been among the world's most celebrated pianists. A close friend and duet partner with Sviatoslav Richter, she has appeared as a soloist with leading orchestras under the baton of such conductors as Kurt Masur, Sir Colin Davis, Christoph Eschenbach, Christoph von Dohnányi, Kurt Sanderling, Mariss Jansons, Yuri Temirkanov and Iván Fischer.
