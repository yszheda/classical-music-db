## Liner Notes by Levon Hakobian

As is well known, Galina Ustvolskaya was Shostakovich's favourite pupil. The teacher even said to her: 'You are a phenomenon, while I'm but a talent'. It's not that he was under her influence; rather I am under yours'. A motif from the finale of her Trio for piano, clarinet and violin (composed in 1949, first performed in 1968) is quoted in two works by Shostakovich, namely in his Fifth String Quartet (1952) and in the ninth movement ('Night') of the late vocal Suite to verses by Michelangelo (1974). Behind these quotations from Ustvolskaya there is a certain history of personal relations: in the early '50s Shostakovich was in love with his student, and the reminiscences of the bygone affection remained alive until the end of his life. The relations between Shostakovich and Ustvolskaya fell into decay due to her difficult character: in one of his letters Shostakovich mentioned her 'somewhat Dostoevsky-like' penchant for suffering. Be that as it may, Shostakovich officially expressed his opinion on Ustvolskaya in this following eloquent terms: 'I believe that the name of Galina Ustvolskaya will win world recognition among all those who care for the real music'.

Galina Ivanovna Ustvolskaya was born in St Petersburg (then Petrograd) on June 17, 1919, and died there on September 22, 2006, aged 87. At the age of five she began composing with Professor Shoshinovich in 1939-41 and then after the war, in 1945-47. In 1948-75 she taught composition at the music college attached to the Conservatory. For the rest of her life she was a free-lance composer, her contacts with the outside world were rare and irregular. Until the end of the '50s she was known as author of works not only on elevated topics, written in a more or less traditional style. Later she declared her worthlessness and virtually banned them for performance. She made exceptions only for her First Symphony (with two treble voices singing a text by the Italian Communist writer Gianni Rodari) and for two battle poems, 'Lies in the Steppe' and 'Heroic Deed'.

As regards Ustvolskaya's music composed in the same years 'for the drawer', it has nothing to do with the Soviet conformism. By the end of the '40s she had already elaborated a personal style of her own. Its most conspicuous features include measured pace of even rhythmical units, lean textures, sharp dynamic contrasts, frequent confrontations of extreme registers, unusual combinations of instrumental timbres. The music's expressive power is intensified due to such factors as the sharpness of strongly dissonant chords (up to dense sound blocks - tone clusters), the quasi-choral antiphon, the hypnotizing reiterations of concise simple motifs. No doubt, in the final years of Stalin's reign, when the struggle with the so-called formalism was at its height, as well as later, in the era of Khrushchev, she had no chances to be performed. Its severe, uncompromising tone reflects the persistent tragedy of human existence; it emanates a high-voltage, indeed 'Dostoevskian' energy. To quote Ustvolskaya's and Shostakovich's common friend, the composer Boris Tishchenko, 'the music of Ustvolskaya literally burns you with its piercing anguish and with superhuman intensity and spiritual force'.

With the course of time, the style of Ustvolskaya evolved but slightly. Let us quote Tishchenko once more: 'Due to Ustvolskaya's sharply delineated self, her inward force and outward restraint, her art develops as a unique whole, as a single block of human spirit - a monolithic, beautiful, and laconic sculpture'. The piano sonatas recorded here attest the striking unity of her style: the First Sonata dates from 1947, while the Sixth - from 1988. The immutability of her manner during these four decades has something in common with narrowly conservative mental outlook. To Ustvolskaya's work, one might apply the self-description of Anton Webern: 'Always the same, but every time in a new way'. Only a superficial or indifferent listener would find Ustvolskaya's 'monotonous' and excessively 'repetitive'. As with Webern, every next piece by her is an attempt to realize the same fundamental idea more powerfully, boldly, eloquently, than in the earlier works.

According to the composer Viktor Suslin, Ustvolskaya accomplishes 'a real feat when in gloomy year 1949 she composed her Second Sonata: When the composers of world-wide renown would be humiliated by Soviet press, when they would acknowledge their "errors" with respect to the Party and socialist realism, would repent and ask forgiveness, a shy, modest thirty-years-old woman, hardly known to anybody, was writing at her miserable piano the music of unprecedented despair and passionate protest'. Though the last sonatas appeared in the liberal years of the so-called 'Perestroika', they are permeated with the same mood of 'infinite despair and passionate protest', which is typical for Ustvolskaya's style is severe, but touches upon the depths of human existence. All the sonatas are rather compact (8-16 minutes of music), most of them are divided into movements, the others consist of several sections attacca. Though the movements and sections contrast with each other as regards tempo and dynamics, they are largely similar with respect to rhythm, harmony, texture, and thematic content. The prevailing type of music is a steady motion with notes of equal duration, in a dissonant chord or ascetic two-part texture, without ornamentation and with minimum pedal. The cycle of 12 Preludes is, in general, somewhat less austere: the mood of some of these pieces is rather relaxed and lyrical.

As was said above, Ustvolskaya's music of the '40s and '50s was unveiled to public with a considerable delay. The Sonatas No. 1 (1947), No. 3 (1952) and No. 4 (1957) were first performed in Leningrad by Oleg Malov only in the '70s. The Second Sonata (1949) was first heard in Moscow in 1967 (pianist Aleksandr Vedernikov), while the 12 Preludes (1953) - in Leningrad in 1968 (Anatoly Ugorsky). The premieres of the last two sonatas, the Fifth (1986) and the Sixth (1988) took place immediately after their completion; in both cases the pianist was Oleg Malov.

On these CDs Ustvolskaya's piano music is performed by Ivan Sokolov. One of the most original Russian musicians of our days, he was born in Moscow in 1960 and graduated from the Moscow Conservatoire, where his main teachers were Nikolay Sidelnikov (composition) and Lev Naumov (piano). As composer, Sokolov is versatile: in some works he is quite traditional, in others he appears as follower of minimalism or conceptualism. His pianistic repertoire is similarly versatile. In Russia and abroad he is known above all as performer of new music of various trends, possessor of exceptional memory, unfailing technique, and lively, uninhibited artistic temperament. Though Ustvolskaya was highly severe, even captious towards the interpreters of her music, her opinion about Sokolov's recordings, first published in 1996, was unreservedly positive.

**Levon Hakobian**

## Ivan Sokolov Biography

**IVAN SOKOLOV**

Ivan Sokolov was born in Moscow in 1960, and graduated from the Moscow State Conservatory in 1983, having studied composition with Professor N.N.Sidelnikov and piano with Professor L.Naumov. He then continued at the Conservatory with post-graduate work, completing his work in 1986. He subsequently taught at the Conservatory between 1988 and 1994. Since 1987, Mr. Sokolov has been a member of the Composers' Union of Russia, as well as the Association of Contemporary Music in Russia.

Mr. Sokolov is a regular participant in contemporary music festivals in Russia and abroad, including at the Alternative, Moscow Autumn, Moscow Forum, and December Nights festivals. He performed many contemporary piano works by Stockhausen, Cage, Kagel, Crumb, Feldman, Boulez, some of which were their Russian premiere. He played the world premiere of the Piano Concerto by Edison Denisov. Furthermore he worked with the Ensemble Modern and the Kronos Quartet. His own works have been performed in Russia, Germany, Switzerland, and Denmark. He is the author of numerous chamber and symphonic works, and regular performs as a piano soloist. Mr. Sokolov has lived both in Germany and in Russia since 1994.
