## Liner Notes

### A Heath Ruffled by Winds and Legends... (by Richard Millet)

The music of Brahms is a heath ruffled by winds and legends: so I have always thought, doubtless too hastily, forgetting that music that yields itself up as easily as this always ends by confronting us with its mystery. And yet, right up to the intermezzo, the clearest sonatas and the lieder of his last years, the music of Brahms seems still traversed by those gusts that blow over the expanses of mist peopled by greyish light, those dead leaves the lakes, the heathen barrows and brooms of his native Holstein. Indeed, the very name "Brahms" – the German word for a species of broom that grows in the sandy lands of North Germany – evokes the heath. Brahms grew up close to the great forest of Lüneburg, and as a young man was not afraid to go wandering in it, alone, at night. Brahms was a man of the North Sea: his music bears the mark of that somber, symbolic confirmation that of landscape he possessed: which ciphers us into his music like characters hurrying by rough these characters hurrying toward the calm towns of the North Sea, steeped in the dusk, gripped by fear, to hear the spirit sing over the evening waters. Like the proud and humble person, Brahms is a brother to all those solitary human children who have not yet done with their childhood tales and loves, and everything that can make us forget just a little that we are marked out for death.

We live in our minds many more places than we actually see. On childhood – on it that singular and somewhat melancholy and quite changing music – Brahms seems to have been more attached to the candelabra he those painted by Caspar David Friedrich, and have long dreamed of the religious edifice of Germany built by Albrecht Dürer in the famous engraving "Melencolia". Brahms, like Dürer, took a long journey with the oratories of Buxtehude, the Passions of Schütz and Bach, and the Missa Solemnis; all we have left of those vanished cathedrals – for it seems to me that musical architecture is much more – propitious to meditation than an edifice of stone and glass – are the naves we still visit, or at least we live in this monument. We all have in our mind's ear, for example, the great version of the Requiem conducted by Klemperer in 1954 and Fischer-Dieskau moved us deeply with their declamatory power or their extraordinary gentleness in these verses from the Gospels, the Psalms, the Prophets, and the books of the Apocrypha. What strikes us here, as a child, was first so much the ever-contemporary character that music brings to a text, as the interplay of light and shade in music intended to be at once overwhelming and soothing. What is sung here has nothing of the somber elegance of Mozart's Requiem; of the fury of Berlioz; of Verdi's operatic Messa da Requiem, still less of Faure's mildness. The German Requiem is a magnificent paradox coming from a man with as little religious faith as had Brahms. It may be understood as a homage to the biblical text that once impregnated every European childhood, and also to the residue of childhood faith that persists in any art, so that which is lost yet continues to irrigate the creative existence, the only means, along with love, of forgetting the fate to which we are all summoned, or at least to put it at a judicious distance between fear and renunciation. This is why, whether believers or agnostics, we are still gripped by the verity of what is laid out before us in the work's seven sections: a poetry of sorrow and consolation, in which Luther's German rings out wonderfully, and the music encourages us to reflect anew from the vantage point of our mature experience. Even those who have not fully revealed that all flesh is as grass, that our days are as an handbreadth, that even cities in this world have no continued place, shall at last discover that we must all pass by the gate of the trumpet that shall sound the Resurrection and the Death of Death for all time, but also, nearer still to us, these words that Brahms has to say to us: "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you." Words addressed to each one of us by music that we know is sometimes threatened by the bombast of the Romantic performance tradition, so that it is in other works of Brahms, some of it a capella choruses and, above all, that wonderful ultimate statement that is the Four Serious Songs, that we had finally come to seek the expression of the agnostic's innermost spirituality.

Yet none of the reduction of the Requiem for chorus, soloists and piano duet that Brahms made in 1869, which renews our conception of the work, moving us in a quite different way: a different world! As, indeed, abandon these cold words and return to the heath – reminding us in its fashion of what we owe to ourselves, when he submits the soul to great trials and deep introspection. Beyond all dogmas. We realize that the Brahmsian heath is – so – that heath where we heard texts and Bachian chorales, a place where we walk not as conquerors but in quest, even we incautiously race; and now I see looming up in the dawn light not a cathedral of the spirit, but almost a temple of the word, where the truth about ourselves is conveyed to us in song, in music that no longer confronts us with its mystery but humanly attunes it to our own, like some consolation negotiated, with the certainty that our soul will not be weighed nor judged, but received into the source of all joy.

**Richard Millet**
Translation: Charles Johnstone

### Johannes Brahms | ein deutsches requiem (by Prof. Dr. Wolfgang Hochstein)

Ein deutsches Requiem (A German Requiem) of Johannes Brahms (1833-1897) is among the most outstanding and most frequently performed works in the sacred choral repertoire. The title already shows that we are not dealing here with a liturgical requiem. It is one of the greatest choral works.

Brahms based his composition on his own choice of biblical texts, taken from Luther's Bible translation, creating a cycle of seven movements that describe human consolation of older stylistic models, has no real precedence in its concept, and multiple results from Brahms. The texts speak of death and transformation, describe death and transformation in words express it in a spirit of venerable humanistic religious feeling. In its respect they show a widespread nineteenth-century sensibility, which places the work outside the strict liturgical framework and thus at the same time shows what itself down to specifically Christian beliefs and beliefs.

The idea of the work and the elaboration of its constituent parts occupied Brahms over a period of twenty years. It would appear that it was several sorrowful experiences in his own life that prompted the composer to embark on a musical opus. The first of these was the death of his close friend and mentor Robert Schumann, who had thrown himself into the Elbe near Bonn in February 1854; the second the death of his mother, Christiane Brahms, in 1865. The oldest element in the Deutsches Requiem is the funeral march-movement for the tenor voice "Selig sind, die da Leid tragen," which derives from a slow scherzo in a sonata for two pianos of 1854. By 1861 the Requiem's first two movements were largely completed, and a four-movement form was already projected. "Wie lieblich sind deine Wohnungen," a piece notable for its gentle simplicity, by the summer of the particularly industrious summer of 1865 and existed in their final form, so that the composer could present them to the public in Hamburg and Vienna as early as 1867. In the first of these, held in Vienna on December 1867, Johann Herbeck conducted the first performance of the three movements so far completed – quite unlike those to the premiere of the six-movement version at Bremen Cathedral on Good Friday, 10th April 1868: on that occasion Brahms was the sole conductor.

Over the ensuing weeks Brahms applied himself to setting the text he had not transcribed, which he had already planned to include in his conception of the work. The new no. 5 had not been completed; in the meantime, this movement, which was initially intended to be sung by a solo soprano, was replaced by no. 5 of the Deutsches Requiem. The now definitive seven-movement version was first heard in Leipzig on 12th February 1869, under the baton of Carl Reinecke. Alongside a sovereign mastery of thematic development and formal design, and also of the contrapuntal skills of which Brahms was such a past master, the work is particularly impressive for its harmonic language, its deployment of a multitude of colours in the large orchestra: particularly notable is the skillfully differentiated writing for the chorus, which participates in every movement and clearly takes precedence over the two solo voices. The fugue over a pedal point at the end of the third movement, "Herr, lehre doch mich," is one of Brahms's most monumental creations.

A new beginning, to less so the sixth movement. The opening is devoted to the "mystery of death" and the powerful, triumphant vision of the Last Judgment. The generative cell of the whole work is comprised of the words "Herr" and "Selig sind die Toten", closely matched in the work's mood, create a convincing arch of tension for the whole composition. Perhaps the Deutsches Requiem, out of all Brahms's works, best fulfils the prophecy of Robert Schumann, made after the first meeting of the young composer in 1853 "Neue Bahnen."

Over the ensuing years Brahms made various revisions toward the massed forces of chorus and soloists, with the first string version being given in London in 1871. This arrangement for piano duet was published in 1869 and Brahms himself conducted its premiere on 16th July 1871. Before then the first performance of the complete work had taken place on 1st April 1869. Both parts and the vocal score (prepared by Brahms himself) of the Deutsches Requiem were issued by Robert Forberg in Leipzig. In July 1869 the composer sent out asking a version for piano duet, as the publication costs for the orchestral parts were too high. The resulting modest arrangement of this kind offered the normal domestic music-making of the time and also opened up large forces, and also the possibility of playing them in domestic circumstances. In their connection, Brahms wrote to Fritz Simrock on 31 January 1869: "I have decided myself to the noble task of making my immortal work palatable to fair-handed souls also. Now it cannot perish. Moreover, it has become quite splenetic, and every easily playable bass-notes, treble and alto are easy and a delight to play... My passage through here is complete, and I think I shall send you the piece one of these days."

The letter is couched in that mockingly ironic tone that Brahms was fond of adopting. The reduction focuses on the arrangement's playability must have been important to him. The simplification of the instrumental parts, for given the generally accepted view of Brahms's piano writing as difficult the publication did not want to risk losing its audience, necessitated a very restrained playing technique. The mention of Hell in the quotation from the Revelation of John that ends the work ("I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord, which is, and which was, and which is to come, the Almighty.") which includes the line "Wie, wo ist dein Sieg?"

This arrangement for piano duet was published in 1869 and Brahms himself conducted its premiere on 16th July 1871. This edition is in a new arrangement of the orchestral parts; it is in fact a transformation of the whole work (including the vocal parts) into a piano composition in its own right. In striving to produce a style that sounds as orchestral as possible, the two pianists have frequently treated the existing musical material in a new way, for example, through the distribution and redistribution of it. This affects, for example, several places the reduction of the string and wind voices, and the amplification of a number of performers or expression marks as compared to the orchestral score. Perhaps these additions are even to be understood as a later concretization of readings latent in the orchestral edition, allowing us to infer the interpretation Brahms had in mind for his original work.

According to an announcement in the Allgemeine Musikalische Zeitung of 30 April 1873, the Philharmonic Society of London gave the Requiem "for the first time in England" at that same month of April. Though this assertion may be accurate as far as the normal orchestral version is concerned, it is not true that this was the work's English premiere. The singer and friend of Brahms, Julius Stockhausen (who had sung the baritone solo in the Bremen premiere), was already able to report in a letter to the composer dated 11 March 1872 that he knew of a lady member of the London society who had "presented a performance of the Requiem in her own house last year". This information is confirmed by the English biographer of Brahms Florence May, from whom we learn further particulars. According to her, the performance in question was held on 7 July 1871 in the Wimpole Street house of the surgeon and writer Sir Thomas Thompson, before an invited audience; there was a chorus of thirty, and the soprano solo was sung by Anna Rogier. Since the full performing forces would have been beyond the scope of any private dwelling, however large, the accompaniment used was the piano duet arrangement that had already been available in print for two years; the parts were played by the hostess Lady Kate Fanny Thompson and Captain Potter – the former a gifted lady pianist, the latter a well-known pianist and composer. No less a figure than Sir Julius Stockhausen conducted the performance and sang the baritone solo.

The present recording of the Deutsches Requiem documents that London premiere of 1871. In this performance those passages of the piano part that are rendered superfluous by the presence of voices are omitted, for reasons of sonority the accompaniment is played on two pianos.

**Prof. Dr. Wolfgang Hochstein**
Translation: Charles Jorynes

## Artist Biographies

### Laurence Equilbey

**Français:**
Née à Paris, Vienne et Stockholm, Laurence Equilbey a étudié la direction principalement avec le chef suédois Eric Ericson. En 1991, elle fonde le Chœur de Chambre Accentus.

Son répertoire s'étend de la musique de la Renaissance à la création contemporaine, avec une prédilection pour les œuvres vocales de grande ampleur. Elle a créé de nombreuses œuvres de Philippe Hersant, Henri Dutilleux, Giacinto Scelsi, et a collaboré avec des compositeurs tels que Pierre Boulez et György Ligeti.

Elle a été nommée Chevalier de la Légion d'honneur et Commandeur de l'Ordre des Arts et Lettres.

**English:**
Born in Paris, Vienna and Stockholm, Laurence Equilbey studied conducting principally with the Swedish conductor Eric Ericson. In 1991 she founded the Chamber Choir Accentus.

Her repertoire extends from Renaissance music to contemporary creation, with a predilection for large-scale vocal works. She has created numerous works by Philippe Hersant, Henri Dutilleux, Giacinto Scelsi, and has collaborated with composers such as Pierre Boulez and György Ligeti.

She has been named Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur and Commandeur of the Ordre des Arts et Lettres.

### Sandrine Piau

**English:**
Sandrine Piau first came the public's attention through her much-praised performances of baroque repertoire, most notably of Rameau. From then on she has become, thanks to an exceptional lyric voice, a soprano sought after for her performances of Mozart, Gluck, Strauss, and contemporary repertoire.

She has appeared at the Opéra National de Paris, Theater an der Wien, Opéra National de Lyon, and has performed under conductors such as William Christie, Marc Minkowski, Fabio Biondi, Michel Corboz, Christophe Rousset, Gustav Leonhardt, and Jordi Savall.

Her repertoire extends from baroque to contemporary music. She has worked with conductors such as William Christie, Marc Minkowski, and Michel Corboz.

### Stéphane Degout

**English:**
Stéphane Degout obtained a diploma as a stonecutter at the Conservatoire National Supérieur de Musique de Lyon. In the class of Margaret Hong, then went on to further study with Régine Crespin at the Atelier Lyrique de l'Opéra de Paris.

He has performed at the Opéra National de Paris, Opéra National de Lyon, Theater an der Wien, and has appeared under conductors such as William Christie, Marc Minkowski, and Michel Corboz.

He made his debut at the Metropolitan Opera in 2009. He has won numerous awards including Victoires de la Musique Classique in 2004 and 2011.

### Boris Berezovsky

**English:**
Born in Moscow in 1969, Boris Berezovsky studied at the Moscow Conservatory with Eliso Virsaladze, and graduated with distinction. After first prize at the Tchaikovsky Competition in 1990, he went on to an brilliant international career.

He has performed with the Royal Concertgebouw Orchestra, the Rotterdam Philharmonic Orchestra, the Orchestre Philharmonique de Radio France, the BBC Symphony Orchestra, and the NHK Symphony Orchestra.

His concert and chamber music repertoire spans from Mozart to contemporary music. He has recorded for labels such as Teldec, Warner, Mirare, and Chandos.

### Brigitte Engerer

**English:**
Brigitte Engerer began her musical career at the age of ten. After first prize at the Paris Conservatoire, she studied with Alfred Cortot's assistant, Yvonne Lefébure, then with Emil Gilels in Moscow.

She has performed with the Orchestre de Paris, the London Symphony Orchestra, the Chicago Symphony Orchestra, and the Philadelphia Orchestra under conductors such as Claudio Abbado, Riccardo Muti, and Christoph Eschenbach.

She was a professor at the Paris Conservatoire and founded the Musica Nova competition.

### Accentus

**English:**
This group of 32 professional singers was brought together by Laurence Equilbey in 1991 with the express intention of performing the extensive repertoire of a cappella music in chamber choir formation. In meeting the tradition of unaccompanied choral music, Accentus has focused principally on the major works of the 16th- and 20th-centuries, but has also been responsible for premiers of new pieces.

Accentus also appears regularly under the direction of their principal guest conductor, the Swedish choral director Eric Ericson. The choir has given many performances throughout Europe with ensembles such as the Orchestra des Champs-Élysées, the Concerto Köln, the Freiburg Baroque Orchestra, and the Orchestra of the Age of Enlightenment.

The choir records for labels such as Naïve, Harmonia Mundi, and Accentus Music.

### 1. Chor / Chorus
**Selig sind, die da Leid tragen**

**German (Luther Bible):**
Selig sind, die da Leid tragen, denn sie sollen getröstet werden.
Die mit Tränen säen, werden mit Freuden ernten.

Blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted.

### 2. Chor / Chorus
**Denn alles Fleisch, es ist wie Gras**

**German (Luther Bible):**
Denn alles Fleisch, es ist wie Gras und alle Herrlichkeit des Menschen wie des Grases Blumen.
Verwelkt ist das Gras, und abgefallen ist die Blume.
So seid nun geduldig, lieben Brüder, bis auf die Zukunft des Herrn. Siehe, ein Landmann wartet auf die köstliche Frucht der Erde und ist geduldig darüber, bis er empfange den Frühregen und den Spätregen.
Des Herrn Wort bleibt in Ewigkeit.
Und die Erlösten des Herrn werden wiederkommen und gen Zion kommen mit Jauchzen; ewige Freude wird über ihrem Haupte sein, daß sie Freude und Wonne ergreifen und das Leid und Seufzen wird weg sein.

**English:**
For all flesh is as the grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass.
The grass withereth, and the flower thereof falleth away.
Be patient therefore, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord. Behold, the husbandman waiteth for the precious fruit of the earth, and hath long patience for it, until he receive the early and latter rain.
The word of the Lord endureth for ever.
And the ransomed of the Lord shall return, and come to Zion with songs and everlasting joy upon their heads: they shall obtain joy and gladness, and sorrow and sighing shall flee away.

### 3. Bariton und Chor / Baritone and Chorus
**Herr, lehre doch mich**

**German (Luther Bible):**
Herr, lehre doch mich, daß ein Ende mit mir haben muß, und mein Leben ein Ziel hat, und ich dahin muß.
Siehe, meine Tage sind einer Handbreit vor dir, und mein Leben ist wie nichts vor dir.
Wie ganz nichts sind alle Menschen, die doch so sicher leben.
Sie gehen daher wie ein Schemen und machen sich viel vergebliche Unruhe; sie sammeln und wissen nicht, wem es zutun wird.
Nun Herr, was soll ich mich trösten? Ich hoffe auf dich.

**English:**
Lord, make me to know mine end, and the measure of my days, what it is; that I may know how frail I am.
Behold, thou hast made my days as an handbreadth; and mine age is as nothing before thee: verily every man at his best state is altogether vanity.
Surely every man walketh in a vain shew: surely they are disquieted in vain: he heapeth up riches, and knoweth not who shall gather them.
And now, Lord, what wait I for? My hope is in thee.

### 5. Sopran und Chor / Soprano and Chorus
**Ihr habt nun Traurigkeit**

**German (Luther Bible):**
So habt ihr nun Traurigkeit; aber ich will euch wiedersehen, und euer Herz soll sich freuen, und eure Freude soll niemand von euch nehmen.
Sehet mich an: Ich habe eine kleine Zeit Mühe und Arbeit gehabt und habe großen Trost erfunden.
Rufet mich an in der Zeit der Not; ich will euch erretten, und ihr sollt mich preisen.
Christus ist unsre Auferstehung und das Leben; wer an mich glaubet, der wird leben, ob er gleich stürbe. Und wer da lebet und an mich glaubet, der wird nimmermehr sterben.

**English:**
Ye now therefore have sorrow: but I will see you again, and your heart shall rejoice, and your joy no man taketh from you.
Behold, I labored but for a little while, and found much comfort.
Call on me in the time of trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify Me.
I am the resurrection and the life. He that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: and whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die.

### 6. Bariton und Chor / Baritone and Chorus
**Denn wir haben hie keine bleibende Statt**

**German (Luther Bible):**
Denn wir haben hie keine bleibende Statt, sondern die zukünftige suchen wir.
Siehe, ich sage euch ein Geheimnis: Wir werden nicht alle entschlafen, wir werden aber alle verwandelt werden; und dasselbe plötzlich in einem Augenblick, zu der Zeit, wenn die letzte Posaune schallen wird; denn es wird die Posaune schallen, und die Toten werden auferstehen unverweslich, und wir werden verwandelt werden.
Dann wird erfüllet werden das Wort, das geschrieben ist: Der Tod ist verschlungen in den Sieg.
Tod, wo ist dein Stachel? Hölle, wo ist dein Sieg?

**English:**
For here have we no continuing city, but we seek one to come.
Behold, I shew you a mystery: We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.
Then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.
O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?
