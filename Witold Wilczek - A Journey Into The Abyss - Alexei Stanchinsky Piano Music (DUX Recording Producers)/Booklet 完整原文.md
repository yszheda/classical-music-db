## ALEKSIEJ STANCZINSKI – ZAPOMNIANY GENIUSZ ROSYJSKIEJ SREBRNEJ ERY

Srebrny wiek kultury rosyjskiej przypadający na przełom XIX i XX wieku to czas, kiedy apogeum osiągnęły narastające od stulecia tendencje w sztuce rosyjskiej stawiające ją w rozkroku między fascynującym wschodem a zachodem, a zakorzenieniem w słowiańskiej tradycji wielkiej Rusi. Epoka ta, choć trwała około dwóch dekad, wydała wielu znakomitych artystów, takich jak poetka Achmatowa, malarze Kandinsky i Chagall, czy kompozytorzy Skriabin i Strawiński. Wschodzącą gwiazdą, jaśniejącą blaskiem swego nie-tradycyjnego talentu, był też oczywiście geniusz muzyki Aleksiej Stanczinski, młody kompozytor z kręgu Taniejewa i Skriabina, którego pochód na wielkości przerwała nagła i tragiczna śmierć w 1914 roku, podobnie jak Srebrny Wiek zakończył się wraz z pochodem rewolucji bolszewickiej trzy lata później.

Wiek XIX okazał się być w Rosji czasem bolesnych narodzin młodego imperium. Po epoce ekspansji terytorialnej roztaczających swe wpływy zarówno od Bałtyku na zachodzie po Morze Czarne na południu i Pacyfik na wschodzie. Fascynacja nowymi horyzontami i wreszcie urzeczywistniona realnie szansą kontaktów handlowych i politycznych, zarówno ze światem Europy zachodniej jak i dalekiego Wschodu, doprowadziło do życia obywatelom państwem – Słowianami Zjednoczonymi, zrodziła w mentalności kierującej rosyjskiej inteligencji i wizję wielkiej Rosji jako okrętu targanego burzami, zmierzającego ku nowym, nieznanym brzegom. Obraz ten najpełniej znalazł ujście w rolniczej i niejednokrotnie monumentalnej rozmaitości twórczości najważniejszego z rosyjskich malarzy – Iwana Aleksiejewicza Ajwazowskiego, którego masywne, czasem nawet o rozpiętości piętnastu metrów, płótna przedstawiały obraz okrętu na nieposkromionych falach, budząc respekt, ale i przytłaczając swym rozmachem obserwatorów na wystawach.

Ten niepokój nad kierunkiem, jaki metaforycznie obraz miał dla olbrzym powstającego imperium znajdował też odbicie w mentalności, inteligencji, filozofów i artystów tamtych czasów, rodzaj tych mędrca fascynację kulturą zachodnią Europy, zwłaszcza Francji i Niemiec, a przywiązaniem do tradycji swojskiej, słowiańskiej Rusi, postrzeganej przez nich jako kolebka rodzimej kultury oraz alternatywne wobec świata wielkiego zachodniego ganeżo zachodu. Obojętni pochodzeniowy był Santi Petersburg, zbudowany przez Piotra Wielkiego od podstaw na wydarłym morzu terenie, miał w zamyśle być stolicą przyszłego imperium państwowego, wzorowaną na metropoliach Europy. Rosyjscy okcydentaliści, wzorujący się na zachodnich społeczeństwach, ciążyli ku liberalnemu społecznemu i gospodarczemu status quo, ale też artystycznie wzorować na zdobyczach zachodu.

W muzyce najpełniej bodaj znalazło to odzwierciedlenie w dziełach Antona Rubinsteina czy Piotra Czajkowskiego, którzy wprawdzie, choć niepozbawiona oczywiście elementów typowo rosyjskich takich chociażby jak tańce temperamentyka historyczna narracja, stała się jedną z ważniejszych części światowych tendencji rozwoju budowy światowej kultury muzycznej. W Petersburgu działał też pieriedwiżnicy, grupa malarzy, stojących w opozycji do realistów, skupiająca się na ukazywaniu niedoli codziennego życia w Rosji i zacierania lojalności. Z drugiej strony Moskwa, do wielkiego pożaru z 1813 roku miasto jeszcze głównie drewniane, będąca tradycyjną siedzibą Cerkwi oraz carów, była centralnym ośrodkiem władzy, nierzadko sięgającej po mniej subtelne niż cywilizacja zachodnia środki przymusu i ciała państwowego. Przeciwko tejże władzy aktywnie występował narodnicy, zwracający się ku tradycyjnemu słowiańskości i postulujący wprowadzenie do Rosji ustroju socjalizmu, opierając się o prymat władzy chłopskiej. W swoich działaniach nie stronili oni też od aktów terroru, wystrzały wspomnieć założoną w 1879 roku organizację Narodnaja Wola, której członkami byli między innymi odpowiedzialni za zamachy na cara Aleksandra II i których proces znalazł odbicie w powieści Biesy Fiodora Dostojewskiego. Na gruncie muzycznym owo odwołanie do tradycji narodowej i tradycji kultury słowiańskiej była również obecna. Jak pisze Bogusław Schaeffer: „Muzyka rosyjska – to muzyka ludowa lub na niej wzorowana, muzyka oparta na tematach narodowych, często baśniowej, muzyka tkwiąca korzeniami w ludowej glebie". ## ALEXEI STANCHINSKY – A FORGOTTEN GENIUS OF THE RUSSIAN SILVER AGE

The Silver Age of Russian culture spanned the end of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century. It was a period when Russian art found itself at the crossroads between a fascinating West with Western culture and a celebration of its own roots and the Slavic tradition of the Great Rus'. That mounting century-old trend reached its apogee at the time. And even though lasting only two decades or so, it produced a host of outstanding artists. These included the poet Akhmatova, the painters Kandinsky and Chagall, and the composers Scriabin and Stravinsky. One other was Alexei Stanchinsky, a young composer from the circle of Taneyev and Scriabin. Hailed as a rising star of classical music, Stanchinsky was endowed with a rare talent. His march to greatness came to a halt with his sudden and tragic death in 1914. Meanwhile, the Silver Age itself was also brought to an end with the Bolshevik revolution three years later.

In the history of Russia, the 19th century turned out to be a time of the growing pains of the young empire. After an era of territorial expansion and numerous wars, the newly-born political giant for the first time reached the Baltic in the west and stretched all the way to the Black Sea in the south and as far as the Pacific Ocean in the east. This cultural awakening of Russia, along with Western culture, and a celebration of its own roots, fascination with the new horizons and the long-awaited emerging prospect of closer commercial and political ties with both Western Europe and the United States – a new rising force on the world stage – fed into a vision of a great Russia, one conjuring up the idea of a ship tossed by storm waters toward new, undiscovered shores. This kind of vision found its fullest reflection in the work of Ivan Aivazovsky, one of Russia's finest realist painters. Many of his monumental canvases, some of them fifteen metres long, depict a vessel in rough going over seas, an image that both commands respect and overwhelms onlookers.

Metaphorically speaking, there was a distinct sense of anxiety among the intelligentsia, philosophers and artists of the time over the future course of the new empire. Their mindset was torn between a fascination with Western culture, particularly French and German, and an attachment to the indigenous, Slavonic tradition that was perceived as the cradle of their native culture and a familiar alternative to a mistrusted West. St Petersburg was the mainstay of pro-Western thought. Built by Peter the Great from scratch on an expanse of land reclaimed from the sea, it would become the capital of a modern state, modelled after the metropolises of Europe. Russian occidentalists looking at Western societies for their models favoured moderate social and economic liberalism, and tried to emulate Western achievements in their artistic endeavours.

In music, this trend was especially pronounced in the work of Anton Rubinstein and Pyotr Tchaikovsky. Their music, imbued with typical Russian flavours such as folk dance and ample references to national history, became one of the country's main artistic contributions to world musical culture. St Petersburg was also the hub of the peredvizhniki (the wanderers), a group in opposition to realism and focused on depicting the poverty of Russian peasants and the country's backwardness. Moscow, a predominantly wooden city until the great fire of 1813, served as the traditional power seat of the Russian Orthodox Church and the tsars as well as the home of central state authorities, who often resorted to methods less subtle than verbal campaigning when seeking to maintain law and order. It was the narodniks (from 'narod' – people, folk) who rose against the central authorities, invoking Slavonic roots and urging Russia to a model of socialism based on the rule of the peasants. They did not shun terrorism, as exemplified by the Narodnaya Volya (People's Will) group, founded in 1879, that organization was responsible for assassinations, including on Tsar Alexander II. The trial of its members is described in Dostoyevsky's novel *The Possessed*.

The tendency to draw inspiration from Russian roots and the Slavonic heritage and tradition could also be seen in music. As Polish musicologist Bogusław Schaeffer observed: "Russian music is folk music or drawing on folklore, music based on national themes, often fairytale-like; it is music rooted in people's soil". Members of the Mighty Handful drew extensively on that tradition. One of them, Modest Mussorgsky, the greatest innovator in Russian music since Mikhail Glinka, was keen to give music a folk touch. His two great historical operas, Boris Godunov and Khovanshchina, cover a period beginning before the Smuta (The Times of Troubles) and ending with Peter the Great ascending the throne. They are something of a manifesto of the narodnik ideal, in which Russia's Slavonic tradition and simple folk, the true heroes of Mussorgsky's masterpieces, were elevated to the highest rank.

Alexander Borodin's *Prince Igor* (or, an opera finished after the composer's death by Nikolai Rimski-Korsakov and Sergey Glazunov, can be described as a crowning achievement of the musical ideals of the narodniks. It combines an epic tale with masterfully-crafted music and a historical spectacle. Instrumental music also drew on the folk tradition. The most common practice for national schools in music is to be inspired by themes from folk songs. A standout example is the second theme in the first movement of Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 1, Op. 23 in B flat minor. It uses a traditional Ukrainian song that the composer heard sung by a blind beggar in a village marketplace near Kiev.

The common denominator of the work of the Russian writers, artists and composers of the time – as well as the hallmark of their historical thought – was a sense of fatalism germinal in the minds of the Russians during the reign of the House of Romanov. It was combined with a certain premonition of an imminent historical collapse that was meant to shake the very foundations of the state and nation. This is reflected in the tragic plight of the literary heroes of Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky, the death of the main characters of the great Russian operas, and the overall mood pervading the people as they witnessed the decline of imperial Russia.

Alexei Vladimirovich Stanchinsky entered the Russian music scene towards the end of the imperial period. He was born on March 9, 1888, in Dolbosunovo, Vladimir province (now Nizhny Novgorod). His father, a clergyman, often changed jobs, which forced the family to move from one place to another. In his early years, Alexei resided in Vladimir, Tver, Pskov, Smolensk (where he graduated from a secondary school), eventually settling in the family estate at Logachyovo. Musically trained themselves, his parents did their best to create conditions that allowed him to study the piano and continue his musical education. His formidable talent was spotted by a prominent Moscow Conservatory professor, Alexander Grechaninov, who one summer visited Dolbosunovo with a group of his colleagues in an effort to get closer to ordinary folk. Grechaninov decided to become Alexei's teacher, and so from 1904 onwards, while still attending high school, the boy started to make visits to Moscow to study music.

In 1907 Stanchinsky entered the Moscow Conservatory, where, on Grechaninov's recommendation, he became a student of Sergey Taneyev, the pre-eminent Conservatory professor, who had studied with Tchaikovsky and taught household names such as Rachmaninov and Scriabin. Thanks to Taneyev's tuition, Stanchinsky developed his talent in the fields of counterpoint and composition. The two analysed Beethoven's sonatas, Bach's fugues and Mozart's quartets, and went on to concerts together. Taneyev gave Stanchinsky books to read and introduced him to Leo Tolstoy, whose literary works influenced much publicity in Russia and made an impact on Alexei's spiritual development. Taneyev stressed the need to shape a musician's style on a personal way, while also arguing that they needed to perfect their individual style in an organic bond to the musical style of their time. It is small wonder then that Stanchinsky became increasingly obsessed with constantly trying to improve his compositional skills. This is reflected in his diaries, which he diligently kept throughout his formative years.

Stanchinsky's diaries also provide evidence of the first symptoms of his mental illness. He saw himself as an intelligent and sensitive man, but also a depressive personality. On one occasion, when asked by Taneyev to come for a class during the day, he was said to have responded: "But I love the night!" He came across as a strange person to fellow students, someone "not quite normal, at any rate unbalanced". A real crisis came in January 1910 (according to family correspondence, or 1908 as some English-language sources would have it) when his beloved father died in Moscow. After a night-long vigil at his coffin, Alexei suffered a nervous breakdown. He was hospitalized for a clinical period of six months. He was diagnosed with dementia praecox (precocious madness), a mental disorder classed as a type of schizophrenia in modern medicine. This personal crisis and the time he spent in the clinic made a lasting impact on the young composer. Even though the resumed his studies in the summer of 1910, he was no longer able to withstand the pressure of living in a big city.

He therefore moved to his family estate at Logachyovo, where he continued his studies with Taneyev in the form of a correspondence course. Incidentally, this is thought to be the first documented instance of someone studying music in such a way. Thanks to that, his large output in this period of his has survived, though some of the scores were destroyed by the composer in outbursts of frenzy. Around that time, he've stopped composing for the piano. His Prelude and Fugue being his only work from that period. Stanchinsky would spend most of the time wandering the countryside in order to broaden and writing down folk tunes. The region he lived in was perfectly suited for this kind of activity. Mikhail Glinka once explored local folkloric sources for his Kalinka, defined by Stanchinsky as a masterpiece.

During the last two years of his life, Stanchinsky reached the peak of his creativity. It was when he penned his several of his key works, including the Piano Sonatas, Sketches and Canon Preludes, his studies in the summer of 1910, he was no longer able to withstand the pressure of living in a big city.

On March 2, 1914, persuaded by his professor, he even gave what was his only public concert, performing his own compositions at the Moscow Conservatory. The event was a great success and the musical circles of Moscow hailed him as a young genius. The composer's rise to fame was cut short by his tragic death. On October 8, 1914, he was found dead by a river close to his home. While the exact circumstances of his death have never been ascertained, it is reasonable to infer that he committed suicide as a result of a nervous breakdown. Prominent musicians organized a commemorative concert in his memory on November 1, 1914. "The disease wreaked his powers, before his great rare talent could reach its upper hand. He was one of those who came along but once in a century".

Stanchinsky's extant output, apart from his student pieces and several compositions that have survived in sketches, comprises the Piano Trio in D major, about twenty works for piano and ten song settings to words by the Scottish poet Robert Burns. While being a fairly modest legacy, there are mature, and in many aspects, visionary compositions. The decade between 1904 and 1914 saw Stanchinsky making great strides in development, both spiritually and compositionally, that were not met by many other composers who lived much longer. The works penned before his nervous breakdown in 1910 were largely mimesis of early works by other composers. One can hear in them echoes of Chopin's lyricism (mazurkas, nocturnes, preludes), and the musical idiom of the period (etudes) and Mussorgsky (Humoresque). There are not imitations, however, but works exhibiting a personal idiom, innovative harmonic ideas and uncommon metrical patterns. The Prelude in the Lydian Mode, based on a modal scale, notated on three staves, is a perfect example.

A predilection for classical forms did not hamper the composer's resolve to develop his own musical language, but it was only the events of 1910 that proved to be the catalyst that allowed Stanchinsky to upgrade his compositional skills in a tangible way. Prelude and Fugue was a harbinger of his more thorough exploration of contrapuntal technique, with the simultaneous use of an harmonic palette bordering on atonality. It is these features, alongside his fascination with folk melodies, that became the hallmarks of Stanchinsky's music. Irina Lapatina, a prominent expert on Stanchinsky and the editor of the first and so far only collection of his works, wrote: "In his music, he blended, in a natural and organic manner, sublime lyricism with creative fantasy, his inspiration drawing on folk melodies with somewhat bizarre and awkward harmonic ideas, a tender and bright pastoral sonorousness with mere barbarism, as well as a colourful, poetically sublime timbre with neo-classical dryness of notation".

Having started by exploring an expanded major-minor tonality in his early works, Stanchinsky then drew on modal phenomena, eventually arriving at using the 12-note chromatic scale while maintaining a tonal orientation. His freedom in employing diatonic scales in the 12-note chromatic scale is characterised by the interdependence and coherence of the internal elements, in each case defined by the artistic goal. The chromaticism in Stanchinsky's music was a trend that resulted in tonal instability, which was particularly evident in his polyphonic compositions, such as the Canon Preludes and fugues. His sense of freedom, courage, imagination and mastery in using advanced contrapuntal techniques surpassed even Taneyev, making Stanchinsky a forerunner of the new polyphonic style that was developed in later decades by such composers as Webern, Shostakovich and Hindemith.

According to Valentina A. Loginova: "In Russian music Stanchinsky became one of the first and finest representatives of neo-classicism, and he proved that the use of classical forms filled with new ideas and style of the musical idiom had lost nothing of its topicality. His compositions evoke associations with the black-and-white palette of a graphic drawing that is dominated by sharp lines, shapes and text, but always pure, rather than decorative. In a prophetic way, he anticipated the vitality and self-sufficiency of constructivism, of geometrical principles governing a musical composition".

It is reasonable to infer that this decade of Stanchinsky's creative evolution was only a stage anticipating further developments in his artistic career. No one will ever know how it would have proceeded had it not been tragically cut short by the composer's premature death. It is to be regretted that his legacy has in a large measure fallen into oblivion, not because of the shortcomings of his music but because of the greatness of his contemporaries who unwillingly pushed him into the abyss of oblivion from the pedestal of the precursor and apologist of neo-classicism.

*Witold Wilczek*
