## Pierre Fournier - Complete Biography (by Iain Hamilton, © 2008)

As the twentieth century recedes from us, Pierre Fournier seems to be the central figure among a galaxy of great cellists. Free of the occasional eccentricities of Casals or Rostropovich on the one hand, virtuosity of Fournier hit the middle ground not in any regular but most magnificently. He was every woman's dream of what a French musician should be – suave, good-looking, with a glamorous tone to match his Gallic gallantry – and he made light of the disability that was the fruit of a childhood illness: a weakness in the ankles that degenerated into polio, everything he played with a kind of nobility; in accordance with his credo *Every passage on the cello must be sung*. He never forced tone but drew it out of the instrument with elegance – the product of all his crisp, precise finger movement warmth and feeling.

Pierre Fournier, as he became, was born in Paris on 24 June 1906. Father Gaston was a distinguished soldier, mother Gabrielle a pianist, younger brother Jean a violinist. Pierre made a start on the piano with his mother but an attack of polio left him with a severe limp and he could no longer open the pedals. At nine he was able to ride again but his feet remained weak thereafter. Through her unique personality it learnt patience and technique and the control of intonation through the use of flexible wrist movements. He was immediately taken up by Bazelaire and André Hekking at the Paris Conservatoire, taking first prize in 1923. He played in Hekking's cello ensemble and in 1925 made his debut with the Lamoureux Orchestra in Lalo's Concerto. Debussy's Sonatine for cello and piano was dedicated to him in 1922; he ceded his place in the quartet to Paul Tortelier, himself was obvious, that he was a soloist. In 1927 he ceded his place in the quartet to Paul Tortelier, himself was obvious, that he was a soloist.

Fournier came to Britain as an orchestral principal before his official London debut in December 1934 – a recital at the Wigmore Hall where he introduced Jean Hurel's Sonata at the Aeolian Hall in 1937 he premiered Roussel's Concerto in Paris and in 1939 gave the first performances of newly-written Martinu Concerto No 1. He settled in Britain for the duration of the Second World War. On 11 January 1939 came his Berlin Philharmonic debut with the Haydn D major Concerto under Paul Paray. In November 1943 he returned to Berlin for four performances of the Schumann Concerto with Wilhelm Furtwängler conducting. For these performances Fournier used a Judas-sized ventures led to his being ostracized by his French colleagues for this time.

From 1949 he played the Elgar Concerto numerous times, and the recording with the Royal Albert Hall in consecutive evenings. Fournier regularly visited Britain, after broadcasting for the new Third Programme in 1946 he played Beethoven's Triple Concerto in London with Artur Schnabel and Arthur Grumiaux and the following year at the first Edinburgh Festival and then in London he played chamber music with Schnaub, Joseph Szigeti and William Primrose. Early in 1948 he premiered Ohlmeyer-Dohnanyi Concerto in Zurich and later that year he toured America for the first time.

Francis Poulenc dedicated a sonata to him and gave him the premiere in Paris in May 1949 also bringing the work to London. That November Fournier made his Carnegie Hall debut with the New York Philharmonic under Stokowski, playing the Schumann Concerto. During the 1950s and 1960s he made a number of American tours under such conductors as Fournier's reputation was enhanced by two concerto by Jean Martinon (1965) and Frank Martin (1966). From 1970 he spent more time at his home in Geneva (where he had settled in 1956), concentrating on teaching – he had previously taught at the École Normale (1937-39) and the Paris Conservatoire (1941-49). In addition to giving private tuition to many musicians, he died in Geneva on 8 January 1986.

Whereas his colleague Paul Tortelier played the Elgar Concerto as a student and was always closely associated with it, Fournier did not take it up until urged to do so by his English pupil Anneliese Rothenburger. It is a measure of his dedication to the music of Elgar that his very first recital devoted to the composer. After that he brought it out regularly and his interpretation, at its best in the 1955 recording with Hans Rosbaud, is full of noble dignity and warmth.

Added interest is lent to this Cologne performance before a live audience by the fact that it is conducted by one of Fournier's most sympathetic colleagues, Hans Rosbaud.

The story of Fournier and the Dvořák Concerto was rather different. Fournier studied the concerto as a young man and played it regularly from 1940. He scored one of his first post-war triumphs with a June 1947 Prague Spring Festival performance with the Czech Philharmonic under Rafael Kubelik, and made several commercial recordings: the first (unpublished) in 1941 and the last with George Szell, a year before the Cologne performance. The two men had originally been introduced by Schnabel and despite a great difference in temperament they got on well together. Fournier called him 'my conductor' and Szell called Fournier 'my cellist'. This record is the Dvořák, having made a celebrated recording with Casals and the Czech Philharmonic.

Our programme ends with one of Beethoven's two sets of variations on themes from Mozart's Zauberflöte. Fournier and Holetschek clearly knew and admired one another. This is an intimate, recital-like known as a pianist, this versatile Viennese artist also performed and recorded on organ and harpsichord.
