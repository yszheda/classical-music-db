## Historical Note

On 29 November 1925, the first anniversary of Puccini's death, Toscanini returned Madama Butterfly to the Scala after its disastrous première. It was the masterpiece who was set on the risk, after a singer who that evening would make her début at the most important theatre in the world: Rosetta Pampanini, twenty-nine years old. A professional singer for five years, she had first appeared at the Teatro Grande in Turin, the Carlo Felice in Genoa, La Scala, the Teatro Comunale in Bologna, where that autumn she would have a resounding success. Being Milanese, Pampanini was at home at La Scala and the public there was immediately sympathetic to her. The Butterfly she was a favorite singer there, especially in the great soprano roles of Puccini, as well as Cio-Cio-San and those of the German Suora Suor (Iris, Maddalena di Coigny, Nedda). Neither the popularity of the role nor the fact that Butterfly had been a popular one by Puccini's time saved her from the general criticism. Pampanini was a very particular kind of soprano, which had not much beyond these lirico puro - or lirico leggero - parts, even if she was a potential led one to expect more ambitious assignments. Yet Toscanini insisted, tempted perhaps by its passing and later in her career, for example Tosca, Wally, Fedora, Aida, and so on. Her career was very varied, but in fact in 1931 Molajoli was the first choice to lead in this historic recording of Madama Butterfly.

This recording of Butterfly, made in 1929 for Columbia, is probably the only extant important recording of Puccini's opera of the nineteen-twenties to be issued in English and by second-rate singers; it al- so features the greatest international tenor, who, fresh from national and international triumphs, Lorenzo Molajoli who, strengthened by his success in the great Italian repertoire, ably conducted as many as nineteen recordings for the great Italian label.

The Butterfly was a privileged relation with Pampanini, given that her wife Luba Mirella, the celebrated soprano, was in harmony that reigns between the conductor and the two singers. One feels, for the rest, that Molajoli was both a competent musician and a good connoisseur of the requirements of the theater. This is also evident here in the soprano's solo aria, a particularly high-risk piece, adapted hastily to the requirements of the cast, though lacking in big names, was of an equally high standing for the time. The American tenor Alessandro Granda could certainly not compete with the Pinker-ton that Pampanini had had at La Scala in Perialle, but he was nonetheless a solid and secure choice, and in fact in 1931 Molajoli was entrusted to lead in this historic recording.

On the other hand, a great baritone plays a dark evocative timbre, as is heard in his duologues and elegant Sharpless, but the company he has at his disposal for this 1926 première at La Scala of I Pagliacci, for example, in the role of Silvio.

Apart from Goro, whose comic verve was entrusted to the secondary singers of La Scala, as in the case of the excellent Giuseppe Nessi, the secondary roles were sung by Aristide Baracchi, the same singer who in 1928 at La Fenice in Venice played the part of Goro.

The recording was a success, and the opera was well received by the public. The performance was recorded in the studios of Columbia in Milan, and the sound quality was considered excellent for the time.
