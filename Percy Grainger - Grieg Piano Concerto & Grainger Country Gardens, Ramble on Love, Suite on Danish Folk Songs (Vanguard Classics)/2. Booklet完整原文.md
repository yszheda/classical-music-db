A HISTORY OF THIS RECORDING

This recording presents the live performances that took place on February 25, 1957, at
Aarhus, the second largest city of Denmark. Mrs. Percy Grainger, the former Ella Ström,
informed Professor Gustav Reese of the existence of this recorded concert; he, in turn,
brought the tapes to my attention. Although the beginning of the performance contained some
many wrong notes and inaccuracies in precision, I felt that the recording contained some
extremely expressive playing that made it unique. A contract was signed, the record
jacket was designed, and 1000 copies of the long playing disc were produced in 1963.

However, because of my concern about the inaccuracies, I asked Harold C. Schonberg,
then the chief Music Critic of The New York Times, to listen to the record and let me know
what he thought. He called back and said that although the playing was for the most
part extraordinary, the inaccuracies might mar the reputation of a great pianist.

We decided to withhold the record. The 1000 copies were put in our warehouse and
that, I thought, was the end of the story. However, some years later, in doing a routine
inventory of the warehouse, we found, to our astonishment, that all 1000 copies of the
disc had disappeared. Presumably someone had "made them available," and, since that
time, this record has become one of the legendary rarities of the LP era, currently selling
to collectors at $1,000.00 or more per copy, on the rare occasions when one becomes
available.

Many years later, on re-hearing the master tapes, we realized that in withholding the
release we had suppressed a rare and important document, a performance that was
coached and approved by the composer himself. Thanks to digital editing, we have
cleaned up the opening of the concerto, which had the most inaccuracies, but otherwise
the record is identical to that which appeared on the original LP. The release of this CD
is intended to bring to the public a document that is both historically important and
musically thrilling.
—Seymour Solomon, Producer

## 内页第三页
# The Grieg Concerto

Grieg's was a strong spirit encased in a sickly body. Since adolescence he had suffered from a collapsed lung and it was the cumulative strain that this put on his heart that in the end caused his death. Born on June 15, 1843, at Bergen, it was only in his early twenties that he made acquaintance with Norwegian folk music. He became one of a small band of creative figures — the most eminent was the novelist, poet, dramatist and later, Nobel Prize winner, Bjornstjerne Bjornson — determined to build an art that would bring the life, traditions, and mind of the Norwegian people to the world. Grieg met hostility at home as well as abroad, but never did he relax in his concern for the plight of his fellow men. In 1899, when France was aflame with the anti-Semitism of the Dreyfus affair, Grieg refused an invitation to conduct his music in Paris, saying in a public letter, "I am shocked at the injustice in your country and do not feel myself able to enter into any relation whatsoever with the French public." A storm of abuse came from the French press, and when a year later Arthur de Greef announced that he would play the Grieg concerto at Lyons, he received threatening letters. But the public applauded the concert.

The Piano Concerto in A minor, Op. 16, was composed in the summer of 1868 when Grieg, twenty-five years old, was living in a cottage near Copenhagen with his wife and young daughter. Its original melodies, which have the feeling of peasant songs, lend an "open air" quality that makes it very different from, for example, the Schumann piano concerto, which Grieg admired and used as a model.

The first movement, Allegro moderato, reveals Grieg's genius and limitations as a composer. There are no awe-inspiring dramatic conflicts or imaginative formal innovations. The movement is based on two main themes, presented first by the orchestra and then the piano. There is a short development section, based on the first theme, followed by the traditional recapitulation and a sparkling transformation of the first theme to make the coda. But the music is imbued with Grieg's consummate originality of lyricism, with a special texture of its own provided by a chromatic harmony that is exactly right for the melodies, a sensitively colorful orchestration, and a marvelously pianistic keyboard writing that from the curtain-raising soloist's statement through the big cadenza — between the recapitulation and coda — is always musical and communicative.

The second movement, Adagio, is extraordinary in its beautiful integration of piano and orchestra, each in its own character, the orchestra opening muted and brooding, the piano entering with delicate filigree passages and then meditating on the orchestral phrases, the whole creating a magically compelling mood from the first note to the last.

The last movement, Allegro marcato, opens with an engaging dance-like melody, highly pianistic, and there is a second melody so captivating that it was later "borrowed" to make a tin-pan-alley hit. It is the second theme that provides the grand climax of the work, which owes its undying power, for pianists and public, to the fact that for all the resplendent virtuosity it demands, there is no hint of glitter for its own sake, and every phrase rings true.

## Percy Grainger's works

Country Gardens, one of the most popular of Grainger's piano compositions, was based on an English Morris Dance tune. The Ramble On Love — Grainger preferred the informal word "Ramble" for what others would call "Fantasia"— is a transcription of the final duet of Richard Strauss's opera Rosenkavalier. Grainger loved the music and felt the impulse to "sing" it with his own marvelously evocative pianism.

The Suite On Danish Folk Songs, for orchestra with piano, shows the tenderness and taste at Grainger's handling of folk music. It is one of the products of Grainger's song collecting tours, in company with his friend Evald Tang Kristensen, in Jutland during the 1920's. The songs used in the four parts, all of them beautiful, are as follows: I — "The Power of Love"; II —"Lord Peter's Stable Boy"; III — "The Nightingale," "The Two Sisters"; IV (Jutish Medley) — "Choosing the Bride," "The Dragoon's Farewell," "The Shoemaker from Jerusalem," "Husband and Wife." The piano here plays a relatively minor role.

## 内页第四页
### Grieg and Grainger

The friendship between Percy Grainger (1882-1961) and Edvard Grieg (1843-1907) in the last two years of Grieg's life is one of the very touching episodes in music history. In May, 1906, Grieg, then sixty-three years old, heard this young Australian titan of the keyboard play a program in London that included two of Grieg's folk dances. He was enraptured, and wrote in his diary, "I have never met anyone who understands me as he does. And he is from Australia. What about the way the critics have blamed my music for being too Norwegian!" A sore point with Grieg had been the sniping at him by some of the stuffier German critics, who asserted that by using melodies that sounded like Norwegian folk music, Grieg was taking a provincial path, and forsaking "universality" (that is, the ability to sound like a German academician). And so for Grieg the fact that a musician born on the other side of the world could so clearly feel his music with such warmth and insight was proof that the path closest to his heart had also been the right one for him as a composer.

The friendship ripened rapidly. Grainger turned pages for Grieg at his concerts, and played Grieg's music at his own recitals. Grieg wrote "There is no Norwegian at present who can touch him . . . though understanding is not found where it should be found, in our own country, it can be found abroad, yes — even in Australia, where the wonderful Percy Grainger was born." This touched on another sore point with Grieg, the fact that the more snobbish people at home were scornful of the thought of a music redolent of the Norwegian countryside and people as the critics abroad.

Grainger proved to be the greatest interpreter Grieg had heard of his piano concerto. A tremendous virtuoso, playing with enormous vitality and bravura, Grainger however had none of the capriciousness of some famous pianists who, to Grieg's pain, tore apart the music of Beethoven and Chopin to make their own spectacular effects. In 1907, Grieg chose Grainger to play his concerto at the September Leeds Festival, where Grieg was to conduct. In the summer of that year, the two men worked closely together, although Grieg was already suffering from his final illness. He wrote again, "As a piano player, I do not know to which of the greatest I should liken him. But all comparison is futile when greatness is the question. He is himself. Possibly I am partial to him because he has actually realized my ideals of piano playing. If I had his technique, my conception of the nature of piano playing would have been exactly the same. It is a man, a great and distinguished man who plays. May life go well for him!" They rehearsed the concerto. Grieg still hoped, despite his weakness, to go to Leeds, but on September 3rd he died.

### Percy Grainger

Grainger, born in Melbourne in 1882, had become a brilliant pianist at the age of ten, able to earn enough money from a concert tour to go to Germany. He studied with Ferruccio Busoni. He was received by the critics as one of the great pianists, but he soon acted like a rebel, detesting the stuffed-shirt atmosphere that gathered about the concert artist, preferring to go off on trips collecting folk songs — of which he wrote many beautiful arrangements — taking long hikes and sometimes appearing at a concert date without having had time to change from his hiking clothes. He had a breezy disregard for formalities, and was happiest when he felt that his music was bringing him into companionship with the common people whom he belonged.

On February 11, 1915, Grainger made his New York debut in a solo recital. The critic of Musical America quoted Schumann's famous remark on Chopin, "Hats off, gentleman, a genius!" and added "Grainger's performances are surcharged with electricity." Henry T. Finck wrote, "In less than half an hour he convinced his critical audience that he belonged in the same rank as Paderewski and Kreisler." On March 12 he played the Grieg concerto with the New York Philharmonic Orchestra, and Finck again wrote in the New York Post, "He is the greatest of them all . . . He certainly played the concerto as no other pianist has ever played it here, with a poetic insight, a variety of tonal effects and shadings, a tenderness and delicacy in the soft passages and a dash and brilliancy in the final movement that was altogether enchanting."

Grainger became an American citizen. When the United States entered the first World War, in 1917, Grainger disappeared, and his friends were later astonished to discover that this famous concert artist had enlisted to become Pvt. Grainger. The army, when it discovered his musical fame, put him into a band as a saxophone player. He lived in America for most of the remainder of his life, occasionally giving concerts that reminded critics of how great a pianist he was, and writing more of the modest and captivating pieces like "Shepherds Hey" and "Country Gardens" that made his name familiar to countless people. In 1920 his edition of Grieg's piano concerto was published, and H.T. Finck wrote, "Grainger gleaned a priceless body of experiences concerning Grieg's intentions regarding the rendering of the concerto, including expression marks written by Grieg himself into the score of the work, as well as his own notes descriptive of Grieg's way of playing the solo part . . . Here is a real message from the spirit of the dead. Musicians will know henceforth exactly how Grieg wanted his concerto to be played. He has spoken through his disciple."

Grainger died at his home in White Plains on February 20, 1961.
