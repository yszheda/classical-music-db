## A HISTORY OF THIS RECORDING

This recording presents the live performance that took place on February 25, 1957, at Aarhus, the second largest city of Denmark. Mrs. Percy Grainger, the former Fella Morrison, informed Professor Gustav Reese of the existence of this recorded concert; he, in turn, brought the tapes to my attention. Although the beginning of the performance contained many wrong notes and inaccuracies in precision, I felt that the recording contained some extremely impressive playing that made it unique. A contract was signed, the record jacket was designed, and 1000 copies of the record were produced in 1961.

However, because of my concern about the inaccuracies, I asked Harold C. Schönberg, then the chief Music Critic of The New York Times, to listen to the record and let me know what he thought. He called back and said that although the playing was for the most part extraordinary, he thought the record might not be representative of a great pianist.

We decided to withhold the record. The 1000 copies were put in our warehouse and, like I thought, was the end of the story. However, some years later, when one of the inventory men died, we found, to our astonishment, that all 1000 copies of the disc had disappeared. Presumably someone had "made them available," and at the time of this writing has become one of the legendary rarities of the LP era, currently selling to collectors at $1,000.00 or more per copy, on the rare occasions when one becomes available.

Many years later, on re-hearing the master tapes, we realized that in withholding the release we had suppressed a rare and important document, a performance that was coached and approved by the composer himself. Thanks to digital editing, we have cleaned up the worst of the wrong notes, which had most inexcusably intruded; the record is identical to that which appeared on the original LP. The release of this CD is intended to bring to the public a document that is both historically important and musically thrilling.

**—Seymour Solomon, Producer**

## THE GRIEG CONCERTO

Grieg's life was a strong spirit encased in a sickly body. Since adolescence he had suffered from a collapsed lung and it was the cumulative strain that this put on his heart that led, in the end, to his death on September 4, 1907. He was born in Bergen, Norway, in early 1843. In early twenties that he made acquaintance with Norwegian folk music. He became one of a small band of creative figures — the most eminent was the novelist, poet, dramatist Henrik Ibsen — who were imbued with a sense of a national culture, an art that would bring the life, traditions, and mind of the Norwegian people to the world. Grieg's Piano Concerto in A Minor, Op. 16 was composed in the summer of 1868 at Sorgenfri, a country resort near Copenhagen. He had gone there to recover from the plague of his fellow men. In 1869, when France was aflame with the anti-Semite, anti-Prussian fervor of the Commune, he took his new Concerto to Paris, saying in a public letter, "I am shocked at the injustice in your country and the violence of your passions. I want to show my own people that we are a nation apart."

A storm of abuse came from the French press, and when a year later Arthur de Goury announced that he would play the Grieg concerto at Lyons, he received threatening letters. But the work itself apparently was not affected.

The **Piano Concerto in A minor, Op. 16** was composed in the summer of 1868 at Sorgenfri, a country resort near Copenhagen. Grieg was twenty-five years old, recently married, and a young daughter. Its original melodies, which have the feeling of peasant songs, long before the folk music vogue, and the whole design is that of the Schumann piano concerto, which Grieg admired and used as a model.

The first movement, *Allegro moderato*, is a mixture of lyricism and limitations as a composer. There are no awe-inspiring dramatic conflicts or imaginative formal innovations. The movement is based on two main themes, presented first by the orchestra and then by the piano. There is a short cadenza, based on a variant of the first theme, followed by the traditional recapitulation and a sparkling transformation of the first theme to the second major. *Adagio*, which is almost pastoral in the simplicity of its lyricism, with a special texture of its own provided by a chromatic harmony that touches the theme on the melodic line in the woodwinds and the brass. The music is lyrical keyboard writing that from the curtain-raising soloist's statement through the long and ardent love scene between the recapitulation and coda — is always musical and communicative.

The second movement, *Adagio*, is extraordinary in its beautiful integration of piano and orchestra, and in its own character, the orchestra opening muted and brooding, the piano entering with delicate filigree passages and then meditating on the orchestral phrases, the whole creating a magically compelling mood from the first note to the last.

The last movement, *Allegro marcato*, opens with an engaging dance-like melody, highly pianistic, and there is a second melody so captivating that it was later "borrowed" to make a tin-pan-alley hit. It is the second theme that provides the grand climax of the work, which owes its undying power, for pianists and public, to the fact that for all the resplendent virtuosity it demands, there is no hint of glitter for its own sake, and every phrase rings true.

## Percy Grainger's Works

**Country Gardens**, one of the most popular of Grainger's piano compositions, was based on an English Morris Dance tune. **The Ramble On Love** — Grainger preferred the informal word "Ramble" for what others would call "Fantasia" — is a transcription of the final duet of Richard Strauss's opera *Rosenkavalier*. Grainger loved the music and felt the impulse to "sing" it with his own marvelously evocative pianism.

The **Suite On Danish Folk Songs**, for orchestra with piano, shows the tenderness and taste at Grainger's handling of folk music. It is one of the products of Grainger's song collecting tours, in company with his friend Evald Tang Kristensen, in Jutland during the 1920's. The songs used in the four parts, all of them beautiful, are as follows: I — "The Power of Love"; II — "Lord Peter's Stable Boy"; III — "The Nightingale," "The Two Sisters"; IV (Jutish Medley) — "Choosing the Bride," "The Dragoon's Farewell," "The Shoemaker from Jerusalem," "Husband and Wife." The piano here plays a relatively minor role.

**—Notes by S. W. Bennett**

## Grieg and Grainger

The friendship between Percy Grainger (1882-1961) and Edvard Grieg (1843-1907) in the last two years of Grieg's life is one of the very touching episodes in music history.

In May 1906, Grieg, then sixty-three years old, heard the young Australian titan at the keyboard play a program in London that included two of Grieg's folk dances. He was enraptured, and wrote in his diary, "I have never met anyone who understands me as he does. And far from doing the music that was mine — taking what I have written seriously — he seems to see two Norwegians! A sore point with me at the sitting at his home and his performance, I felt that as a player he was one of the elect, that he could, like Norwegian folk music, Grieg was taking a provincial path, and forsaking 'Genuine Music' to become a pillar of the establishment and, in so doing, to lose the very fact that a musician born on the other side of the world could so clearly feel his music with such strength and insight was proof that the path chosen to its core had been the right one for him as a composer."

Grainger chose Grieg to play his concertos at the Seppehus who, to Grieg's wife, support the music of Beethoven and Chopin in a way that no one else could.

Grieg was to meet him. In the autumn of that year, the two men worked closely together, although Grieg was already suffering from his final illness. He wrote again, "As a piano player I do not know which of the greatest I should liken him to. If I had his technique, my conception of the nature of piano playing would have been exactly the same. It is a man, a great and distinguished man who plays. May his life go well for him!"

Grieg and Grainger spent as much time together as they could. Grainger later rehearsed the concerto. Grieg still hoped, despite his weakness, to go to Leeds, but on September 3rd he died.

## Percy Grainger

Percy Grainger was born in Melbourne in 1882. He became a brilliant pianist at the age of ten, able to earn enough money from a concert tour to go to Germany. He studied with Iwan Knorr in Frankfurt. He was reviled by the critics as one of the great pianists, but they also acted like a rebel, destroying the stuffed-shirt atmosphere that gathered around the concert artist, preferring to go out on trips collecting folk songs — of which he wrote to the whole of Europe and the United States — rather than practice his instrument. He did not have time to change from his hiking clothes. He had a breezy arrogance that he thought was the greatest of them all. He played the Grieg Concerto as no other pianist has ever played it here.

On February 25, 1957, Grainger traveled to Aarhus to solo the Grieg Piano Concerto with the Aarhus Municipal Orchestra. The performance was recorded and later released by Vanguard Classics.

Grainger became an American citizen. When the United States entered the Second World War in December 1941, Grainger, then 59, was too old for the draft. He enlisted and was assigned to the Army Signal Corps as a radio operator. He served in Europe and the Pacific. After the war, he continued to perform and record.

Grainger died at his home in White Plains on February 20, 1961.
