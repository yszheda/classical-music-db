## 内页文章
### JUBILEE CONCERT: CELEBRATING THE 80TH BIRTHDAY OF DISTINGUISHED PIANIST BELLA DAVIDOVICH
Bella Davidovich, one of the best-known representatives of the Russian piano tradition, celebrates her 80th birthday in July of 2008. This may come as a surprise to many. It is not only her appearance, but also the quality and quantity of her continuous performances, throughout her extraordinary career, that defy this age. She remains one of the few women pianists of her time to achieve and sustain worldwide recognition.

After some 70 years on the concert stage (at the age of nine she played the Beethoven Piano Concerto No. 1 with the Philharmonic Orchestra in her native city of Baku, Azerbaijan), she is still in demand around the world as a soloist and a chamber player, as well as a juror for the most prestigious music competitions. During the 2007-2008 season she performed in Germany, Spain, Holland and New York.

Most importantly, she plays with astonishing freshness and energy. Her interpretations continue to exemplify the best qualities of great piano playing: careful attention to the musical text and to matters of style; deep understanding of the composer's intentions and historical perspective; and a perfect command of the expressive resources of the piano. Hers was always a style of precision, clarity and refinement, "accuracy and vigor" (to quote the New York Times), enriched immensely by a wide cultural background, and achieved through a remarkable creative honesty and work ethic. Through the years her extensive experiences in the concert world have added to this magic potion.

How does Ms. Davidovich herself explain her creative longevity? "It is simple," she says. "Music and piano playing are my life. My entire existence is connected with this instrument. I find the biggest joy in the process of work, of learning or revisiting and polishing my repertoire, of discovering something new in old pieces, something which I did not notice before. The classical heritage is so rich, that it gives me endless opportunities for such discoveries. When I was younger, I toured more, but that meant less time to study the text, to think about details, to explore all interpretive possibilities. Now I have more time for that and I am more attentive to the tiniest nuances, which probably escaped my attention earlier in my career.

"Of course, physical problems can arise and become a burden. It is not as easy as before to fly back and forth between America and Europe or to sustain two hours of a solo recital. I prefer now to play with an orchestra or a chamber group. This also gives me the opportunity to perform more with my son. Actually, I feel more responsibility while performing with him: I must be in ideal shape in order not to fail him."

Ms. Davidovich's son, violinist and conductor Dmitry Sitkovetsky, has been a frequent performing partner since he was very young. "As a child, I knew that I had an unusual mom: she toured a lot, and when she played in Moscow, I was taken to her concerts. However, her real uniqueness escaped me for years. Pretty early she began to accompany my performances. As an 11-year old violin student of professor Y. Yankilevich at the Central Music School of the Moscow Conservatory, I started to prepare for the Concertino Prague first international competition. It is a Radio competition, so we had to send a tape, and Mom accompanied me on that recording; we played Handel's Sonata #4 and Martinu's Sonatine. I got the first prize, and we performed together in Prague at the winners' concert. Later, in 1973, she accompanied me at my big recital debut in Moscow. But I often argued with her: I had my own creative ideas, and defended them furiously.

"Then I emigrated to the United States, ruining her flourishing career in the Soviet Union. She followed me to America a year later. Soon we began to perform together again. Only later did I realize that she is not only a wonderful artist but the most professional musician I have ever met in my entire life. On no occasion was she late for a rehearsal or, God forbid, for a concert, and no matter what, she is always 150 percent prepared. This does not mean that she practices more than others. But the quality of her preparation, her attitude toward her work, her work ethic, her knowledge of the text is outstanding — on the level of perfection. That is why conductors have always loved to perform with her. This unique musical honesty and responsibility is one of the major secrets of her art."

A few among the many distinguished conductors who have performed with Ms. Davidovich are Semyon Bychkov, Sergiu Comissiona, James Conlon, Christoph von Dohnanyi, Charles Dutoit, Christoph Eschenbach, Neeme Järvi, Mariss Jansons, Neville Marriner, Riccardo Muti, Eugene Ormandy, Kiril Kondrashin, Raymond Leppard, Mstislav Rostropovich, Kurt Sanderling, Leonard Slatkin, Yevgeny Svetlanov, Yuri Temirkanov, Klaus Tennstedt, Michael Tilson Thomas and Edo de Waart.

Another secret of Bella Davidovich's art is her schooling. Even now she never fails to mention her teachers. "They taught me well. I was lucky, because from the very beginning they instilled in me a very serious attitude toward music and the piano."

Growing up in Baku with its rich musical life, and being part of a family with a strong professional lineage in music, definitely helped. Her maternal grandfather was the Concertmaster of the orchestra at the State Opera Theater of Azerbaijan; her mother was a voice coach at the same company; and little Bella soon picked up and repeated tunes and even harmonies of the music performed by her mother's students or heard on the radio. When she was three and a half, she played one of Chopin's Waltzes on the piano, by ear. By the age of six she was attending the Special Music School for gifted children, and soon began to perform on stage. The great Konstantin Igumnov (1873-1948), one of the pillars of the Russian Romantic piano tradition and a prominent professor of the Moscow Conservatory, noticed her and agreed to take her into his class, making an exception for such a young student; she was just 12.

World War II interrupted her Moscow studies. She returned to the Moscow Conservatory only in 1946, and while in her third year there (after Igumnov's death she studied with Igumnov's former student, another outstanding pianist, Yakov Fliyer), she was chosen to represent the Soviet Union at the Frederick Chopin competition in Warsaw, one of the most important piano competitions in the world. She won first prize (sharing it with Halina Czerny-Stefanska of Poland), which launched her extraordinary concert career.

A student, and then a professor, at the legendary Moscow Conservatory, Ms. Davidovich witnessed — and later became a key figure of — its Golden Age. The giants of the older generation of the Russian piano school, such as Igumnov, Goldenweiser and Neuhaus, were still teaching and performing. The most brilliant young talents of the country polished their art under the tutelage and influence of these masters. The first international competitions, and the example of the first Soviet winners, such as Gilels, Fliyer and Zak, as well as the first news from the West, served as a powerful stimulus for her creative development.

Chopin, Schumann, Mendelssohn – romantic music of the 19th century — became a cornerstone and much-loved part of Ms. Davidovich's repertoire. She was also praised for her interpretations of Beethoven, Mozart and Bach, as well as Scriabin, Prokofiev and many other composers. The tragic loss in 1958 of her husband, the outstanding violinist Julian Sitkovetsky, seemed to propel her into concentrating more on her work. She combined her performances around the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe (the West was closed for a long time to most Soviet musicians) and her recording activities, with teaching (for 16 years) at her alma mater, the Moscow Conservatory.

The year 1978 saw the beginning of her life in America. Scarcely known in the USA, she soon won American audiences, beginning with her sold-out Carnegie Hall debut. Overcoming the difficulties of adapting to a new country, a new language and lifestyle, she kept a very busy schedule performing around the world and teaching at the Juilliard School in New York. In 1988, at the time of perestroika, she returned to Russia — the first among musician-émigrés — for a series of triumphant performances. She continues to perform, among other places, in the former Soviet Union, including her native city of Baku.

This recently recorded "Jubilee" program, initiated by Constantine Orbelian and planned as a celebration of Ms. Davidovich's 80th birthday, echoes the manner in which she celebrated her 70th and 75th birthdays. On both occasions, at the invitation of the Concertgebouw in Amsterdam, where she has been a frequent and well-loved guest, her birthday celebrations were marked by special concert performances together with her son, friends and students.

Both mother and son have a special place in their hearts for the Ernest Chausson (1855-1899) Concerto in D Major for Violin, Piano and String Quartet, heard here in Dmitry Sitkovetsky's arrangement for Violin, Piano and String Orchestra. The great violinist David Oistrakh first introduced this work to Soviet audiences, and Ms. Davidovich performed it numerous times with Oistrakh and the Taneev String Quartet. They were scheduled to play it again when the news came from Amsterdam that Mr. Oistrakh had died suddenly from a heart attack. Bella Davidovich, together with the Taneev Quartet and one of Oistrakh's former students, Liana Isaakadze, performed the Chausson Concerto at the Memorial Concert for Oistrakh. Ever since that time she has performed this Concerto numerous times with Mr. Sitkovetsky.

The Concerto (1889-1891) is one of the few well-known works of the French composer, a student of Massenet and a stylistic link between Franck and Debussy. Its four-movement structure is remarkable for its complex, but always "singing" texture; its soaring and elegant melodies, rooted in French song idiom; its deep lyricism (especially poignant in the elegiac third movement) and dramatic intensity of the finale. In this satisfying version of the Concerto, with solo violin, piano and string orchestra, the Moscow Chamber Orchestra strings lend a discreet richness to the texture, while maintaining the flexibility demanded of a string quartet.

The Piano Concerto in G Minor by Felix Mendelssohn (1809-1847) on this recording is a collaboration with conductor Constantine Orbelian and the Moscow Chamber Orchestra. Interestingly, Ms. Davidovich turned to this Concerto only later in life, in America, as a mature musician. "I find Mendelssohn's G Minor Concerto very appealing and not 'child-like' at all, as many think. I enjoyed performing it not long ago at the Great Hall of the Moscow Conservatory with the Philharmonia of Russia and Constantine Orbelian, for a very demanding and discriminating Russian audience, with many of my colleagues in attendance. They thought it was a true success."

The Concerto, written in 1831, two years before the famous Italian Symphony, during the most prolific and busy period of Mendelssohn's life, is a perfect example of the composer's sunny and harmonious style. It is energetic and tuneful, full of virtuosity and lyricism. The Sonata Allegro of the first movement is based on the opposition of the heroic main theme and the dreamy, arioso-like second. The beautiful meditative middle movement puts the piano in a true leading role, giving the soloist many opportunities to demonstrate a wide range of colors. The presto finale is a fascinating and charming journey from initial anxiety, to playfulness, to a triumphant conclusion.

Dmitry Sitkovetsky, world-renowned violinist and conductor, is the Music Director of the Greensboro Symphony, Principal Guest Conductor of the Russian State Symphony in Moscow and Conductor Laureate of the Ulster Orchestra.

As violin soloist, chamber musician, and conductor, Sitkovetsky's compelling artistry has firmly established him as one of the foremost talents of his generation whose music making speaks eloquently to audiences throughout the world.

His career as a concerto soloist in North America has taken him to the major orchestras of the continent, including New York and Los Angeles philharmonics, the Cleveland Orchestra, the Chicago Symphony, the Philadelphia Orchestra, the orchestras of St. Louis, Toronto, Detroit, Baltimore, Indianapolis, Atlanta, Cincinnati and Seattle, the New York Chamber Symphony, Ravinia, Hollywood Bowl, Blossom and Mostly Mozart festivals. He has collaborated with such renowned conductors as Claudio Abbado, Vladimir Ashkenazy, Herbert Blomstedt, Sir Colin Davis, Kurt Masur, Wolfgang Sawallisch, Sir Neville Marriner, Mariss Jansons, Yuri Temirkanov and Michael Tilson-Thomas.
