## John Khouri

John Khouri studied modern piano in Geneva, London, Leeds and Seattle with pianists Fanny Waterman, Béla Síki and Eva Bernathova. He played concertos in England with the New Philharmonia and London Symphony orchestras under Sir John Pritchard, Edward van Remoortal and David Lloyd-Jones. In 1980 he gave up playing the modern concert grand and began his study of early English and German pianos. He owns six pianos, 4 Broadwood grands (1801, 1808, 1812 and 1833) a German grand by Jacob Pfister (6 octaves) and a copy of the Johann Schmidt pedal piano in the Metropolitan Museum, New York. He has recorded all of these pianos and regularly uses them for concerts and lecture-recitals throughout California. # Clementi: Works for Pianoforte

## Clementi's Op. 40 Sonatas

This is the first time these three great sonatas have been recorded not only on a piano of Clementi's time but also on a piano from his own workshop. Precise dating of the piano is not possible at the present time but it would seem to have been made before the disastrous fire which destroyed Clementi's piano factory in March 1807. Precise dating of the composition of these works is also difficult as Clementi seems regularly to have destroyed his autographs after publication but the first edition appeared in early September 1802 shortly after Clementi's departure for the Continent. It is intriguing that they are called Opus 40 Book 1 as Book 2 never appeared and there has been speculation that the 3 Sonatas Op. 50 may already have been "in the works." The Op. 40 Sonatas were a major publication and were brought out also in Paris and Vienna. Clementi undoubtedly labored long and hard on them, refining and polishing until his thoughts and ideas were precisely conveyed. That they are so unlike his previous 57 solo sonatas should not surprise us for they mirror the Clementi who had well and truly "arrived" – an eminently successful performer, teacher, composer and entrepreneur. These sonatas are immensely assured and in their variety of texture, their vibrant color, their fantasy and imaginative ideas and enormous energy, they are unique in his oeuvre. As in so many of his other sonatas, Clementi continues to expand his horizons, tirelessly experimenting with the form of the piano sonata. This set opens with Clementi's only Sonata with 4 independent movements, the traditional Minuet or Scherzo 3rd movement being replaced by 2 Canons, the 2nd in the tonic minor in place of a trio. In Op. 40, No. 2, the two Allegro movements are preceded by slow introductions and very extensive introductions at that, the 2nd recurring in the Allegro finale at a point where one would expect the recapitulation to begin (the Presto ending becomes both recapitulation and Coda). And Clementi alters the conventional approach to writing 1st movements by greatly expanding his development sections, sometimes adding completely new fantasy-like sections as in Op. 40, No. 3.

Finally, Clementi's harmonic vocabulary seems to have expanded and becomes positively Schubertian (Op. 40, No. 1 IV and Op. 40, No. 3 III).

How fortunate we are that this wonderful piano has been preserved and that we can hear these sonatas from Clementi's golden years in all their beauty.

## Playing the Clementi Piano

Clementi grands are very special instruments. Richard Burnett calls them 'the aristocrats' of the English pianos and he is right. Clementi's grands always seem to have the most wonderful, effortless bass sound, organ-like yet luminous and clear. The sound of these instruments couldn't be different from German grands of the same period. Instead of sharply defined, precise sounds, we have a sound which is slow to "speak" and of great beauty. They are also quite different from Broadwood grands as their sound is more covered and veiled.

At first the Clementi action feels heavy and this can be explained by the keys being shorter than comparable Broadwood grands and with a quite different balance point. Yet the action is very reliable and one soon adjusts to the extra weight.

The piano is quite remarkable in other ways. It was tuned by me in May 1998 to A 430 but when I came to use it for this recording it had gone up, quite evenly in pitch to c.A 442. It was moved 50 miles for the recording and it was still basically in tune even though it was now October and it hadn't been touched since May. It took perhaps a half hour to retune some unisons and then the piano was ready to be recorded for the first time. I decided to leave it at its higher pitch as early pianos react badly to drastic raising or lowering of pitch. It is always a great pleasure to play this wonderful piano especially in Clementi's own sonatas.

Clementi himself was constantly improving his models both technically and musically. We see many differences in quality and tone throughout the early development of his pianos. This piano in particular has a very warm tone as well as an unusually beautiful una-corda stop.

Structurally, the case is thicker by 1/3 than other pianos of the period. The interior framing is much thicker and improved in design and the bracework is closer to the soundboard. The belly-rail is solid and not split into two sections like the Broadwood pianos.

Although not as ornate as a lot of other surviving pianos, the nameboard oval which has a pearl-studded black background with gold lettering is a holdover from the Longman-Broderip period (pre 1798) and was apparently discontinued after the fire that destroyed the Clementi factory in March 1807.

John Longman, after his separation from Clementi & Co. in 1800, also used this same style oval up to c. 1806 on his barrel organs etc.

### History

This Clementi was purchased at auction by Alfred Koch Antiques of San Francisco sometime in the late 1950's or early 60's. No earlier history is presently known.

The condition of this piano was appalling. Although complete and mostly original, except for hammers and strings, the case was warped and the tuning block had disintegrated. The keys were badly warped. A lot of work and time was involved to bring this instrument up to good playable and authentic condition. All materials and efforts are as close to the original concept of the builder as possible.

After restoration, Mrs. Koch felt strongly that this piano should be placed somewhere it could be properly used, maintained, and appreciated. Consequently through her association with Mrs. Frank de Bellis, this piano became an important part of the Frank de Bellis collection of San Francisco, a world famous library of music manuscript and literary resource.

## About the Frank V. De Bellis Collection

The de Bellis Collection comprises over 30,000 documents and artifacts and represents a lifetime of collecting by Frank V. de Bellis, a San Francisco business man and patron of Italian culture. Presented as a gift to San Francisco State University in 1963 and housed permanently on campus in the J. Paul Leonard Library, the collection includes manuscripts, rare editions, prints, books, musical scores, instruments and recordings, which illustrate Italian contributions to the development of Western culture. Through his generosity, Frank de Bellis has made his valuable collection available to students and scholars throughout the world.

Musical items number around 10,000, with strengths in 14th and 15th century manuscripts and 18th century scores. Of special interest is Mr. de Bellis' collection of 22,500 vocal recordings, capturing rare examples of early vocal artists such as Caruso, Besi Abbott, Frieda Hemple, Fanny Toreisello, etc., on the recording industry's earliest wax cylinders and platters. Many labels are represented, as well as the entire vocal output of Fonotipia and several extremely rare Zonophones. About 3,000 of these recordings are 78 rpm and 33 1/3 rpm discs, which were used for broadcasting Mr. de Bellis' long-standing radio program entitled *Music of the Italian Masters*.

## Notes on Op. 40

### No. 1, G major

I. Listen carefully to the opening turn or grupetto as it is frequently used at strategic points (e.g. the introduction of the 2nd group and the lead-up to the recapitulations). The restless and passionate development moves through G, C, A, D, F# and E minors before settling down to G major and the recapitulation.

II. Form - A, B1, A, B2. The A sections are in E major – B1 in C# minor, B2 in E minor. Note the luxuriant figuration in the right hand – groups of 5's, 14's, 7's and 20's.

III. Canon I – The tenor part strictly mirrors the opening treble line throughout. Canon II – In G minor, the tenor always moves in contrary motion to the treble. Canon I is repeated giving an ABA form.

IV. A magical movement with gossamer figuration and a barely concealed emotion throughout. There is a large, highly-charged and very experimental middle section in G minor which is derived from the opening idea and vigorously expanded using sequences and a highly spiced harmonic texture which is developed to an anguished climax. This leads directly to a drastically shortened presentation of section I and the exciting ending. Note the 16 bars of "open pedal" where the 2 pedals are down throughout – on one string and raised dampers creating a gorgeous wash of sound.

### No. 2 in B minor

Always the most popular of the set, this is a powerful and exciting work.

I. Harsh 5ths and astringent harmonies lead to the fiery Allegro. The large and difficult development begins in G major and moves restlessly through D minor, B major and minor, Eb minor and major, G minor, D minor and C major before returning to B minor.

II. Appoggiaturas are extensively used for the quirky Largo which also introduces the opening idea for the Allegro. After a return of the Largo there is a furious Presto ending featuring Scarlatti-like hand crossings.

### No. 3 in D major

I. A gravely beautiful 13 bar Introduction with some startling dissonances introduces the suave, very orchestral opening Allegro, so strangely reminiscent of Beethoven's Sonata in D, Op. 28. The superb development is extensively enlarged by a shimmering insert in B minor which dissolves into a long trill leading back to the 1st theme.

II. Haydnesque in D minor leading directly to III. Plaintive pleas are roughly answered by bass octaves.

III. A seemingly inconsequential triplet motif which opens nonchalantly soon assumes a very large role as it whirs around, passing from one hand to another. It reappears as a stern canon in D minor and is ingeniously inverted. This leads to a most original and exciting climax which must have intrigued Franz Schubert for he used similar material in his C minor Sonata. The opening section is repeated in shortened form for a scintillating close.

The four movements of this sonata have seen described as "loose sprawling structures" and the whole work as "a collection of miscellanea." I couldn't disagree more. Clementi's writing is masterly and well-organized with an amazing control of large structure.

## 4 Monferrinas

Clementi published a set of 12 Monferrinas in 1821 and they are dedicated to Barbarina Frigerio an Italian pianist resident in Milan. All of these dances (originating in the state of Monferrato) are in 6/8 time and are beautifully written.

No. 3 in E is perhaps my favorite with its sighing, sinking suspensions and warm sentiment. No. 5 in A has a middle section in the high treble which sounds silvery and ethereal while No. 7 in D features a drone bass in its minor middle section which makes a ghostly impression when played with the una corda and raised dampers.

## Grand Pianoforte

**Muzio Clementi & Co. Seri. # 6213, No. 662, c. 1806-1807**
*Cheapside, London*

Bjarne Dahl of Sunnyvale, California restored the Clementi grand piano in 1968 and writes about the piano and its history:

The English "Pianoforte" is of the 5 1/2 octave (F-C) type that existed in England from 1789 to around 1810. It has ivory naturals and ebony sharps and is 7 feet, 5 inches long, 4 feet, 2 inches wide, and 1 foot deep. It sits upon a trestle stand with castors and it has two pedals, one for una-corda and one for the damper raiser, or sustaining. There are 3 strings throughout and the bass strings are of straight strung brass. There is a "split" bridge. The diameter range for string thickness goes from .016 treble to .028 bass. On the treble keyboard cheek there is a stop block lever which was originally designed for tuning purposes. When the una corda pedal is depressed, the keyboard moves from left to right and the hammers play on two strings. When the block lever is pulled up, the hammers play on one string when the pedal is depressed. So in effect one has 3 tone changes for musical effect as well as for tuning purposes.

Even though larger octave instruments were built in England from 1794, this 5 1/2 octave type was the most commercially popular up to 1810. After this period, the 6 octave type was preferred and later on the octave range was gradually increased.
