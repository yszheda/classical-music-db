# Música callada

Subtitled "Suite No. 1," Música callada ('Silent Music') is the masterpiece on which Federico Mompou built his reputation – and created something unique in the literature of the piano. In its 28 brief pieces, we hear a music of quiet contemplation, of an intimacy so close that it verges on the "silent" of the title. And yet there is nothing meagre or undernourished about it: the music speaks with its own gentle urgency, and sustains a mood that is at once reticent and profoundly expressive. "I believe in the music that is heard with the soul more than with the ear," wrote Mompou, and that belief is embodied in every bar of this magical score.

Mompou composed Música callada over a period of more than thirty years, completing the first group of pieces in 1917, and finishing the work as a whole in 1949. This was the era of musical upheaval: the first generation of modernism had come and gone, the avant-garde had staked its claim, and the century's great aesthetic wars were being fought out. Mompou stood aside from all this. He was a Catalan composer who had returned to his native Barcelona after studies in Paris, where he had been befriended by Gabriel Fauré and others. His music, like that of his near-contemporary Erik Satie, is the antithesis of grand Romantic statement. He believes, it seemed, in small things. "Art is a language which translates the intangible into tangible form," he said. "The infinite cannot be measured. It is more beautiful to suggest it than to state it."

In the early 1920s Mompou published his first piano works, among them Scènes d'enfants and Cants màgics. They were an immediate success. Critics compared him to Chopin and Debussy, and even to the young Beethoven. And when the first book of Música callada appeared in 1927, Mompou's place in contemporary music was secure. Yet his music found itself out of step with the increasingly loud, aggressive and complex character of twentieth-century composition. He wrote little in his later years, and for a long time he was out of fashion. He lived to see the revival of his reputation in his final decade, and died in 1987 at the age of 94. Today we can hear him afresh, in a musical world which his art so clearly anticipated. We may even think that what once seemed a weakness – the quietness, the repetitions, the deliberate restriction of his palette – is precisely what makes his music compelling now.

As Mompou himself acknowledged, Música callada reflects the atmosphere of the cloisters and gardens of the monastery of Montserrat in Catalonia, which he visited as a young man. "Sometimes one feels that one can hear the silence," he wrote. And at every turn this music touches that silence, gives it voice, makes it sing. It speaks of reverie, of meditation, of the world seen in a grain of sand. Its harmonies – often modal, sometimes hovering between major and minor, touched by the modes of Spanish folk music and of Gregorian chant – create a world in which time seems to stand still. Its melodies haunt the memory, they seem to have existed always, like plainchant. And yet there is nothing archaic or retrospective about the art of Mompou. He is a composer of our time, who found his own path between traditionalism and modernism, and spoke in an individual voice that is unmistakably his own.

In the 28 pieces that go to make up the whole work, there is infinite variety within the consistent quietness. One can single out the rapt meditation of the first piece, the luminous sweetness of the "Pregària a la Mare de Déu" (No.5), the deep intimacy of "Vall" (No.10), the limpid beauty of "Nuatges" (No.13), the gentle gaiety of "Dança de la fada" (No.17), the gravely beautiful "Cançó de l'amor que passa" (No.18), the chaste passion of "Claror de lluna sobre l'aigua" (No.21), the mysterious stillness of "El pont de l'olivera" (No.23), the sombre power of "Cada passo que dono" (No.24), the quiet ecstasy of "Cantar de la nit obscura" (No.28). Such a list, of course, does no more than scratch the surface. This is a work that must be absorbed whole, in the continuous, gentle flow that its composer gave it. It becomes what its title says it is: a silent music that speaks to us directly in those quiet spaces of the mind where art, nature and contemplation meet.

The music of Música callada has attracted the interest of some distinguished pianists in the past, though it has been strangely neglected in the recording studio. Mompou himself always acknowledged that it was music that needed to be played with the utmost sensitivity, and he admired the way in which the young German pianist Herbert Henck has approached it. "He understands me," he said, and that is a commendation that speaks for itself.

Herbert Henck, who was born in 1948, studied in Detmold with Amadeus Webersinke and Hans-Olrich Hudelholz. From his earliest appearances he has been associated with adventurous repertory, from the music of Scriabin and Satie to the piano works of John Cage. He is also an experienced composer, and his many-sided experience of contemporary music has given him an especial insight into the work of a composer as unusual as Mompou. He records exclusively for ECM.

## About ECM New Series

ECM New Series was launched in 1984 to present contemporary composition and new interpretations of classic modern works. The series reflects producer Manfred Eicher's long-standing personal involvement with the contemporary music scene. The programming concentrates on works which share, in widely varying idioms, a concern for clarity and for the individual voice.

The opening of the series was marked by the release of Arvo Pärt's *Tabula Rasa* (ECM New Series 1275), which received widespread acclaim. Subsequent releases have included Gavin Bryars' *The Sinking of the Titanic* (1285), John Cage's *Early Piano Works* played by Herbert Henck (1291/92), the first recording of Igor Stravinsky's *Les Noces* in the composer's original version for four pianos and percussion (1299), and the acclaimed recording of Steve Reich's *Desert Music* (1304), among many others.

## Biographical Note on Federico Mompou

Federico Mompou was born in Barcelona on 16 April 1893, into a family of Catalan industrialists. His mother was a good amateur pianist, and his earliest musical experiences were gained at home. He studied at the Barcelona Conservatory, and in 1911 went to Paris to continue his studies at the Schola Cantorum with Fernand de La Tombelle, and also took private lessons from Isidor Philipp.

In Paris he came into contact with many of the leading composers of the day, including Fauré, who was particularly sympathetic to his work, and also Satie, whose aesthetic stance – his belief in simplicity, his rejection of Romantic rhetoric – he found deeply congenial. At the outbreak of World War I he returned to Barcelona, where he lived for the most part for the rest of his life.

His first published works, including *Scènes d'enfants* (1916-18) and *Cants màgics* (1920), quickly brought him to the attention of critics and public alike, and he was hailed as one of the most original young composers of the day. His music was characterised by its extreme harmonic refinement, its quietness, its dependence on repeated small motifs, and its devotion to small forms. He once described himself as "un artista intimista", an artist of intimacy.

Throughout his long life he wrote comparatively little, and often revised his works over many years. He completed the Cançons i danses between 1918 and 1972. The first book of *Música callada* was completed in 1919, the second in 1925, the third in 1951 (revised 1963), though the sequence was published complete in 1968.

Apart from his piano music, he wrote a few songs and a ballet. He received many honours in his old age, and died in Barcelona on 30 June 1987, just two months after his 94th birthday.

## Note on Música callada

In 1965, Mompou wrote:

"I think that music must not always be loud and declamatory. I have always believed in the virtue of quietness. In painting, what intimacy there can be in a small canvas! In music, what depth there can be in a phrase of few notes! I believe in the music which is heard rather with the soul than with the ear. I have always fled from the grandiloquent and the spectacular: the spectator of life, I have preferred rather than vast symphonies and dramatic works, the intimate expression in small forms, and the simple and songful line, and the harmony which is not loud but which acts on the spirit like a caress or a gentle breath."

The name Música callada – "Silent Music" – came to him, he said, when he was visiting the Montserrat monastery near Barcelona:

"One morning I was in the cloister, and I heard the silence, that marvellous silence in which you can perceive the most distant sounds and the birds singing. It was a morning of full sun, with a great silence. I felt that I was breathing peace and tranquillity. And almost involuntarily the title of Música callada came to me."

The music does not attempt to describe or depict this experience directly. It is the expression of a contemplative state of mind, which Mompou felt to be fundamentally religious in its essence. The whole of Música callada is imbued with this quiet religiosity, this sense of wonder in the presence of the natural world.

The Catalan titles are intensely evocative of the landscape and atmosphere of his native region. The titles suggest moods rather than pictures, states of feeling rather than scenes. Mompou was never a descriptive composer, in the sense that Albeniz or Granados were. His is an essentially internal music, and the titles serve to point the direction in which the music is going, rather than to describe an external scene.

The work as a whole consists of 28 pieces, as we have said. This recording presents the complete work for the first time on record.

## Note on the Performance

Herbert Henck writes:

"Mompou's music has always seemed to me to be a kind of miracle. It is so simple, so direct, so unassuming, and yet it has the power to transfigure the quietest moment. When I first discovered it, I was immediately struck by its extraordinary concentration – each piece is like a small, perfectly-formed gem, it contains a whole world in a few bars.

"To play this music requires a different kind of attention from playing the big Romantic works. You have to find the right weight of sound, the right balance between the voices, and above all you have to let the silence breathe between the notes. Mompou does not push the music forward, he lets it unfold naturally, like a flower opening. It is a music that asks for patience and sensitivity, and I think that if you give it that, it gives you back more than you put into it.

"I had the privilege of meeting Mompou not long before he died, and he heard some of this recording. He said that I had understood his music, that I had found the right tempo and the right quietness for it. That meant more to me than any other criticism I could have had. I hope that this recording will help people to rediscover this wonderful composer, who has so much to say to us in this noisy age of ours."
