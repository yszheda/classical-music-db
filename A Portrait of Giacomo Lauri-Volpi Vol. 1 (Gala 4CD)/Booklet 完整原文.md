## BIOGRAPHY

After a 1933 performance of *Il Trovatore* at La Scala, Milan, the great Italian bass-basso Ezio Pinza sent a telegram of congratulations to the leading tenor about his interpretation of Manrico: 'which is matchless, combines drama, passion, *fuocaggine* and *bel canto* and has the quality of Tamagno, Masini, Marconi and Stagno. Best wishes for many more years.'

**Giacomo Lauri-Volpi** was in fact blessed with a further *forty-six* years of his life which he spent in front of the public as one of the greatest and most enduring singers of his generation. Gala celebrates his artistry in this two-volume collection of recordings.

He was born **Giacomo Volpi** in 1892 to a large family in Lazio near Rome. When he was eleven he and his fourteen siblings were orphaned, but he was able to complete his schooling at the seminary in Albano. In 1914 he began studying law at the University of Rome and music at the city's Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia, but soon afterwards his studies were interrupted by conscription into the Italian army. During the First World War his bravery and leadership were rewarded with Italy's highest decoration for military valour.

After the war resumed his vocal training it was in a school of singing that had come to be considered old-fashioned. His teacher was the baritone Antonio Cotogni, then in his eighties, and the artists who most impressed him in his youth seem to have been opera performers like the baritones Battistini and de Luca and the tenor distinguished him from two other tenors who shared the name of Volpi.

An excellent amateur flute-player, Lauri-Volpi spread himself throughout Italy; he quickly established himself as one of the country's leading young singers. The death of Caruso in 1921 prompted much speculation as to who would assume his mantle, for no other tenor appeared to possess all the necessary qualities: Lauri-Volpi had the lyricism but not the vocal strength; Pertile had the intense musicality but not the beauty of tone; and Martinelli had the voice and everything except the same vocal warmth. The young Volpi demonstrated most of the qualities that had made Caruso great and added to them a powerful upper register that allowed him for roles beyond the range of his illustrious predecessor.

His technique would eventually enable him to perform roles that ranged from tenor leggero in *Il Conte di Siviglia* through tenor lirico (*Rodolfo*) and *drammatico* (*Raoul*, *Manrico*, *Alvaro* and *Calaf*) to *tenore robusto* (*Otello*) and spinto roles like *Cavaradossi* and *Canio*. In the early part of his career his repertory was restricted to lighter lyric roles rather than *verismo*. Later he added Puccini roles that enabled him to fulfil engagements throughout Europe. These included *Il Barbiere di Siviglia*, *Manon* and *Rigoletto* in Buenos Aires and *Rio de Janeiro*, *Bohème*, *La Favorita*, *Rigoletto* and *Tosca* in Madrid, *Bohème*, *Madama Butterfly* and *Tosca* in Monte Carlo, and then *Rigoletto*, *Manon* and *La Favorita* in Barcellona.

In 1923, barely two years after his Rome debut, he made his first appearance at the Metropolitan Opera in New York. Over the following ten years he gave more than three hundred performances with the company that included the US premieres of *Luisa Miller* with Rosa Ponselle and *Turandot* with Maria Jeritza. By the late 1920s he had extended his repertory from lyric to dramatic roles; indeed, he sang not only Calaf but also Rossini's Armido—he took part in La Scala's centenary celebration production of Guillaume Tell in 1929 that admitted that he was asked to rest his voice for many weeks afterwards. He also sang regularly across America in *Madama Butterfly*, *Andrea Chénier*, *L'Africaine*, *Aida*, *Carmen*, *Lucia di Lammermoor*, *Norma*, *Faust*, *La Gioconda* and *Pagliacci*.

Despite his many successes in the USA he remained uneasy with the cultural atmosphere of the country and always knew that his heart would be based in Europe. Interviewed by *Time magazine* in September 1928 while in San Francisco for performances of *Rigoletto*, he was reported as saying that "For the US, which has watched his progress, he has small use. Its people are not culturally old enough, he says, to appreciate music... For three years more, says Lauri-Volpi, he will come to the US to fulfill his contract. Then he will stay in Europe, where he is more popular."

This decision, shared by many visiting Italian singers of the time, may have been influenced by the effect of the Great Depression in reducing fees: from the mid-1930s very few of them remained in the USA (where the future of opera became largely dependent on non-Italian artists).

During this period he made his UK debut, singing *Andrea Chénier* at Covent Garden in 1925, to decidedly mixed notices. At the age of thirty-three his voice had developed a thrilling power, particularly in the upper notes, but his success in Europe and the USA was still not completely under his control. One London critic reported that "After the first act, during which he sang with volcanic power but out of tune and shrill on his high notes, the burst of applause was such that... the conductor gave up his attempts to get the orchestra going again. Thereupon, Lauri-Volpi, who had meanwhile crept behind a pillar at the back of the stage where he was getting hot, came smiling forth and flung his hands (and heart) at the gallery." Contrasting the burnished richness of the singer's chest register, the critic nevertheless concluded that "His voice as a whole 'may well become great if he can continue to discipline it.'"

There were also reservations about the opera chosen to introduce him to British audiences. The famous critic Ernest Newman questioned the purpose of bringing to London "certain artists who are not really first-rate in order to inflict on the public operas that they have not even 'heard of'."

Lauri-Volpi returned to Covent Garden for only one season, in 1936, appearing in performances of *Aida*, *Tosca* and *Otello*. Although it was reported by one critic that he sang Radames "with all his old ease and lovely quality but with a noticeable refinement of style", another questioned his apparent need "to take his breath so often in the wrong places and so to fiddle in the right ones." And yet it was this essential soundness of his vocal technique that enabled Lauri-Volpi to sing *Otello* and *Aida* at La Scala in February 1942, to much acclaim; the *Corriere della Sera* critic wrote that he sang with "beauty, power and sumptuousness of sounds". However, the deeply negative comment was provided by that of a colleague who sang with him in *Otello* and *Aida*: "he acted like an amateur of good voice, without style or school, without tradition and without art."

After the war he spent ten years touring Italy and the rest of Europe. In 1956 he sang his last Calaf, and three years later, at the age of sixty-seven, he made his last operatic appearance, performing Manrico in Rome. But he continued to sing in concert during his nineties, often in demanding operatic music. At the age of eighty he took part in a Barcelona concert of famous singers and made the Leoncavallo's *La donna è mobile* and the Verdi duet from *Madama Butterfly* with Montserrat Caballé and Plácido Domingo. He died in 1979, aged 87.

Lauri-Volpi's long recording career can be divided into three periods. His early acoustic 78 r.p.m. records were made for the Italian label Fonotipia and demonstrated an established *bel canto* technique, the voice sometimes darkly colored but with its ringing upper register firmly in place in the 1920s and 1930s, when his voice was at its peak, he also made a small number of recordings for RCA Victor. His later recordings for HMV, made during the 1940s, revealed a light lessening of vocal tone but captured for posterity his commanding interpretations of the heavier roles by then in his repertory, notably *Otello*.

Finally, towards the end of his stage career, he took part in LP recordings of several complete operas for Cetra and Italian Radio. Not surprisingly, with the passage of time the quality of his voice became worn, and some of its *squillo vibrancy* was lost, but its range and strength were remarkably long-lasting. His top C' continued to ring out with a brilliance and purity that a much younger tenor would be proud of, and what also remained were the lyricism (including a superb *legato*), verbal clarity and dramatic expressiveness of a true artist.

**ANDREW PALMER**
