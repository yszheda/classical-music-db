## Liner Notes by Paul Crossley

Toru Takemitsu's music for solo piano is one of the most distinctive, distinguished and best of the second half of the 20th century and consistently represents him at his finest. That is, already saying a lot for his is a quite extraordinary and original oeuvre, a music that, above all, breathes differently.

To invent himself in such a way that he proved unquestionably Japanese whilst, at the same time, taking his rightful place in the hierarchy of composers of the "Western" tradition could not have been easy: there were no models – he was the first. By doing this, not only did he liberate the first composers from Asia, let alone Japan, to pursue their own creative paths, but to us in the West many things about our tradition which we had, not possibly overlooked, or which we had assumed were not obvious. That Schoenberg by his own admission having once started out from Debussy, that, although self-taught, he considered Debussy his teacher, is not surprising. Debussy was, and remains, the composer who made the most radical break with the accepted Western tradition and he did this in a way very far from what he had learned from Asian culture. His 'feel' for the East was to find an eager disciple in Takemitsu's 'feel' for the West: a shared conviction that, ultimately, music lies beyond the realms of character, and should, wherever possible, be opposed to strong emotional and rhythmic accent; everything organised around qualities of sound patterns rather than melodic significance; an architectural schematic division and definitions created out of a weaving flux of sound; the world of dream, water, sea, garden, rain, and wind, of which Takemitsu so often spoke, which is there also in Debussy's titles – the unpredictable fluctuations of water, of rain, of garden shadows and light.

The earliest piece on this CD, discovered amongst his papers after his death, is Takemitsu's first, *Romance*, composed in 1948; in the following year, a very slightly revised version was taken to, and dedicated to, Yasuji Kiyose, the only composer from whom Takemitsu ever received guidance. The title 'Romance' once so familiar in the 19th century, is, Takemitsu suggests, was prepared from the 1948 and 1949 manuscripts. *Romance* is a highly significant addition to the Takemitsu canon in that it proves his innate genius was present in its best form very early, (I think, if Takemitsu had lived, he might have re-named it thus) suggested by its expressive instruction *Adagio sostenuto, nobile e funebre*. Three of Takemitsu's piano pieces are specific memorial tributes, but, in all his music, from the early sombre period (*Romance*, *Litany*, *Requiem* for strings), through his experimental period of the 60s, and even in the liberated sensuality and colour of his later music with its lingering walks through gardens of earthly delight, there is a prevailing tone of elegiac sadness, of dignified yet impassioned lament of the beauty of that must die and betray.

For the first performance of *Litany*, Takemitsu wrote: "When I heard that my good friend Michael Vyner had passed away, the composition used for my first public performance immediately came to mind. This piece entitled *Lento* in *Due Movimenti* was originally composed in 1950 when I was a young composer and not quite twenty years old. The feeling of death was quite close to me at that time. And this piece reflects emotional grief. Unfortunately, I have had numerous sad experiences with the deaths of musical loves but here I feel for the most part with only certain fragments remaining. Therefore, I decided to re-compose the piece from the fragments and also from my memory. I have tried to avoid adding any extra embellishments; it must be simple." Not that Takemitsu resists any added embellishments, he, in fact, did quite the opposite. The piece remains in outline but concentrated, refined, stripped down to the bare bones. (I had considered including the original *Lento* in two movements on this disc; its theme may have been written in 1950, but their disposition is very much that of a mature master.)

The obvious influence of Messiaen's *Préludes* in the second part of *Litany* (considerably less overt than in the 1950 score) is equally to the fore in the set of *Uninterrupted Rest No. 1* (1952). The titles are taken from a poem by Shuzo Takiguchi, but they take no more than a tiny figure of Takemitsu's formative years. The two movements that were added in 1959 bring the work to the period that saw the composition of *Piano Distance*. *Piano Distance* and its peculiar alchemy, there is no sense of stylistic incongruity. That piece and *Uninterrupted Rest No. 2* are, partially, experiments with a more radical 12-note language, and, partially, experiments with the use of silence, and intervals measured by the breath. The sonorities are approximately indicated, but ultimate rhythmic responsibility rests with the performer. This was an extraordinary step for an unestablished, not yet 20-year-old composer, a certain sign of not asserting a central authorising identity, of 'removing' the author; and *Piano Distance* was to be followed by two purely graphic works for piano(s) *Corona* and *Crossing*; forms of pure abstraction from which the personality has almost entirely departed.

It was to be another twelve years before Takemitsu wrote another piano solo piece, but, at that time, the piano was never out of his thoughts. From that period of intense experimentation and involvement with the mainstream of the post-war avant-garde, *Arc* and, particularly, *Asterism*, both for piano and orchestra, stand out for his originality. In them, he learned how to expand and elaborate the resources of his music into ever more fascinating, evocative and gripping patterns and shapes. This direction reached its apotheosis in *Rain Tree* for six percussionists – *gagaku* – "my impression of *gagaku* was that of a music that challenges measurable time."

*For away* is the first of many titles he was to draw from James Joyce's *Finnegan's Wake*. Titles were very important for Takemitsu. Unlike most composers who find their titles after the composition of the notes, Takemitsu used to say that once he had found his title, half the notes were written. He liked Joyce's use of words, in their usual connotations – words used to evoke resonance before lettering, if, at all, intoning, dreamlike language that approached the condition of music. I suspect that Takemitsu was Joyce's ideal reader, the inquiring savage carrying no baggage? And, in many ways, he approached Joyce's works as he did his own: if he was able to forge such a personal and original style. Starting out from nothing, he encountered an open horizon. When he first met Messiaen (I was with him in Princeton in 1969), Violin Concerto, Webern, some Boulez, Cage, gagaku, whatever – never centre courses, nor historians, and certainly never any traditions with their imperatives. (I remember saying to him in freshness of discovery when he told me that he had never had an idea for a melody that occurred to him after a chance first hearing of Mozart's Symphony No. 40 in G minor?!) For many of the pieces the inspiration was a haiku-like phrase. First conceived for *Prelude for Piano* but it turns its of there is nothing picturesque, nothing folkloric, nothing "exotic" unless it be that all his musical encounters were in some way "exotic". It is incorporated and absorbed with seemingly effortless naturalness, never to be paraded, or exploited; but the result of his patient, his multiple grace notes challenging the otherwise precise rhythmic scoring to provide an extraordinary full texture in the most delicate way, as indeed is true for so much of his music. In the last part he is able to show that it was quickly followed by, perhaps, the masterpiece *Les yeux clos*. (These two, together with *Les yeux clos II*, are, surely, his greatest piano pieces.)

The title *Les yeux clos* is borrowed from a series of works, in lithograph and in colour, by the French artist Odilon Redon, beautiful images of ineffable and mysterious expressivity. The piece – "in memory of Toru Takemitsu" – is profoundly his, is profoundly his, is perfectly formed: the *yeux clos* is to produce subtle changes of colour and the time as floating – the course of a whole life that is no more. For Takemitsu, a synesthete, sound was colour; but in the early 60s, to which he had been a part, had become, for him, too intellectualised, too de-personalised, especially too lacking in the "poetic" poetic language which he had made his. A particular and very restricted vocabulary of the "music of the future" was becoming a "time" as Messiaen had achieved. To do that he added the ferment of colour that he had exploited increasingly in orchestral works like *Green* and *A Flock Descends into the Pentagonal Garden*, and there can never have been a composer who enjoyed the sensuous world of sound with more relish and gusto than Takemitsu. This is, however, not indulgent; he was a fastidious perfectionist, used to suggest, to body forth, a timeless immanence. Interestingly, and significantly, his original thought for the title of *Les yeux clos II* was *Prayer Bell*; but, as he told Oliver Knussen: "Prayer Bell maybe rather too religious". Nevertheless, the suggestion is of bells, and, as a composer who considered all music a form of prayer, and in both *Les yeux clos* with their endlessly rising simple melodies and there is a marked quality of urgent and impassioned imploration.

*Rain Tree Sketch* merits another digression into the world of Takemitsu's titles. It is generally assumed that the *Rain Tree* was taken from the short stories of that name by fellow Japanese writer Kenzaburo Oe, a charming and suggestive poetic affinity that Takemitsu was perfectly happy to endorse. However, the original stemmed from the first encounter with the poet whose name is of American sharing crown and that he always read the words as Rain Tree so that the works of art could also be part of both his Rain Tree series of pieces and so his *Les trois essais de poèmes* was natural an allusion. The first essay to become *Rain Tree* was possibly because he uses some of the material of the piano part of his chamber orchestra piece *Rain Coming*, written in 1982. It is a pity that he did not continue the series to a third *Rain Tree Sketch*. As it is, his first, short piece in memory of Messiaen. Compositionally both works explore in particular the upper registers of the piano. In a television interview Takemitsu once declared: "My music is bodies – I only have the top – that's because I'm Japanese."

When this recording was first mooted, during Takemitsu's lifetime, he, unsolicited, said that he intended to write me a new piece especially for it. Sadly, that was not to be, but, in the event, I felt I wanted to return the compliment. In 1990, he wrote a two-movement orchestral piece for the Chicago Symphony Orchestra under the title *Visions*. The second movement was an orchestral version of *Les yeux clos*. Takemitsu fiercely maintained that it was not a transcription of the piano piece, but that, as with Redon who made various versions of his *Les yeux clos* in black and white and in colour, it stood as a new composition. By the same token, I have taken the first movement of *Visions*, called *Mystère*, and made a piano solo version of it. In particular, I have "played" the resonances in ways that I have learned from long study of other pieces by Takemitsu; I have called it *A Vision of Takemitsu*.

A final brief note about the two *Piano Pieces for Children*: they were written for an NHK Education Television programme *Piano Lessons* broadcast from October 1979 to March 1980. Their simple sophistication and utter lack of condescension make one wish that he had lived to write more, to complete a little book of them as, apparently, had been his intention.
