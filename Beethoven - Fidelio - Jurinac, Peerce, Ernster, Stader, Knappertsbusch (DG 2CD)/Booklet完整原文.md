## Background

of the opera is based on an actual event that took place in a prison during the French Revolution. A contemporary witness, Jean-Nicolas Bouilly, used it as the basis for a French libretto, which shifted the setting to Spain 'in order to appease the censors'.

In 1804 and 1805 Beethoven set a German version of the text, translated and adapted by Joseph von Sonnleithner. The premiere on 20 November 1805 at the Theater an der Wien could hardly have been less auspicious: only a week earlier Napoleon had occupied Vienna. The audience in the half-empty auditorium was almost entirely made up of French soldiers, whose applause was guarded at best. However, Beethoven took the fiasco as an opportunity to rework the score thoroughly. On 29 March 1826 he had a new version performed, but soon thereafter withdrew the work again. A third version followed, but not before Georg Fredrich Treitschke had completely revised the libretto. It was given on 23 May 1814 in the Kärntnertortheater and proved to be an unprecedented success.

Beethoven had feared that his opera would be appreciated properly only by posterity. Now, however, he could claim with satisfaction to his admirers in a newspaper notice 'that the present finds kindred souls and sympathetic hearts for that which is great and beautiful...'

## Hans Knappertsbusch

Hans Knappertsbusch (1888-1965) was the son of an industrialist. His career as a conductor was decisively shaped by the time he spent working as an assistant at the Bayreuth Festival during 1909-12 and by encounters with Hans Richter. At just 24 he was appointed opera director in his home town of Elberfeld. Engagements in Leipzig and Dessau then led to his appointment as Bruno Walter's successor at the Bavarian State Opera in 1922. However, Knappertsbusch was driven out by the Nazis in 1936 and spent the following years in self-imposed exile at the Vienna State Opera and conducting the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra. He regarded his years at Bayreuth, following the festival's reopening in 1951, as the high point of his career, but he also achieved international recognition in the concert hall, where he sought to hide his morbid sensitivity behind a ribald tongue. Knappertsbusch conducted with minimal wrist movements and could sustain the broadest tempi thanks to his ability to build long-breathed, arching paragraphs. Orchestras liked him for his short rehearsals. Rather than working everything out down to the very last detail, he preferred to place his trust in the inspiration of the moment and to rely on a musicianship born of impulse, to risk all in preserving the freshness and spontaneity of the truly great conductor.

*Uwe Kraemer*
Translation: Stewart Spencer

## Synopsis

Don Pizarro, the tyrannical governor of a prison near Seville, has secretly incarcerated a number of political prisoners. One of them is the freedom-fighter Florestan, who had sought to expose Pizarro's crimes. Florestan's wife Leonore, in an attempt to secure his release, has disguised herself as a man and, under the name of Fidelio, gained employment as assistant to the governor's jailer, Rocco.

## OPERA AS CONFESSION

In trying to fathom the thoughts and emotions of composers from the past, it is tempting to use the texts of their vocal works as a point of reference. Although this procedure not infrequently has led to misinterpretations, in the case of Beethoven's *Fidelio* it is fully justified. The composer himself declared that he could set a libretto to music only if he was entirely in sympathy with its message. As a consequence of those high aspirations, he planned over 50 opera projects in the course of his life but realized only one.

The story of the heroic Leonore, who, disguised as a man, rescues her husband from the dungeon where he has been imprisoned by a despot, corresponds in two respects with Beethoven's own ideals. It reflects his Enlightenment, republican views, inspired most notably by the French Revolution and his reading of Schiller and Kant, and it embodies in the character of Leonore his model of a self-sacrificing, unconditionally loving wife. The desire for such a partner occupied him for many years, as is indicated, for example, by a personal remark dating from the time of *Fidelio*'s genesis: "O God – let me at least find her – the one – who will support me in virtue." But it is not only statements like this that confirms Beethoven's identification with the libretto of *Fidelio*, it is also the music itself, which does much more than simply illustrate the text. It reinforces its message by means of an often declamatory style that deepens the sound and rhythm of the words. There is hardly another opera of the time in which libretto and music are so seamlessly integrated.
