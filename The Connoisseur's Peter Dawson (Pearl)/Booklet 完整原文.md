**The Connoisseur's PETER DAWSON**

Our admiration for Peter Dawson is almost boundless. [Witness the releases on GEMM CD 9336, 9384 & PAST CD 7007, on the last of which are nearly all of his most popular recordings, in marvellous sound.] This disc brings together the first volume of what might be described as 'Desert Island' selections for lovers of technical vocal virtuosity allied with sensitive musicianship and a vastly human *verve*. His range was amazing, he could act, and his enunciation remains a miracle never surpassed. He was a baritone and a very great singer.

## 内页说明

### Peter Dawson 生平介绍 by Tony Watts

Although there is now a thriving operatic scene in the United Kingdom with many first-class regional companies making interesting, thought-provoking productions, this wasn't always so. Different between two wars. In those days activity was almost exclusively centred on London, with the short star-studded 'international' seasons at Covent Garden and with English language performances at Sadler's Wells, the Royal Opera House being a shell that housed various attempts to set up a British National Opera Company. In other words stage work for singers was nowhere near as plentiful as it is today. Many artists earned their daily bread by travelling up and down the country singing performances of encores such as *The Merry Widow* and *Lily of Killarney*. While wandering minstrels often did find life artistically rewarding, it was rarely so financially. It was probably this potential lack of opportunity which led a singer as well-schooled and possessed of as magnificent a natural instrument as Peter Dawson to seek fame and fortune in Britain's variety theatres rather than in the opera house or on the concert platform. Today he would, no doubt, be an enormous international star, as perhaps, by Ben Tevli. As opera was, apart from occasional recordings of lieder and popular operatic airs, Dawson made his name as a singer of the sort of rousing popular ballads so popular at the time – music hall dances here, that will surely, less often heard these days as instances of singing in Scala or The Met, as he surely could have, the bass-baritone spent most part of a long career in the halls, wearing false beard, kilt and Tam O'Shanter, singing Harry Lauder songs under the name of Hamish MacCunn, then appearing under his own name in the same variety theatres alongside comics, acrobats, magicians and performing animals. There were, no doubt, times when Dawson's irrepressible sense of humour was tested to the limit and it is reported that Lauder never forgave him for the loss of income that the impersonation caused him. Once established as a music hall singer, Dawson graduated to the more congenial surroundings of the concert platform and made as many appearances on radio as a young man. But pragmatically speaking, it was too much work for too little money. He would have thought differently had he been offered Covent Garden with its large fees.

The most immediately appealing aspect of Peter Dawson's singing was a rock-like technical accuracy over a considerable range, with extensions from low F to high G or A flat. The voice was evenly produced over a wide range of notes by a well-trained Baritone. Dawson was also a vivid vocal actor, as is shown by a splendidly characterized account of *The Diver*, in which the final imprecation against God and eternity is whispered with chilling effect through clenched teeth. If less grotesque than Chaliapin's famous rendering of Boris, *Song of the Flea* is infinitely more faithful to the score while the rhythmically treacherous *Don Juan's Serenade* is performed with considerable flair and commendable accuracy. Naturally the ballads are sung as written without interpolations. *The Blind Deceiver*, heard here in a fascinating version, accompanied by the composer herself, is also the familiar orchestral recording. The sleeve note gives us even a portrait of an artist whose versatility was considerably greater than his rather bland public reputation might lead you to believe.

On a personal level Peter Dawson was such an inveterate practical joker that it was sometimes difficult to reckon with colleagues who took the unfolding victories of the outrageous pranks. This aside he seems to have been a most congenial companion, popular with all who knew him. Dawson's character was an Australian passion for sport, football and cricket especially. This endearment end he had excelled in both pursuits while a youngster. He was also enormously fond of a few pints of beer, particularly in mid-recording sessions when, he argued, it helped to keep the voice well lubricated. Extraordinarily Dawson was also a smoker, favouring cigars. Obviously the personal habit had no detrimental effect on his vocal chords or breath control.

Born on 31 January 1882 in Adelaide, South Australia to Scottish immigrant parents, Peter Dawson was originally destined not to work in the successful family heavy industry business, but despite bitter opposition from his parents, was determined to make a career in music. He took early lessons from a local teacher. Winning a local singing competition only served to fuel the fires of his ambition even further. When he finally decided for England to study singing, Dawson was fortunate to have the great British baritone of the Victorian era Sir Charles Santley teaching him. Santley was a hard task master, but instilled into his young charge the eternal verities of correct vocal production which were to serve Dawson so well for more than half-a-century. Santley was so convinced of the young man's potential that he arranged a tour of the West of England for him in which Dawson appeared together with Enrico Tietjens. Appropriately enough his first record, recorded at a private session at the Guildhall, Plymouth. A First London engagement followed in 1904 when the baritone was still only twenty-two years old. With this initial success, it was not long before he was contracted to make his first records for the Edison Bell label under the name of Leonard Dawson. It was an odd name.

To My First Love. In the early years he made hundreds of records for various labels, using a names other than his own. Among some twenty or so other aliases were Geoffrey Baxter, Charles Brander, Frank Goodwin, Will Strong, Frank Danby, Peter Allison, Denton Thomas, Charles Webber, Clifford Mundy, Arnold Flint and one J.P. McGill who has earned a place in musical history by setting Rudyard Kipling's *Boat's* to music. Hector Grant was plainly in good company! One normally reliable reference book even notes that Dawson used the name Allison Miller for recording purposes, but this dramatic change from bass-baritone to the upper reaches of the treble on the record in question, has been stretching even his noted adaptability rather too far.

Towards the end of 1909 Dawson began a fifty-two year association with the Gramophone Company and Typewriter Company. The first disc to be released was an old post-raid to the Edison Bell label which Dawson received the sum of $1.00. It has been suggested that during this long, long period he made more than three thousand records, which is a total in excess of *twenty-five million* copies, roughly 80% of which were recorded using the old acoustic method of recording onto a wax disc, by the electrical process on shellac 78 rpm discs, then later on mono to be eventually transferred to stereo test recordings when in his mid-seventies. In addition to operatic arias and rousing ballads, Dawson also sang in a number of complete recordings of the Gilbert and Sullivan operas alongside members of the D'Oyly Carte Opera Company.

With the outbreak of The First World War Peter Dawson attempted what was a rapidly increasing necessity. He volunteered for the Australian forces — in which he served with distinction. Upon returning to Australia he was declared fit resuming work and most of Dawson's many commercial recordings were made during this period, just as his vocal powers were reaching their peak.

Although Peter Dawson was conscripted into the Australian Army, he was rejected for active service in the Second World War, an intense patriotism inspired Dawson to do something to help the cause. So, even though through he decided to return to Australia where he worked as a warden he was joined by his family. Paradoxically, though this desire to play a part in the war effort, his recording industry finally caught up with him and once again he found himself in a position of having to make a choice. By 1945 Dawson was approaching his mid-sixties. At an age when most singers are either suffering serious vocal decline and living off their reputations, or have already faded, he returned to the exhausting round of concert duties, radio appearances and recording sessions with all the zest of a man thirty years younger. Miraculously he kept his sturdy vocal resources; it seemed to be an almost totally undimmed joy for the passage of time.

It was not until the early fifties that Peter Dawson finally bowed to the inevitable and began to wind down his activities. He announced his retirement in 1956 with the intention of moving back to his native Australia at least to enjoy a better climate. While he did return home, it was not until 1958 that he eventually gave up singing. One of the last engagements was recording a concert for the country's major broadcasting organization. Sadly Dawson was granted only three brief years of comparative quietude before his death in September 1961.

Peter Dawson may not have had the sort of glamorous career sought by exoths such as Caruso or Alexander Kipnis, but the singer's vast recorded legacy continues to be re-issued, proving that a good song, well-sung, never loses its appeal. And connoisseurs of the finest singing hold him in the highest possible regard as an artist and as a man.

**TONY WATTS**

### Producer's Note

One of this writer's earliest musical memories is of listening to Peter Dawson's recording of 'Song of the Flea' – and demand as three year olds tend to do, endless repeats. This of course necessitated my father having to constantly re-sharpen the thorn needle. Eventually the record was changed to 'Now your Days of Philandering are Over' (Obviously I didn't understand what it was all about!) but what a wonderful tune and to sing with such verve and life. From then onwards I was a Dawson fan and that has continued ever since. Despite now having a fairly extensive vocal record library, I still treasure the Peter Dawson whether for his 'serious' recordings such as those of Mozart or for his lighter repertoire.

His ability to turn material which could verge on the mawkish or jingoistic into pure gold becomes very obvious when one hears versions sung by other singers.

With really fine songs such as those by Mussorgsky or Tchaikovsky he produces records which are without peer. Indeed I would place his song of Volga Boat alongside that of Chaliapin, recorded in Russian; and the Tchaikovsky Don Juan's Serenade is the best version I have ever heard.

His most famous song recording, The Horn of Hunter, has almost become a trademark and for this CD I have used his first electrically recorded version (the later 1934 recording with orchestra can be heard on PAST CD 7007). It has the benefit of a piano accompaniment by the great Gerald Moore. Incidentally, for When the Sergeant Major's on Parade I have used the earlier version as the latter is slightly to be preferred for the orchestral balance.
