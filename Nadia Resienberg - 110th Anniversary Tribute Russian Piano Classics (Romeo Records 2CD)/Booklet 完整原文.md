## Recollections by Robert Sherman

### Page 4-5

I will always be grateful to the renowned pianist Earl Wild and his long-time partner Michael Davis for being the first to reissue any of Nadia Reisenberg's RCA. As my mother's supervisor on her Ivory Classics CD label, her crystalline playing of Haydn Sonatas appeared in 1998, and the delectable 2-CD of Russian pieces issued to mark her 2004 centennial, together reintroduced Mother to a whole new generation of listeners, and sparked a renewed and growing coterie of admirers.

"Thirty years after her death," wrote Laurence Vittes in the January 2014 issue of Gramophone, "Nadia Reisenberg continues to exert a profound influence through (reissues of) her major recordings for Westminster." Another recent convert is Peter J. Rabinowitz, whose Fanfare Magazine article earlier this year began: "Every so often, I'm asked to review an artist whose work is so good that it makes me jealous, and that's how I feel about Nadia Reisenberg: her playing was so overwhelming, both intellectually and emotionally that I found myself mesmerized by her playing."

Following the Ivory CDs came a quarter of superb albums on the Bridge label – the four-hand Mozart Sonatas with Mother's deeply admired colleague Artur Balsam; "A Child is Born" (which might well be the best-seller of the lot), following Anthony Tommasini's glowing review in The New York Times; her 1947 Carnegie Hall recital, and "Clara Rockmore's Lost Theremin" featuring the most complete Nadia Reisenberg accompanist side ever issued.

Three more masterful albums followed on Romeo Records, taken from radio broadcasts and recitals – "Music In And On the Air" with Clara Rockmore; Mother's 1977 concert at the 92nd Street Y with violinist Erick Friedman, and her brilliant showcase evening in 1980 with the Juilliard String Quartet.

Needless to say, much has happened to the record business over the last couple of decades, and when it became apparent that the Ivory CDs were basically out-of-print, with no plans for another pressing, I asked Michael Davis to allow me to put forth these historic performances on a different label. His permission being freely and graciously granted, I hope gradually to bring back the Haydn, and am thrilled now to make available again these glorious Russian miniatures, many of them still rarely encountered.

A nice sidelight: as I mentioned earlier, Ivory originally released this 2-CD set to honor Mother's 2004 centennial; this completely re-mastered edition is offered to mark her 110th anniversary year.

A few words about Mother's Russian connections. She was born in Vilna (now Vilnius, Lithuania), the astonishing Jewish center of learning and culture that was long known as Jerusalem of the North. (As her semi-official paper, she was of all proportion to its size. Among other soon-to-be legendary native sons of Vilna were Jascha Heifetz, Alexander Schneider and Leopold Godowsky.

In 1915, my grandfather, Aaron Ossipovitch Reisenberg, printed up a card to announce that two of his daughters, 5-year old Clara and 11-year old Nadia, had both passed "remarkably well" at their entrance examinations at the St. Petersburg Conservatory. Clara, the youngest child ever accepted there, studied violin with Leopold Auer (some 25 years later, using her married name, Clara Rockmore, she would become the preeminent virtuoso of the theremin); Nadia, meanwhile, entered "the highest course, with honors" and began working with Leonid Nikolaiiev.

"When I was called to audition in the study of the director of the Conservatory, Alexander Glazunov," Mother recalled nearly sixty years later, "I also found there Professor Isabella Vengerova (soon to be the instructor of Leonard Bernstein) and Glazunov leaned over to Isabella and said 'Well, what do you say, shall we give her the 5-plus (the highest grade)? She doesn't need any other exams.'"

### Page 6-7

"Almost everything I know about the physical side of piano-playing, the real Russian schooling with economy of movement and the art of legato, I owe to Nikolaiev's extremely detailed training. He was an incredible pedagogue, not so much in musicianship, which I developed afterwards, but in terms of giving me the basic foundation which I have had all my years since. He made me aware that economy of motion is one of the most important things. If you watch me play, you don't see my hands flying up in the air, going here and there and all over the place. I don't look busy. I'm busy with other things, with listening, with music."

Came the Revolution, and the Reisenberg family left Russia, traveling first to Warsaw (where Nadia made her concerto debut at the Philharmonic with a young man marking his own first appearance as a conductor. His name: Artur Rodzinski). Then came a number of performing stops along the way, including London, Berlin, and finally, in 1921, New York. It's a long story, but the short version is that my father Isaac J. (Sasha) Sherman, an Odessa native then representing a Russian bank in New York, met the boat, helped the Reisensbergs to get settled, organized Mother's first private home recitals here, and married her, more or less in that order. Except for a short honeymoon trip, they never returned to what was by then the Soviet Union, but the Russian soul was very much part of our family life. Borscht and piroshki were diet staples. Russian was the language of first choice at home, and when the party occasions arose, Mother could play some pretty nifty gypsy songs.

Not surprisingly, Slavic music was always prominent in the Reisenberg repertory. Mother played the Rimsky-Korsakov Concerto at that Warsaw concert with Rodzinski, made her American debut in December of 1922 with Paderewski's "Polish Fantasy," and introduced two works by Glazunov at her first New York recital.

Later she gave the first American performances of works by Shostakovich, Miaskovsky and Stravinsky, played concertos of Kabalevsky, Prokofiev and Mischa Portnoff (another premiere) with the New York Philharmonic, and chose etudes of Scriabin, Stravinsky and Prokofiev to close what would be her last solo recital at Carnegie Hall in 1947 (available on Bridge #9304).

With a few notable exceptions (one of them a performance of Beethoven's "Emperor" Concerto with the Boston Symphony under Serge Koussevitzky), Mother thereafter devoted herself to teaching and chamber music performances, lots of Russian music, and her family. As for her semi-official papers, I remember a great Tchaikovsky Trio with Michel Piastro and Joseph Schuster, then concertmaster and first cellist of The New York Philharmonic, respectively; and two wonderful Rostropovich Violin Sonatas – one with William Kroll and Erick Friedman; glorious Rachmaninoff Cello Sonatas with Schuster, Leonard Rose and – at one of her last chamber concerts, in the spring of 1980 – the Juilliard Quartet's Joel Krosnick (available on Bridge 7293/4).

Although Mother made a few recordings in the 1940's (collectors will happily brandish her 78 rpm Columbia album of a Brahms Sonata with Benny Goodman), her first important solo discs date from the mid-1950s, when she began an LP series for Westminster that would eventually include the previously mentioned Haydn Sonatas and Variations. The very first composer on the Westminster list, though, was Tchaikovsky, followed in short order by a collection of Rachmaninoff pieces, and what I recall was the first recording outside of Russia of the Kabalevsky Preludes.

Fast forward to 1974, when the Musical Heritage Society was preparing a 6-LP "Tribute to Nadia Reisenberg" in double celebration of the 50th anniversary of her American debut and her 70th birthday. I sat together with her in our backyard and asked her how she felt about the performances in retrospect. "It's a little painful listening to them again," she admitted, "because I always feel that I could improve things, but basically I'm very satisfied. I've always aimed for simplicity in my conceptions. I hate doing anything 'original' for its own sake, and while I feel everything I play very deeply, the music always comes first. I have no right to meddle with a work. As a result, many of the basic values are still there. I might play certain things more simply now, or with greater refinement – after all, I'm a few years older! – but I would still try to get at the truth of the music, and that truth hasn't changed."

Did Mother have any specific comments about the works being reissued here? Only a few, just for the record, as it were:

"The Tchaikovsky pieces are charming, not all of the same value, but great fun. His folklike dances are marvelous, for instance just some – like the Etude – technically quite brilliant. Almost all are very simple in melodic content, yet lovely sounding all the same."

"Although I never try to imitate anybody, Rachmaninoff had a great nobility, a particularly deep quality on the piano. Since I'm so preoccupied with finding my own personality myself, I think I probably played these pieces with his touch in mind."

"I've long been fascinated by the Kabalevsky Preludes, and still use several of them as teaching pieces. They're not terribly profound, but they're imaginative, versatile, clever, and excellently written for the piano."

One added piece of information: I don't recall why they were omitted from the Ivory set, but for the first time on CD, this new collection includes Mother's four Rachmaninoff performances that closed the box: the Polka de W.R. Romance, Op. 10, #6. (His Nocturne, Melodie and Humoresque, Op. 10, #s 1, 4 and 5, are available on Romeo 7293/4).

OK, end of commercial. Enjoy the music! RS

### Page 8-9

On her 76th birthday, July 14th, 1980, Mother wrote a letter to her family that did not actually come to light until after her death, less than three years later. I will not try to edit the grammar or smooth out the syntax, because you might as well read the words with her lilting Russian accent in mind:

"My heart is overflowing with gratitude for every day of my life. As I have but a few years left (if at all), I try to look back and evaluate what my life was like and what I have achieved. I feel that I have little to be ashamed of and much that I can be proud of. I value above anything else love and work, both of which sustained me and made every day a valuable experience for which I am extremely grateful. I hope that I will be granted the biggest present of all – clarity of mind till the very end, and ability to feel, to retain my enthusiasm and love..."

Those last three years were difficult – Mother had to fight back from two heart attacks and a stroke, which impaired her speech and damaged the flexibility of her right hand. She went back to teaching, though, and practiced constantly, perhaps harder than ever, because she had to re-learn things that for decades she had taken for granted. Happily, her major request was honored: her clarity of mind did indeed remain to the end, and her enthusiasm, too. On the day she died – serenely, while taking a nap – she had earlier talked on the phone with friends, offered advice to a student, and, open on piano rack was a Liszt Etude that, to my knowledge, she had never played before. On the last day of her life, Mother was still learning.

~ Robert Sherman

**About the Cover Photo:**

One more word about the cover photo, which was not reproduced in any of the other Nadia Reisenberg LPs or CDs, and was lovingly restored by my son Steve from a tattered picture we found in a Russian album. Therein, actually, lies lots and lots of history, which if you'll indulge me, I'll relate as briefly as possible. In 1987, on a trip to the then Soviet Union, we went to visit a childhood friend of my late sisters. This friend of my sisters (who, alas, my brain, musty from recall her last name - was the most gracious host, plying us with all sorts of delicacies that I'm sure came close to breaking her family bank. Eventually, she brought out an old photo album brimming over with vintage Reisenbergiana, and assorted other Reisenberg memorabilia, including quite a few items we had never encountered before.

Ever conscious of family pride, Steve photographed some of the photographs in Vera's apartment. Once back in New York, though -- and ever conscious of his incredibly busy schedule -- he was then on regular call as Carnegie Hall's principal photographer) -- he forgot all about them. Fast forward 27 years to the preparation of this CD reissue of Russian music, and that Moscow night with Vera came to mind. It was during that time that the three scans of the Reisenberg sisters were painstakingly repaired the tears, stains and fold-lines, gradually bringing the photo back to what I suspect is a much clearer and brighter image than it was when it was first printed.

**Technical Note:**

This reissue has been newly remastered from the surviving session tapes that were the sources of the original sessions issued by Westminster in the mid 1950's.

The original recording sessions were done over a period of nearly two years in different venues and on different instruments. As a result, the sound varies from session to session, and unfortunately, there is some distortion in several of the Tchaikovsky and Rachmaninoff pieces. Through careful signal processing and equalization, most, but not all of the distortion has been minimized.

The balance of the Op. 10 Rachmaninoff works that were originally issued on LP can be found in – Romeo Records 7293/4: Nadia Reisenberg, Piano – Live in Concert: The Juilliard Theater, April 29, 1980.

Seth Winner

**Nadia Reisenberg's Letter to Family (July 14, 1980):**

*July 14th, 1980*

*To my entire family -- sisters, children, grandchildren, and a few selected very close friends who should not find it difficult to recognize themselves. Today I reached my 76th Birthday and my heart is overflowing with gratitude for every day of my life. As I have but a very few years left (if at all), I try to look back and evaluate what my life was like, and what I have achieved. I feel that I have little to be ashamed of, and much that I can be proud of. I value above anything else -- love and work, both of which sustained me and made every day a valuable experience for which I am extremely grateful. I hope that I will be granted the biggest present of all -- clarity of mind till the very end and ability to feel, to retain my enthusiasm and love and gratitude for a very happy life.*

*I love you all and wish every one of you to have a rich and rewarding life such as mine has been.*

*Nadia, Mom, Didi*
