## Pages 8-9: Piano Sonatas & Forgotten Melodies

Nikolai Medtner was born in Moscow on 5 January 1880. He was descended from Livonians – Germans long settled in the Baltic countries – though he considered himself a Russian.

In his lifetime a great deal was made of his Teutonic rather than Slavic heritage, as if his exceptional grasp of form, and conservative idiom thought to resemble that of Brahms, set him apart – in Western eyes – from the more exotic and colourful Russian composers. Nevertheless it is the Russians who have taken him to their hearts. Perhaps too much is made of his ancestry, after all, Chopin is not considered a Frenchman, nor Schnittke a German.

His father, Karl Medtner, who owned a lace factory in Moscow, greatly admired Goethe and Pushkin and instilled in his son a profound love for poetry, art and philosophy. Medtner's mother was a musician, and though Medtner chose music as a profession at a very early age, it was later remarked that few musicians ever frequented the Medtner home, which was famous rather as a centre for the discussion of philosophy and aesthetics.

After initial piano lessons with his mother, Medtner had the good fortune to study with Safonov at the Moscow Conservatoire. Safonov was a formidable teacher whose influence, handed down through two generations of pupils, can still be detected in the most recent of the Russian pianists. He gave Medtner a flawless technique and every assistance to embark on a virtuoso performing career. Medtner however chose to devote himself to composition, and under the guidance of Taneyev mastered the rules of harmony and counterpoint to an astonishing degree. The young composer-pianist graduated from the Conservatoire in 1900, and his name is still to be seen in gold on the great plaques of honour, together with those of Rachmaninov and Scriabin.

Medtner returned to the Conservatoire in 1909 as a professor. Some idea of his original, and by all accounts memorable, teaching may be found in the book compiled from his teaching notes, *Pianist and Composer's Everyday Work*. The recordings of the great pianist Vladimir Sofronitsky bear further witness to Medtner's teaching. Sofronitsky spoke of his studies with Medtner affectionately and with gratitude.

Medtner resigned from teaching after one year in order to devote his time fully to composition, and several of his large-scale works date from this time, such as the Sonata in G minor, Op. 22, and the huge Sonata in E minor 'Night Wind', dedicated to Rachmaninov. Returning to the Conservatoire in 1914 and resuming his professorship, Medtner continued to teach there throughout the First World War, the Revolution and its aftermath. His First Piano Concerto, the largest and greatest of his works written in Moscow, was composed during this period and was the last work premiered by its composer before he left Russia in 1921 for an uncertain life in the West.

Together with his wife, Anna, Medtner went first to Germany, then Paris, ending his travels in London in 1936. The contrast between the works of his Moscow period and the works of his exile is striking. The three cycles of *Forgotten Melodies* from 1918 to 1920 mark the point at which his music ceases to mirror the turbulent epoch. A new serenity is apparent, and in some cases a deliberate Russian flavour, perhaps nostalgic.

Medtner was now obliged to support himself and Anna by giving concerts in Western Europe and America. His training under Safonov stood him in good stead as a performer, and, but for his rigid insistence on putting composition first and a disdain for any compromise associated with a virtuoso career, he would have been widely celebrated as a pianist the equal of Rachmaninov and Lhévinne. His concerts still linger in the memory of musicians today, who speak of his glowing sound and strikingly integrated playing, giving the effect of a string quartet or ensemble in which the inner voices were clear, the rhythmic pulse infectious, and the bass notes and harmonies supported by masterly pedalling. These qualities can be heard in Medtner's recordings, but it is significant that the sound of his performances in the concert halls can be so readily recalled, after the passing of more than half a century.

Medtner returned to the Soviet Union for an extended concert tour in 1927, performing to a Russian public eager to hear the new works he had written in the West. His music continued to be published in Moscow during the 1920s, but at the start of the new decade the official attitude to the composer changed, and performances of his music were not encouraged. Eventually in the late 1950s his collected works were published in the Soviet Union, edited by Sofronitsky and Goldenweiser, and this led to a revival of interest in Medtner's music that continues in Russia to this day.

Hardship really hit at the outbreak of the Second World War, for his income from German publishers dried up, and ill health became an increasing problem. His devoted pupil, Edna Iles, gave him shelter in Warwickshire where he completed his Third Concerto, performing it at a 1943 Promenade Concert. When all seemed hopeless a miracle happened. In 1946 the Maharajah of Mysore, Sir Jaya Chamaraja Wadiyar, himself a pianist whose piano-playing was abruptly stopped by a wartime injury, founded a Medtner Society. It was through EMI to record all Medtner's works. Medtner was already in declining health but managed to record all his concertos plus numerous songs and shorter works before his death.

The composer died on 13 November 1951 and is buried in North London.

## Pages 10-11: Sonata in F minor, Op. 5

Several books and articles about Medtner appear to praise him extravagantly and make his achievements sound very complicated. Nowhere does anyone say that he was simply a master craftsman in music. His mastery of musical grammar is evident not only in adult works but, rather frighteningly, in his earliest published compositions – an old head on young shoulders.

You can take apart his first sonata, the Sonata, Op. 5, and discover how solidly and clearly it is put together, and do the same with any of the later ones and find the same wonderful craftsmanship. Could this be his appeal to those who find his music so companionable? His music may at first sound complicated, but that is the way of the world.

The Sonata, a four-movement work in F minor composed between the years 1896 and 1903, and thus begun when the composer was sixteen, is a strong contender for the title of Medtner's finest sonata. No allowance need be made for his youth. This is music of complete mastery over thought and design, and the composer's warmth and deep feelings are clearly and candidly expressed.

What makes it special, and similar in quality to the great 'Night Wind' Sonata of 1911, is the relative leanness of the piano writing, unencumbered with the intricacy of much of Medtner's other music.

**Sonata Triad, Op. 11**
Medtner grouped his second, third and fourth Sonatas together to form one work, the *Sonata Triad, Op. 11*. Each of these sonatas is in one movement, and they are linked by an elegiac dedication: *To the memory of Andrei Bratschishchi*. He was Medtner's brother-in-law, a gifted artist and friend of the composer whose suicide affected Medtner deeply.

Medtner's music concentrates on remembered joys rather than the anguish of losing a friend. Although the second sonata is entitled *Elegy* it is the briefest of the three.

The lyrical Sonata in A flat major is based on a theme of the utmost simplicity, like a gesture of farewell. From this Medtner creates an intricate sonata movement. The composer's own words from his book *The Muse and the Fashion* are fascinating:
>The theme is above all intuitive. It is required, not invented, and is the most simple part of the work. It unifies it and holds itself the clue to all the complexity and variety of the work. The theme is not only, and not always a melody. It is more than a melody; for, as Bach proved in his fugues and Beethoven in his symphonies, it is capable of turning the most complex formal construction into a continuous melody.

The D minor Sonata is the shortest and most concentrated and consequently the one in which Medtner's method of constructing a work is easiest to follow. The entire work is built on the interaction of two melodic cells. The first is heard at the outset in a halting rhythm, the second as a consoling melody in F major which is subsequently developed at length, changing character completely and combining effortlessly with the first in graceful counterpoint. After a passionate climax this melody brings the sonata to an unexpectedly vehement conclusion.

The final Sonata in C major is closely modelled on Schumann's *Toccata*. The lovely second theme in particular has won much admiration. The performance indication of the Sonata, *Moderato, con passione innocente*, could well be a description of the peculiarly haunting quality of all of Medtner's music.

**Sonata in G minor, Op. 22**
This Sonata was written in 1910, after Medtner had resigned from his post as professor at the Moscow Conservatoire in order to concentrate on composition.

The plan of the work is closely modelled on that of the Liszt Sonata. It is in one continuous movement which consists of an introduction and three thematically related sections.

The unifying kernel of the work is heard at the very outset. Its melodic and rhythmic cell frequently occurs, often cunningly disguised. It is used by Medtner as a means of giving unity to his large structure, very much in the manner of the famous rhythmic cell of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony. Apparently unrelated lyrical themes eventually reveal a connection to the basic cell, and it is interesting to note that the cell is always clearly present at the beginning of 'paragraphs' which Medtner separates by silences, making the form somewhat easier to follow.

The work begins darkly, and at first closely parallels the Liszt Sonata. A long and lyrical theme in B flat major, heard under a shimmering accompaniment, would seem to be the answer.

**Fairy Tale Sonata, Op. 25 No. 1**
The two sonatas published by Medtner as his Opus 25 may seem an improbable coupling: the longest and most taxing of his sonatas, placed alongside a sonatina-like work possibly intended for children. Apart from showing the range of the composer's imagination and his capacity to build musical structures that show the most careful craftsmanship, these two sonatas reveal a view of the world that is very Medtnerian: a pairing of something childlike with an abyss.

The *Sonate-Conte*, or Fairy Tale Sonata as it is known in English, is the only instance where Medtner combines his title 'Fairy Tale' with "Sonata", so it may be heard either as an elaborate three-part Fairy Tale or a Sonata even more vivid and programmatic than usual. Clearly the Sonata is going to tell a story, and unlike many of Medtner's 'Tales' which are often far more serious and adult in mood than the title might suggest, this one really does sound as if it deals with fairies, giants, witches and goblins – all those wonderful fluttering sounds in the first movement, the sudden changes of mood from lighthearted tenderness to anger: what is this story about?

A new character enters in the second movement. In contrast to the first, this is an enormously long melody in E flat, one of the loveliest that Medtner ever wrote. It stretches seamlessly onwards, accommodating a passionate climax and a wonderful passage of glistening figuration in the high reaches of the piano, then suddenly changes character and becomes menacing, leading straight into a spirited march in the very odd time of 5/2. Perhaps Medtner thought that if Tchaikovsky could succeed in writing the waltz from the *Pathétique Symphony* in 5/4 then he could achieve a march in a similarly 'wrong' metre. This march does not last very long. Previous themes from the Sonata now begin to reappear and combine, and in a very short while the Sonata vanishes in a puff of pianistic smoke.

**Sonata 'Night Wind', Op. 25 No. 2**
Now comes the Sonata in E minor, popularly known as the 'Night Wind' Sonata. This mighty work is dedicated to the supreme Russian pianist Rachmaninov, and bears a subtitle: *The whole piece in epic spirit*. Medtner prints a complete poem by Tyutchev as the motto of the Sonata:
>What are you wailing about, night wind, what are you
>lamenting so frantically? What does your
>strange voice, now muffled and plaintive, what
>do you speak of torment past comprehension, and
>the heart!
>Oh, do not sing these fearful songs about
>ancient, native chaos! How avidly the soul
>longs to burst out of the mortal breast and to
>merge with the unbounded... Oh, do not wake
>the sleeping tempests: beneath them chaos stirs.

The Sonata in E minor has the reputation of being a fearsomely difficult work of extraordinary length, exhausting to play and hear, but of magnificent quality and marvellous invention – the greatest of all Medtner's sonatas, and a challenge to any pianist. Although bar for bar the Sonata is actually far less difficult to play than many of Medtner's other works it deserves its forbidding reputation, for the relentless quality that is characteristic of Medtner's style is here overwhelming, and there is nowhere to rest or to pause.

The form is monumentally simple: two enormous Allegro movements prefaced, joined and finally crowned in a grand peroration by an Introduzione rich in thematic possibilities. The two Allegros mirror the two stanzas of Tyutchev's poem, and the material of the first Allegro is self-contained and in the main uses the same thematic material as before. It is written for the most part in remarkable 15/8, an inspired touch that allows Medtner to unleash tempests without any sense of a regular downbeat. The wind roars and rages to an even greater degree in the second Allegro, which is considerably looser in form than the first. It appears to break down completely in a cadenza that abruptly breaks the momentum that has been building up through the whole sonata. This sudden change of gear, and of key, is the only moment that could possibly be considered a pause in the relentless rushing of the night wind. It is a wonderful moment of stillness in the music; unfortunately it offers no rest to the pianist.

**Sonata reminiscenza, Op. 38 No. 1**
(from *Forgotten Melodies, Op. 38*)
The *Sonata reminiscenzo* is a single-movement work in A minor, and, though often performed independently, it forms the initial piece of the first cycle of *Forgotten Melodies*. Other pieces in the cycle have some thematic connection to it, particularly its long opening phrase. The phrase also returns to conclude both the opening section of the work and the entire Sonata.

Pushkin's poem 'Remembrance' may perhaps have been the basis of the Sonata.

Dances and Canzonas from *Forgotten Melodies, Op. 38*
The first cycle of *Forgotten Melodies* consists of the Sonata reminiscenza which is frequently performed, and the lesser-known Dances and Canzonas. Although some of these shorter pieces refer to the motto theme of the Sonata, they were all published separately and form a loose collection similar to Brahms's sets of piano pieces. The first private performance of these pieces, in Moscow in 1920, was

described by Medtner's friend, the Hungarian violinist Jarosy:
>After accompanying his songs in an unsurpassable manner, he played us his newest compositions – Country Dance! While death and devastation raged outside, while an empire collapsed into ruins and a new state was arising from the blood-fertilised soil, Medtner was writing pastorals and fairy tales!

These 'Forgotten Melodies' were the last works he composed before emigrating to Germany. Medtner's title, reminiscent of Liszt's *Valses oubliées*, indicates their nostalgic character.

The *Danza graziosa* in A major has a subtle ragtime lilt. After a troubled episode in a minor key, it ends abruptly with no more than a hint of the opening ragtime music.

The splendid *Danza festiva* in D major is a wonderful showpiece, and has been described by Gerald Abraham in his notes to Medtner's own 1936 recording as 'an impression of a village festival, probably inspired by some canvas of Teniers of whom Medtner is a profound admirer'. Medtner's dances are generally exhilarating and energetic, and this is one of his best. Some ears will hear it as a close relative to Shostakovich's *Prelude and Fugue in D flat major*.

The chromatic *Danza rustica* introduces a pronounced Slavic element into the melodic line and evokes the sound of bagpipes and primitive flutes.

The *Canzona serenata* resolves the rustic mood of the previous dance into an Italianate and passionate song without words. The motto theme of the *Sonata reminiscenza* frames the Canzona but is transposed from its original A minor to a darker F minor.

The *Danza silvestra* evokes the world of the great northern forests, and is close to the forest world of the young Mahler. Medtner uses the whole compass of the piano to suggest wind rushing through the treetops and the gloomy depths of the forest. Rhythmically complex, one hand maintains a strict rhythm completely at odds with the other – a favourite Medtner device.

Finally, the coda *Alla Reminiscenza* begins in a quietly flowing A major. It is the motto theme of the Canzona serenata and now in a major key. Although the hands play in different rhythms, the effect is wonderfully calming. The Coda swells in a radiant climax and the cycle ends in utter peace.

**Forgotten Melodies, Op. 39**
This cycle of *Forgotten Melodies* is very much

connected with the events of the recent past, and opens with a magnificently dark *Meditazione*. Both this and the subsequent *Romanza* share a wailing, deeply sorrowful melody that the Romanza, hardly lighter in mood than the previous piece, develops further.

Then comes a total contrast with *Primavera* in which spring and life itself rushes in. Just as the first two pieces are connected through sharing the same material, so are the Canzona matinata and the *Sonata tragica*. Medtner is said to have wished these two pieces to be always performed together.

Medtner said he wished them two to be always performed together, showing the 'morn of life' contrasting with the 'realities of life'. He recorded them both shortly before his death in 1936. The *Sonata tragica* is particularly brief and compact, with a monothematic scheme unique among Medtner's sonatas. It ends with a spectacular coda.

**Forgotten Melodies, Op. 40**
Subtitled *Tanzweisen* (Dance Tunes), this cycle consists of six dances in three pairs – a dance in flowing irregular metre followed by one in regular metre. The first of the three dances with unusual time signatures is *Danza col canto*, a combination of dance and song in 5/8 that effectively combines different tempi. The *Danza sinfonica* is a weighty symphonic scherzo in regular 3/4 time.

The cycle concludes with *Danza ditirambica*, dithyrambs, or overswenglichen Ehren- und Lobliedern, these are the extravagant festive praises of the last of Medtner's ceremonial dances. This cycle concludes with the *Danza ditirambica*, last of all Medtner's ceremonial dances, and brings the cycle to a bright, joyous recessional: surely Medtner's bright, joyous hymn of farewell to a vanished world.

**Sonata romantica, Op. 53 No. 1**
The *Sonata romantica* and the *Sonata minacciosa*, both published in 1932 as Medtner's Opus 53, are another pair of sonatas offering an opposing view of life, just as the Fairy Tale and 'Night Wind' Sonatas did some twenty years earlier. The *Sonata romantica* in B flat minor is, in the main, lyrical. Its four movements each have titles and are played without a break so that the work sounds like a long fairy tale, or, as Medtner doubtlessly intended, a romantic story.

The placid mood of the opening *Romanza* is violently changed by the *Scherzo*, one of Medtner's typically frenzied non-stop endurance tests. With the start of the *Meditazione*, a slow improvisation that leads into the *Finale*, Medtner begins to knit the threads of his themes together as the first movement is relaxed, and is energetically summed-up in the *Finale* where all the themes are examined minutely.

**Sonata minacciosa, Op. 53 No. 2**
This Sonata is a tremendous contrast to the mood, and even the musical language, of the *Sonata romantica*. When asked why he had chosen this title for the work, Medtner replied: *'Have I not been threatened by life?'* Medtner's 'threatening sonata' (most definitely not the 'false translation' sometimes encountered, 'stormy sonata') is a superb work of spectacular pianistic difficulty, whose ancestry in the piano repertoire Medtner does not trouble to conceal. Liszt's influence is there, can't you see?

**Sonate-Idylle, Op. 56**
A refreshing pause occurs in the *Pastorale* movement of Medtner's last piano sonata, the *Sonate-Idylle* of 1937. It is just a conventional cadence, followed by a rest, but its appearance is a shock. Medtner's usual practice is to write music that keeps all ten fingers busy all the time, and silences that allow the performer (and listener) to rest are rare.

What a lovely work this is! The key of G major inspired Medtner to write some of his most happy, carefree music; the first *Novelle*, *Daphnis and Chloe*, carried over to the Second Violin Sonata. Even in the 'Night Wind' Sonata there is a lovely, gentle breeze in the warmth of E minor.
