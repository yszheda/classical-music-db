## Inside Booklet

### Biography

Elena Gerhardt (1883-1961) probably did the most to sustain love of lieder in Britain and America through times of war and beyond. The German-born soprano and later mezzo-soprano singer entered Leipzig Conservatory when she was "discovered" and trained by its Austro-Hungarian director Arthur Nikisch (1855-1922), who was also the most celebrated conductor of his day.

She made her debut recital on her 20th birthday, and it will come as no surprise to learn that they were "romantically [sic] involved", according to Groves Dictionary¹, if not to the nth degree.

Having sung two roles in operas, as Mignon and Charlotte, she soon decided to specialise as a lieder performer and, in an international career that lasted until 1953, "captured her audiences by her refined interpretations and inspired singing".² She had a great success in London in 1906, and made her first tour of America in 1912 with Nikisch. In 1932 she married Fritz Kohl, a radio station. Along with others he was arrested by the power-flexing regime, but was acquitted. The couple left for England in 1934, and settled in London where she became widely known and loved for her warmth and depth of interpretation, also championing lieder by Brahms, Wolf and Richard Strauss. During the war she took part in the morale-raising concerts in the National Gallery arranged by Myra Hess. She made records for several labels, including HMV, C&T (Grammophone & Typewriter Companies), and American Columbia and Vocalion (a label now acquired by Dutton). As a devoted teacher in her later years, she influenced the next generation of singers, for example the young Gerald Moore of Kent Opera, and introduced promising pupils to her English acquaintance, the young David Gurlitt.

Many of these recordings of Schubert were timed around the centenary of the composer’s death in 1828. The accompaniments are by Paula Hegner, her "faithful friend" from Leipzig who accompanied most recital tours to Nikisch, but mostly by the Dutch pianist Coenraad Valentijn Bos (1875-1955), according to Gerald Moore the "doyen of accompanists", who had played in the first performance of Brahms's Four Serious Songs.

Gretchen am Spinnrade is the early masterpiece, where the abandoned girl remembers her lost love while at her spinning-wheel. The song "must be sung at enormous speed and slows with her feelings". The song must be sung to a toleration of the two great climaxes, otherwise there is a danger of vocal exhibition.³

In Das Rosenband the lover winds ribbons around the dozing beloved who wakes with a very satisfactory Sleeping Beauty response. This song has "immediacy and freshness ... one of the great glories of Elena Gerhardt's repertoire".²

This CD offers an opportunity to judge how well her recordings (although with the mannerisms of the period including portamento) reveal the much-loved artist and personality of the platform. J B Steane feels that Elena Gerhardt is now 'chief of the great unwonted' from the musical past. She was greatly admired for some for her warm personality, freedom of tempo, variety of shading and characterisations, for example 'the strong, often deliberately harsh, chest notes of her mature voice is learned as though sorrow and bitterness had etched themselves into the very timbre' (Shawe-Taylor⁴ on Der Musensohn). But another school held that she was hard, cold, placid and monotonous, albeit with a clean enunciation and musical vowels. When two forces of opinion collide in one generation there may not be much in common to carry forward to the next. Steane says 'A time must surely come when [the recordings] are heard again and newly acknowledged: and then it should be long before she is once more actually wanted'.

### Programme Notes

**Litanei auf das Fest Allerseelen** (with an added violin obbligato for the feast day of All Souls)
The day after All Saints, dedicated to all who did not win the seats (i.e. heavenly). Jacobi recalls the losses in life, the disappointed, the deserted, those who had only a short while alive, and prays for them. He prays that the departed be not parted from God forever, but that they may rest in a semmingly endless night. The postlude is a rare case which "by introducing a new musical idea, supplements the song, to expand it beyond its limits".

**Schlaflied** is set to words by Schubert's one-time house-sharer, is a rarity. The poem is odd, a boy child is entreated to the river and woods, and is healed. Thus quells cold, and day becomes dewy and full of shimmering colours. Nestling close to his mother he is lulled to sleep by the God of dreams. This discourse of Romantic personifications depicts the poet's deep love of nature.

**Die Forelle** is the ever-fresh evocation of the trout in the brook. (Schubert omitted two further verses by "neck-sense, worming girl, too, to behave to a tempter with a rod"). The devils catching of the fish is a "mock-serious, after which Schubert easily recaptures the lyrical mood of the opening".

Three Goethe (or ascribed) songs follow. After her first songs to the East Wind, the second, Suleikas zweiter Gesang, in addition to the first, carries my message to the distant beloved but, put another way, the tears cool, so not distress him but assure him of my love. "I show Schubert's power to evolve long, sustained phrases on melodic fragments". In Geheimes the lover hides her secret from her company. "Suleika's songs are the best-known of all". The first of Suleikas songs to her beloved, "Ach! Um deine feuchten Schwingen", with the accompanist (Gramophone & Typewriter taken too fast), which takes the Son of the Muses around the world, quickening life as the years, making even dull fools sturt and gauche girls dance to his melody. When will he be able to return to his own beloved? A fearful last question, for the pace must resume.

In Auf dem Wasser zu singen the soul is like a boat on the shimmering water, drawn to the sunset and then to the little stream running to the infinite. The piano writing is brilliant, but the mood is calm and the melody steady, and there is no great Piano Impromptu or D899.

Song from Die schöne Müllerin D795: Wohin? Is this second song of the first cycle from Wilhelm Müller? The brook itself is the protagonist, which draws the lad and his fellows. There are water-ripples below, and mill-wheels above where he can find employment and – maybe love.

**The Romance from Rosamunde** D797 (Vollmond strahl) is not a Schiller lied as such, but a text from his incidental music for the play by Helmine von Chézy which had only two performances, but praise for the music. The romance is pastoral; the moon and the maytime remind her of when the lover was far away, before he went to sea. The music is tender and yearning.

Fischerweise exhibits the life of a fisherman; he has been doing it since he was a boy, as his father taught him many fish is he fit and not entangled himself. The shepherdess on the bridge is angling for him, but he is not hooked.

In Frühling is a flowing song of Spring. "The poem gives us the serenity and joy of a spring morning rather than the sentimental laments of the poet".

**Eight Songs from Winterreise D911:**

Nowadays to perform only a selection from a complete song-cycle is frowned upon, yet it may be a relief, as we are lucky to have this singer's interpretation. She listened to the whole song-cycle herself, recorded excerpts without risking critical eyebrows. Winterreise is perhaps the only account in song of the extended experience of grief, and odds up much more than a series of settings. Yet each is complete in itself, and we are lucky to have this singer's interpretation. In listening it is easier to think of a man still as protagonist and endurer to his grief, though in his cold night, than try any gender realignments – much like a "breeches" role in opera. According to Elena Gerhardt: "If a woman has the strength and voice to interpret a man's words rightly, it will never sound ridiculous".

The songs are in order, and are a précis of the whole. The character is an "everyman, no playboy, perhaps someone who waited until ready to settle down and then fell in love – only to be devastated by the present outcome". In his first song, Gute Nacht, he steals himself to set off, with the good behaviour of the house he is leaving. In Der Lindenbaum (5), the tree at the edge of town was a favourite place in better days; and he is on his way; but this man finds it himself the courage to endure the blast of his feelings. Wasserflut (6), he is still in tearful grief, longing to return, as snow will melt to join the rushing streams. In an empty church-bonner's hut he dreams of his happy times (Frühlingsraum, 11), he is in an empty church-bonner's hut he dreams of his happy times, he tries to rouse himself to face the cold dawn where the only flowers are frost-patterns in the window. In Die Post (13) the lively sound of a post-horn sets up a sudden hope that he might have a message from her. The heart says it is possible, the head that he can never know where he was.

After this reminder of reality he finally renounces any thought of return, and continues travelling – in that dissociated and existential state of mind that follows first grief. He retries what he sees to be his feeling that death is all he can now expect. A carrion crow (Die Krähe, 15) seems to be following him, waiting for him to drop, and he has a bitter reflection that it at least shows constancy. Then, in Der Wegweiser (20), he realises just how much he has cut himself off from people, and that his travels are without purpose or – without rest. There is one signpost that will take him home, but he can never return. In the last song (Der Leiermann, 24) he comes across an old man playing a hurdy-gurdy (a hand-turned type of lyre, supported on a stick). He is outside the village, on the ice, not caring whether anyone hears. The traveller stares at him, the only person he has really noticed in all this time, and marvels at his detachment. Shall he travel along with him, singing his sad songs? Is it the end of grieving – or of sanity?

**Three songs from Schwanengesang D957.**

The set of disparate songs published after Schubert's death was called his "Swansong" by the publisher. They are pure Schubert, doing best what he did before, and a step-change to new heights in his Heine settings. Liebesbotschaft is a forthright invocation to the brook: take a message to my love, keep refreshed the flowers she wears, comfort her, and then lull her asleep with thoughts of my return.

**Das Fischermädchen** is a setting of Heine, a poet new to Schubert. He catches the saltiness of the poem, saying that she should not be afraid of the water because she ventures every day on the heaving waves.

**Abschied** is a thankful farewell to a town as he (maybe a student) rides off for ever. Goodbye to the town, the smoking chimneys, the setting sun, the lighted window. Even when the stars are veiled over he can say goodbye to them too.
