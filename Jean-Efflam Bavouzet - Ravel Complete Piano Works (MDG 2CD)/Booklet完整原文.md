# Maurice Ravel and the Piano

By Roger Nichols

Maurice Ravel (1875-1937) achieved in his piano music a perfect balance between the most refined harmonic and melodic invention and the most imaginative exploitation of the piano's resources. He came late to writing a substantial piano work – the first piece here, *Jeux d'eau*, dates from 1901, when he was already twenty-six – but when he did, he moved with confidence into a territory that had been opened up only a few years before by Debussy. Indeed, in some ways Ravel outdid Debussy in the art of creating floating, evanescent piano sonorities, as *Jeux d'eau* itself shows. Over the following thirty years he produced a stream of piano works that are among the glories of twentieth-century music, each one perfectly formed, each one exploring a different aspect of his creative personality.

### About the works on this recording

#### *Jeux d'eau* (1901)

*Jeux d'eau* ("Play of the Water") was Ravel's first important work for piano, and it already contains in essence everything that was to characterise his later piano writing: the subtle harmonic colouring, the limpid fingerwork, the pleasure in movement for its own sake. Ravel said of this piece that it "owes more than a little to the influence of Liszt and particularly to the Liszt of *Les jeux d'eau à la Villa d'Este*. The title, which I borrowed from an inscription by Henri de Régnier on a fountain in the Villa d'Este, will be enough to show that my musical intention has been to create a hydraulic movement, the play of water under the sun." The piece was a revelation when it first appeared, with its whole-tone harmonies and its delicate shifting sonorities that seemed to dissolve before the ear could grasp them. It has remained one of Ravel's most popular piano pieces ever since.

#### Sonatine (1903-05)

Ravel's Sonatine is a beautifully proportioned work in three short movements: *Modéré*, *Mouvement de menuet*, *Animé*. It harks back to the eighteenth century in its conciseness and formal clarity, but there is nothing pastiche about the harmonic language – it is completely individual, full of Ravel's characteristic modal inflections and subtle colouring. The opening movement is restrained and haunting, the minuet movement is graceful and understated, and the finale is brilliant and effective, with a wonderful sense of momentum.

#### Miroirs (1904-05)

*Miroirs* ("Mirrors") is a set of five pieces, each one dedicated to a member of Ravel's circle of artist friends, the Apaches:

1. *Noctuelles* ("Night Moths") – dedicated to Paul Sordes
2. *Oiseaux tristes* ("Sad Birds") – dedicated to Ricardo Viñes
3. *Une barque sur l'océan* ("A Boat on the Ocean") – dedicated to Alfred Cortot
4. *Alborada del gracioso* ("The Morning Song of a Jester") – dedicated to Michel-Dimitri Calvocoressi
5. *La vallée des cloches* ("The Valley of Bells") – dedicated to Maurice Delage

Each piece paints a different impressionistic picture, and the set as a whole shows Ravel at the height of his impressionistic powers. From the mysterious fluttering of *Noctuelles* to the broad, surging waves of *Une barque sur l'océan*, from the bright Spanish sparkle of *Alborada* to the haunting bell resonances of *La vallée des cloches*, this is Ravel impressionism at its most perfect.

#### Gaspard de la Nuit (1908)

*Gaspard de la Nuit* is a set of three pieces after poems by Aloysius Bertrand, and it is one of the most virtuosic and at the same time most poetic works in the piano repertoire. The three pieces are:

1. *Ondine* – the water-nymph who entices the listener with her song
2. *Le gibet* – the gallows with a body swinging in the wind
3. *Scarbo* – the evil goblin who haunts the night

The work pushes the piano to its absolute limits in terms of virtuosity, but every detail serves poetic ends – the shimmering of Ondine's song, the relentless, quiet tolling of the gallows, the dizzying acrobatics of Scarbo. It has remained one of the supreme test pieces for the virtuoso pianist, and one of the most poetic.

#### Valses nobles et sentimentales (1904)

Valses nobles et sentimentales is a set of eight waltzes, written in response to a competition where composers were asked to write a waltz anonymously and critics were invited to guess who the composer was. Ravel's entry was generally attributed to Debussy – which showed how forward-looking his writing was. The title makes clear the link to Schubert, whose late waltzes Ravel admired. Each waltz has its own character, from the wistful first to the brilliant concluding one, and the harmonic writing is typically subtle and refined. Ravel later orchestrated the set, but the original piano version has a clarity and intimacy that is unique.

### Jean-Efflam Bavouzet

Jean-Efflam Bavouzet was born in 1962 in Niort, France. He studied at the Paris Conservatoire with Pierre Sancan, Geneviève Joy-Dutilleux and Vlado Perlemuter. In 1986 he won the First Prize at the Concours Géza Anda in Zurich. He has since pursued an international career, appearing with all the major orchestras and at all the major festivals. He has a particular reputation for his fine insight into twentieth-century French music, and his earlier recordings have been greeted with enormous acclaim by the critics. This is the first volume of his complete recording of Ravel's piano works for MDG.
