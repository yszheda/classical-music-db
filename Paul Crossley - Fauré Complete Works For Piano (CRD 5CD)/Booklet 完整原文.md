## Liner Notes by Bryce Morrison

### The Nocturnes and Barcarolles

Fauré's greatest piano works, the Nocturnes and Barcarolles, both number a lucky thirteen. The Nocturnes span the whole of his creative life, the first dating from 1875 when he was 30, the last from 1921 when he was 76. Again, the appellation 'Nocturne' is neutral rather than evocative. The thirteen unfold as a kaleidoscopic and fascinating collection of piano sound, composed at various times, which mix the rigour of the Fugues with the allure of the élégiaque and the 8th Nocturne, and the strange power of the Adagiets. Nonetheless, it is also clear that these relatively short pieces, which encompass enormous weight of depth, ranging from the poised equilibrium of No. 4 to the great single arc of No. 13, far from being lines of one. 7 as the terse and enigmatic No. 9, from the unaccountably radiant flow of No. 3 to the inarticulateness of No. 10, from the serenity of No. 6 to the anguish of No. 12.

This emotional range is projected in an equivalent variety of plastic forms and through an ever-growing arsenal of compositional techniques. The first five Nocturnes belong to the first flowering of Fauré's genius as a piano composer. Though as suggested earlier, the piano writing owes less to Chopin than is generally supposed, the ABA, 'calm agitated calm' scheme is typically Chopinesque. Another quintessential Fauréan feature again derived from Chopin is that there are no introductions, but all-important codas toward which everything leads – summations, fulfilments, resolutions, explications.

Fauré wrote regretfully of the 'similarity' of his music: 'it seems that I repeat myself constantly and that I cannot find a noticeably different approach from that already expressed,' and yet listening to the 6th Nocturne which is separated from the 5th by 10 years one is aware of an enormous development. Apart from the much richer, subtler harmonies there is, already, a mixture of sensuous lyrical appeal and something approaching the sparer, more angular manner of the later works. Fauré's music is various, curiously enough, blocks of material which, however, seem to dovetail logically and inevitably. Though quite unanalysable, it remains one of Fauré's perfect works.

The 7th Nocturne is also multi-sectional but what is very striking is the way that each section, even the opening, starts from something unresolved. Although there are no long melodies, just evolving melodic cells, this is the longest, most 'developed' of the Nocturnes, surely because, while everything appears resolved in one of Fauré's most miraculous codas, which seems at the first time the difficulty of saying anything categorically.

For Paul Crossley, as, indeed, for all of us, who have loved and revered Fauré's music over the years, it is difficult to talk about the world of the late Nocturnes. In the case of one of them, No. 11, we are made aware of the influence of the brilliant young pianist and dedicatee, the memory of Noemi Lalo, wife of the critic Pierre Lalo. But, in general, their moods are very private and very elusive. They are not 'comfortable' pieces and the qualities which give them their strength and greatness at the same time explain why they are not often played or better known.

**CD 3 - The 13 Barcarolles:**
1. No. 1 in A minor, Op. 26 — 5'00"
2. No. 2 in G, Op. 41 — 7'19"
3. No. 3 in G flat, Op. 42 — 7'25"
4. No. 4 in A flat, Op. 44 — 4'10"
5. No. 5 in F sharp minor, Op. 66 — 7'35"
6. No. 6 in E flat, Op. 70 — 3'42"
7. No. 7 in D minor, Op. 90 — 3'22"
8. No. 8 in D flat, Op. 96 — 3'31"
9. No. 9 in A minor, Op. 101 — 4'42"
10. No. 10 in A minor, Op. 104 — 3'42"
11. No. 11 in G minor, Op. 105 — 4'50"
12. No. 12 in E flat, Op. 106 — 3'10"
13. No. 13 in C, Op. 116 — 3'58"

The Barcarolles are second only to the thirteen Nocturnes in offering a comprehensive survey of Fauré's art. Listening or playing through from opus 26 to 116 is to be made aware not only of one of music's most remarkable voyages: Gounod-inspired lyricism through the intricacies of No. 5 to the final offering, a work which somehow resolves all former complexity into one of the strangest and most unearthly transparency, an unforgettable farewell and valediction.

Even No. 1 with its conventional 6/8 Venetian lift is immediately coloured by dissonance, an enlivening of a superficially candysweet surface. As early as No. 2 and 3, with their chromatic profusion, we discover (the latter with ornaments which 'crown the theme like sea foam' according to Marguerite Long), everything that is distinctive in Fauré's piano rhythmic flux. No. 4, though less ambitious, modulates with sufficient novelty to delight the ear as well as sound it. No. 5 is, in many ways, the keystone of the set – passionate, rich and strange! In No. 5, Fauré, melody and rhythm (fragmented in the opening terse, fragmented phrases) become virtually interchangeable, and the central très empörté development commencing tranquillament but rising to a Lisztian fortissimo uproar (one of two climaxes) subsides in a coda like shifting sunset vapour, a close as astonishing as it is deeply assuaging. No. 6 is relatively carefree, weaving its way from one idea to another before No. 7 closes the door on such charm. Strange indeed both in sight and sound, its themes emerge wraithlike before being erased. In Nos. 8, 10 and 11, one again senses Fauré's musical style at its most recessive, his increasing compression often resulting in the most discordant juxtapositions. No. 9 turns the filling and romping Barcarolle rhythm to morbid advantage and, like No. 11 (most remarkable of all the Barcarolles) simultaneously suggests both a distant and persistent tolling of impetus. It is therefore with some relief that we reach No. 12, a great favourite of Fauré's ('I composed it licking it over like a true dowry her cuba'), its clarity serener and marine motion leading to the final and profoundly conciliatory Barcarolle.

### The 5 Impromptus

The 5 Impromptus (excluding No. 6, originally written for harp) are not only true to their title but traverse Fauré's creative life. No. 1, ornate and whimsical with its breathless flight of short and long passages and its central elaboration, prompted Cortot to speak of 'suint d'art' as a 'stylised coquetry and regret.' No. 2, a whirling ostinato, is less richly decorated, more resolutely transparent while No. 3 is among Fauré's most idyllic creations, its principle dolce dipping and soaring above a gyrating moto perpetuo, its central idea like 'a swathe of foam tumbling and dissolving.' The 4th Impromptu sees such liberation from, in Nos. 1 and 5 in sharp indeed. In No. 4 the sense of ideas allowed to blossom and expand, as if across some magical trillito, is subdued into an attenuated and feverish utterance. Dedicated to Marguerite Long (one of Fauré's few early champions), it seethes with an unrest lightly confined in No. 5 with its spin of whole-tone scales, its defiant refrain that tentatively break forth with the coda.

### Thème et Variations

For those who find the Fauréan evanescence of the Impromptus Nos. 4 and 5 unsettling, the 10th and 9th Variations are reassuring. It is not that their Schumannesque theme (modelled on the Études Symphoniques) is conventionally exploited, but that, for the greater part, modulatory paths are home-bound. The second of seven variations, while Variation 8 is way to an animated syncopation in Variation 2, extended to rapid alternations of duple and triple time in Variation 3; leisurely and lingering, it is allowed plenty of space, accelerating the pace of events, while Variation 5, despite its outwardly passive quavers, increases rather than diminishes such momentum. Variation 6 dramatically stills such momentum, proceeding in solemn, twice-decorated contrary motion, while Variation 8 is a modification of Variation 5 (a carrefour of wheels with wheels) and Variation 9, the modal and improvisatory central section memorably described by Cortot as 'still, lifted, and almost anguished, the high G sharp, the curve of the melody, the heart sinks down like a star in the evening.' Variation 10 is a glittering and ironic, rather than playful scherzo, with an explosive close. Variation 11 is an epilogue, concluding the Variations on a note of serene and optimistic benediction.

### CD 5 - Valses-Caprices, Romances, Ballade, Mazurka

**CD 5:**
1. Valse-Caprice No. 1 in A, Op. 30 — 5'00"
2. Valse-Caprice No. 2 in D flat, Op. 38 — 5'51"
3. Valse-Caprice No. 3 in G flat, Op. 59 — 8'22"
4. Valse-Caprice No. 4 in A flat, Op. 62 — 5'17"
5-7. 3 Romances sans paroles Op. 17 — 7'28"
8. Ballade in F sharp, Op. 19 — 11'04"
9. Mazurka in B flat, Op. 32 — 5'03"

The 4 Valses-Caprices allow Fauré to exploit all of his improvisatory cunning: to the full extent one described how Fauré and Messager would improvise on the opera then going in their sudden modulations and evasions of the tonic – a revealing comment). More 'upbeat' than 'waltz', all four are rapturous in their unabashed hedonism. Both works combine and extend the scintillating worlds of Chopin and Saint-Saëns waltzes, mocking a left-footed three-in-a-bar before whirling us away in some of music's most aerial virtuosity. Three-times-waltzing world, the ballabile in No. 3 is one of Fauré's most characteristic and serene second, developing such jocularity into music of ever-increasing refinement in Nos. 3 and 4. In Valse-Caprice No. 4, an episode of almost erotic intimacy. Here, then, is lightness away from the basic G flat tonality, a premonition of the central episode where the principle subject and an added bell-like motif are joined and sent soaring skywards into a rarefied stratospheric calm, an episode of ravishing, almost erotic intimacy.

The 3 Romances sans paroles (1863), Fauré's first published piano compositions, form an affectionate and very Gallic tribute to Mendelssohn's urbanity, agitation and ease.

It was Liszt who found the Ballade 'too difficult' referring to the version for solo piano (the orchestration in a more popular and lucid form took place seven years later). Presumably he meant that the writing was intricate without being virtuosic, that the material was too fragile and exquisite for public consumption. Even Liszt, a dazzlingly perceptive and generous critic, must have baffled by the presence of so many difficulties in a piece unlikely to win prolonged applause. Meanwhile Debussy's dismissal of the Ballade as 'an étude as a woman's loose shoulder-strap' says more about his own insecurity than conscious of present neglect.

### Fauré's Style and Legacy

Fauré's neglect remains one of music's most elusive mysteries. Yet his puzzling if fruitful ambiguity is understandable. Celebrated, as Paul Crossley tells us, for misleading because superficial attributes, ('surface charm' and 'mellow autumn' in his earlier and later works, respectively), his musical language is at once radical and conservative. It also shows the way, more than any one else of his period, not only earlier, which 'evades a uniform discipline': in Fauré's case an impeccable if often complex marriage of heart and mind. Even in his earliest Fauréian music one is aware of a gift for melody 'comparable only to Mozart', with a feline ease that can baffle even the most acute pianist leaving both him and his listeners momentarily blinking and stranded. Yet for a subtle a composer Fauré could be with his superposed chords of the piano – Nocturne, Barcarolle, Prélude, Impromptu etc., suggest a Chopinesque bias, even when key influences come from elsewhere. 'From the beginning', wrote Jean-Michel Nectoux, 'his imagination is nourished by Mozart, by whom his development sections abound and those codas in which whole movements are resolved but partially illuminated. But Fauré is above and beyond all a musician of transformation, allowing his music to tell its own tale and leaving his listeners to pick up their own clues regarding attribution or evocation. The Nocturnes and Barcarolles, so central to his keyboard output build bridges between the classical and modern eras. Indeed, it is his harmonic language achieves its distinctive quality, its volatility and fragrance, through its marriage of tonality and modality. The classical and modern eras, often held within contrapuntal web of rare intricacy and mobility. However, such qualities suffer a sea-change into something more strange than rich. As Fauré's art evolved for weakened, according to (and detractors) it became increasingly cloistered and austere, his former bodiless evanescence, his enchanting play of light and shade, transformed into an increasingly gaunt and chiselled expression. Here, surely, is that mix of courage and desolation, of exaltance and despair that faithfully mirror not only a loss of idealism but the dark and eroding forces of our final years. Many pianists happy to charm their audience with the surface brio and civility of, say, the Second Impromptu remain silent when it comes to the Préludes, late Nocturnes and Barcarolles or the Impromptus Nos. 4 and 5, fearing to have all credence or connection with their admirers severed. For here, surely, is music expressing such private passion and isolation that listeners are left uneasy, unsure of their own of Fauré's musical ground; anxious for a greater sense of terra firma.

### Biographical Context

Not surprisingly, biographical considerations are inseparable from Fauré's final years. Official that his music would be heard and appreciated after his death, he remained conscious of present neglect. His publisher's wife used unmold Nocturnes and Barcarolles for jam-jar covers and during his lifetime Fauré frequently found himself consigned to near oblivion. He spoke of pianists (of Cortot in particular) who 'allow my music to pile up without actually playing it' and the difficulty he had in composing ('It is like a stocking full of hair – I can never really achieve a true sense of dilemma. The trauma of his father's death and his own ever-encroaching deafness ('I am shattered by this illness; it is disrespectful or, at the least, hardly polite to compose when one is deaf.') were a 'terrible cloak of misery'. There is evidence, too, that Fauré's marriage quickly became tired and mundane hardly fulfilling the needs of such a fervent and sensitive man. Yet despite enduring such crushing circumstances he was still able to draw on his own spirit, one that Gerard Manley Hopkins so memorably called 'the selfless self of self, most strange, most still'. The last Nocturnes, in particular, seem to reflect a dark night of the soul, yet even these works are pierced with sudden shafts of light, with codas that radiantly or defiantly suggest how Fauré's capacity to shape and articulate such emotional pain and complexity exorcised many of his darkest fears and torments. As Paul Crossley so acutely suggests, such music 'is born of struggle and conflict', in the cases of Nocturnes Nos. 9, 10 and 11, not struggles between extreme elements but of a music 'shocked and fragmented, and, of course, of necessity, in the cases of Nocturnes 12 and 13, anguished, bitter, unresigned; hence the crucial importance, in all cases, of the codas.'
