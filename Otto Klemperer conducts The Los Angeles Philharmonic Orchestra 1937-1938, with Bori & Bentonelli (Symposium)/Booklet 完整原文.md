### Page 3 (Liner Notes - Letters from Klemperer I)

**Otto Klemperer in Los Angeles 1933 - 1939**

Although my father had got to know America a little during his visits to New York as a guest conductor, first in 1924 and then 1927, his main impressions were of a metropolis. I, on the contrary, knew the place from the professional side: he had to learn a new repertoire, but he also found the most amazing peace there. Radio City and the Philharmonic Orchestra had given him Bruckner's Seventh, Schubert's Fifth, Haydn's Farewell Symphony and more were planned for the first program. The first rehearsals went well, and he was able to conduct a great deal. The letters illustrate the period from 1933 to 1939.

**To Johanna Klemperer. 17th October 1933** ... You can imagine how much there is to do in these first few days. Apart from the daily orchestra rehearsals there are always the interminable rehearsals with the artists, and then the long recording sessions in the evenings. On 27th October there is the first philharmonic concert, a dinner that W. Clark is giving in a hotel on my behalf. The Clark is charming. He is very interesting and intelligent. One rather difficult matter for me is my age to yell a lot, as C. is rather deaf, but I thank God that I'm so well looked after in this house; one can't imagine what a lot of work there is here. The village is not yet a town, but there are very few houses. Distances we can hardly imagine. I need half an hour to get to the orchestra, and there the distance between the first and last desk is very wide. At the moment the acoustics in the Bowl are absolutely nothing here, from a cultural point of view. I mean, no opera, no concerts, no theatre. The people here are nice and friendly. The climate is brilliant. The food is very good. There are hardly any concerts. The journey from New York took 3 1/2 days. I hope to be able to send you a little money. Perhaps you can return to me all in good health.

**LA. 31st October 1933** ... The week proceeds here very smoothly. Orchestra rehearsals every day; then perhaps an office hour (programmes etc.); at one o'clock lunch and then a rest. At 3 o'clock to the Bowl for rehearsal, followed by a walk in the garden and then early to bed. The walk in the garden is probably the most beneficial of all the cures here. I read or listen in the evening. I have a lot to study and try also to do new things. At the moment I have learnt four new works. The orchestra is improving from day to day. In the orchestra there are good and bad players, but in the woodwind there is good, but not that good... I don't even think of Vienna... to try and make myself at home is a very difficult undertaking. The first concert went quite well. I really like it, nothing here at all... Sunday, I did my first popular concert. I conducted Wagner's "Siegfried Idyll", Haydn's "Farewell" Symphony and a charming Suite from Bizet's "Children's Games".

**LA. 13th February 1934** ... There was a lot of trouble here last week. They wanted to dismiss many musicians from the orchestra. The guilds were against it and so we reached a compromise. The season remains as it is till the end of the season. It was all rather upsetting. Now that I have become accustomed to the place it is most unpleasant. I have a lot of things... (Nevertheless, the orchestra had made good progress, the number of subscription tickets had increased and it was possible for my contract to be renewed.) So for the first time, after a year there only as a guest, but he conducted a great deal.

**To LA. 3rd March 1934** ... Last week I conducted a fairly difficult Suite by Sessions...

**To Lóor Sztravinsky LA. 27th March 1934** ... I was able to perform four of your works: Petrushka, The Firebird, Apollon Musagète (new here) and the eight small pieces. The three last were extremely well executed, Petrushka less well. The orchestra was not yet accustomed to this music. — In the autumn we have a children's concert and the applause didn't end till I repeated the Galopp...

**To the family LA. 17th May 1935** ... I hope to God that my decision to return to Hollywood in June of the year was not a mistaken one. — The family? — in June.

**Concerts 1933/34** ... 9th June 1935 ... you know how you have withered about coming here. May the difficult decision be for the best. I had quite a good last season. I have conducted a lot of new works. Among them were Schönberg (in the old style), which contains a lot of lovely music. To finish the Mahler Symphony (naturally, no success).

**LA. 17th August 1936** ... Things are extremely bad here. Nothing definite about the next season. It is possible that I'll lose my position. I haven't slept a single night during yesterday (three weeks). I also have to go, on behalf of the orchestra, to a lot of cities, introduce the new season. It's a very unpleasant duty. I have nothing as a result... Our trip was wonderful. Europe is and remains so very beautiful and dear. My wife is very unhappy here. She has nothing to do. And although it is likely that terrible things will happen, the spiritual atmosphere of America is very bad.

**To Arthur Schnabel LA. 24th December 1936** ... I began my season in the third of December week. I believe to have played a great deal about whether the 10.000, when I did it for the first time. I've only now come to realize that it is a brilliant orchestra. I have suggested broadcasting it. A few days ago, I had the most terrible argument with my wife. She is against my American tour. The orchestra is as always excellent. I think a great deal about the question of an car or should settle down here. We both agreed, quite some time ago, that we can only claim a safe segment — in the Rachmaninov, Tchaikovsky and Hofmann. But will that at least be allowed? That is the question...

**LA. 6th November 1933** ... What will become of things here, nobody knows. Clara says he'll pay for this year only. If they can't raise any funds, the orchestra will be dissolved. I don't know what I shall do then. It is very possible they will have you all come over, however — no! When it all comes down to it, it is dreadful to be separated from one's family. The season may be very rich with all my achievements (the last concerts received huge applause), but without at least one person to share this joy, I am very, very lonely. — You can't imagine how unhappy I am! So I am prepared to make every effort but cannot offer more. Never mind, we must wait for a while... There, I already hesitate.

**LA. 25th November 1933** ... Work is the best thing. I have to keep up my nose to the grindstone! My first American season is finished — I'm completely exhausted. — We had a whole season of concerts...

**LA. 6th February 1933** ... Last week I conducted Bruckner's 7th Symphony. You remember I conducted it once in Berlin on the radio? After that the "Prélude to Afternoon of a Faun" followed. Then there's a concert for the radio... another in Pasadena, and so on. The following week, for example the "Fantastic Symphony" of Berlioz and Beethoven's "Pastoral" Symphony. In Hollywood Bowl, there is a very good hall. In the evening every week there are concerts I conduct all the Beethoven Symphonies. There was also a Gershwin Memorial Concert for which I had invited all the great black and orange well-known artists (again expenses). I wanted at least to offer the public something special. Whether it was a success, I don't know, I didn't like here yet. However, I still don't think the orchestra can exist. The subscription has decreased alarmingly.

**LA. 15th December 1932** ... Yesterday it was the "Fantastic" of Berlioz again. A rather weary public...

**LA. 19th January 1934** ... And if I don't write much, it is because work is so demanding and seems to go on and on. For example, today a concert in San Diego and then one in Los Angeles. Then a new series of concerts: Mahler (a very difficult work, which I have to rehearse intensively. It is also getting hot again. I have been working here now for almost three months. I am very busy. The work and doing with. Sun and dryness seem to be taking over for the next few months...

**LA. 26th January 1934** ... Just had another concert. First Symphony of Sibelius, two pieces of Glazunov and Dvorak's "Cello Concerto". The last two I didn't conduct before. The Sibelius Symphony is very popular here. It is still very much under the influence of Tchaikovsky... not something for Europe...

**To Lotte Egelin LA. 20th June 1937** ... We have a pretty cabin on the beach, where I can stretch out... It is strange here, the Pacific is certainly beautiful with big waves, but there's no smell of seaweed. I miss that. The country is beautiful, but somehow is missing. What could it be? I can imagine feeling happier in a small Austrian village in the Alps, even though the scenery would have to be much more beautiful. Why doesn't the bread here taste like it does in Vienna? It is the same wheat! And is it the water? I cannot really put my finger on it, but I am not so happy. I don't wish to complain. There is so much here to be thankful for.

**[Due to illness the engagement had to be cancelled in Autumn 1939. And the political events made Europe seem even more remote also "in the widest sense of the world".]**

**Lotte Klemperer 1985 Translated by Celia Marshall.**

Today, fifteen years after his death and particularly in England, where he was able to show the full extent of his genius, Klemperer is regarded as a figure of the concert hall who specialized in the mainstream German repertoire. He had no time for the superficial and the trivial, and his tempi, guardian of the classical tradition of the last century, were often controversial. All those who were privileged to witness him live around him, would have surprised those who knew his pre-war work in Berlin than the qualities which made him famous: the clarity of line, the avoidance of all sentimentality, the integrity of tempo, the depth of structure and the unity of form, the sense of architectural design, the rhythmic vitality, the avoidance of all sentimentality, the integrity of tempo, the depth of structure and the unity of form, the sense of architectural design, the rhythmic vitality.

Klemperer's relationship with the Los Angeles Philharmonic Orchestra was a complex one. He was often criticized for fast tempi, unusual interpretations and unorthodox programming. His concerts were sometimes met with a mixture of bewilderment and enthusiasm. Yet, from Peter Heyworth's magnificent biography (Klemperer, his Life and Times, Vol. 2) we know that the orchestra and the audience were profoundly affected by his conducting. The letters express deep musical responses, finding an outlet in conducting in his later years in Los Angeles.

These apparently opposite characteristics appeared early in Klemperer's conducting. Even as a student he was attracted by the music of Bach and Wagner on the one hand, that of Mahler and Richard Strauss on the other, a reaction against Wagnerian and expressionist extremes, typified by the rejection of the romanticism of the late 19th century. From the 1920s, his early frenetic conducting manner sobered and he came to reject all mannerisms associated with it, developing a style that was to dominate the more-than-less remained influenced by ferocious intensity. This combination of a deeply emotional nature with a powerful analytical capacity produced the unique synthesis that led him to the new world of feeling which had emerged since 1918... (Heyworth, p. 210).

In addition to the musical and physical difficulties to which Klemperer had also to contend, like so many of his colleagues, with exile from Germany and the climate of Europe in the 1930s. In addition to this he suffered from terrible illnesses and physical misfortunes, sometimes triggered by manic depressions. The condition worsened in America, where he was treated in mental hospitals. He survived a terrible paralysis; an accident in which he broke his femur in 1951 and, most serious of all, the fire in which he suffered appalling burns in 1958.

His character is almost impossible to grasp, still less to define, but those attributes of his work which are most typical, his concepts of morality, responsibility or routine, his only fixed points being artistic determination and musical integrity. Only in his last years in London, where he made his greatest recordings and produced many discs which have aided his earlier work. Those of us lucky enough to have heard him live in concert will remember him not in sheer vintage as great age and ill-health took their toll, will not forget the results.

If one looks at Klemperer's recording career, one has to say that the peak of his powers came in the mid-1950s. However, Klemperer himself has said that he made his best recordings in America in the 1930s. These recordings on Symposium make in those Berlin years are all that survive to enable us to form an impression of his conducting in the 1920s and 30s.

### Page 6 (Singer Biographies)

**LUCREZIA BORI (1887 or 1889-1960)** was the daughter of a Spanish officer, a descendant of the Borgia family, and an amateur singer. After being educated as a concert she studied piano and theory at the Valencia Conservatory. Her voice was discovered whilst travelling in Italy in 1907 and she made her debut as a singer in 1908. Such was her success that she was engaged in La Scala for the following season and such was her rise that in 1911 she sang in the première at that house of Der Rosenkavalier. Important at the time was the fact that she sang in the world première of Mascagni's Iris in Rome in 1911, she received in Barcelona, Buenos Aires, London, Madrid and Paris where she appeared also in Caruso as a last minute replacement for Lina Cavalieri. She was also a great success at the Metropolitan Opera where she remained until 1936. In 1912 she married Mario Mari, the manager of the opera house. After their divorce in 1936 she retired from the stage and was elected to the Board of the Metropolitan Opera — the only woman to be elected in recognition of her work during the lean years of the early '30s.

**GIUSEPPE BENTONELLI** is the name d'arte of Joseph Benton. He was born in Kansas City, Missouri in 1893 and was educated at the University of Oklahoma, graduating with degrees in languages and music. He studied (1923-1924) with Jean de Reszke in France and in 1924 made his debut in Italy as Giosuè in Rossini's Mosè at the Teatro Communale, Bologna. He made his debut in 1928 in Genoa as Edgardo in Lucia di Lammermoor. In 1929 he made his Paris debut at the Paris Opéra in La Traviata early in 1928. That year he sang with an Italian company in North Africa, appearing alongside the name Giuseppe Bentonelli. Then, in the summer of 1929, he appeared at the Verona festival in Faust with Cigala and Pinza. Appearances followed throughout Italy and elsewhere in Europe. Late in 1923 Bentonelli sang with Opera Italiana on one of his American engagements through the United States. In 1930 he sang in Buenos Aires in La Bohème with Claudia Muzio. He made his American debut in Chicago later in the year, singing Cavalleria in Tosca with Merlin and Amato. In 1935-1936 he sang a series of performances on NBC's Opera in English radio hour and in January 1936 he made his debut at New York's Metropolitan Opera House in Manon with Lily Pons. Extensive concert tours of North America followed, but in 1945 he returned to his native state, the University of Oklahoma where he was a professor of singing.
