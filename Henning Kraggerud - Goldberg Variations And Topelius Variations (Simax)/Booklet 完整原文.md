## STORIES AND VARIATIONS
*By Andrew Mellor*

Johann Sebastian Bach had a typically prosaic explanation for his life's work: 'I was obliged by industrious practice to equal industry if indeed I ever succeeded equally well!'

Almost any one of Bach's surviving scores could disprove that statement through sheer individuality and intuitive technical skill. But the piece we know as the Goldberg Variations goes further. This marriage of mathematical perfection to emotional expressiveness would appear to be an increasing power of its creator as encoded into the piece. It's not just one of its multitudinous facets apparently revealed as new truths, possibilities and mysteries to each generation. It is the very definition of the gift that keeps on giving.

It started out as a gift both focused and personal. Like a number of others of Bach's later works, the Goldberg Variations come with a dose of anecdotal mystery. Bach's first biographer, Johann Nikolaus Forkel, recalled that the former Russian ambassador to Saxony, Count Keyserlinck, had commissioned a keyboard work for performance by his servant, the harpsichord prodigy Johann Gottlieb Goldberg. The 'mellowness and gaiety' of Goldberg's technique would apparently keep sleepless nights at bay by the insomnia-ridden Count. Bach, according to Forkel, knew that flexibility and repetitiveness of a set of variations would be best suited to the job.

Some have doubted the details of Forkel's story (there's the issue of variation form that Forkel claimed Bach avoided as a musical template as far too restricting. That might explain why Bach's piece eludes not so much an overall theme itself as its bass line and the harmonic progression implied on the evidence of the Passacaglia in C minor, the Chaconne in D minor and other works. This is a device Bach relished.)

Bach's subtle trick would have been obvious from the work's title, as it appeared on the first edition published in the autumn of 1741: 'Aria mit diversen Veränderungen auf das Clavicembalo con due manuali.' There were various practical considerations to dedicate an aria in the style of a Sarabande. The set then fell into two halves of 15 pieces ending (conclusively) in G minor, before No 15 launches in the manner of a French Overture, an unexpected dramatic gesture. The variations progress in miniature three-part cycles of study, character piece and canon. That makes nine canons in all, equally spaced across the variations and dividing the space between the canonic parts by one interval, rising by rising from the unison. There were two small fugues, No 10 and No 22, each one six voices rising from the middle point. The aria itself is 32 bars long, divided into two halves of 16 bars each. Which would all count for nothing were this procession of patterns and numbers not poised so wondrously between etched perfection and the expressively expansive. If a piece is possessed of a human, beating heart despite its complexity it is this one. The conductor John Eliot Gardiner has observed the fact that technical and content is more palpable in Bach's keyboard works than in his music shaped by sung texts. But looking at the Goldberg Variations, it seems clear that Bach knew how to deploy and manipulate such tension. How, otherwise, would the second half of the set feel more freighted with meaning? Or the variations' progress from No 24 to the end take flight so palpably?

Such questions have long entertained musicians and wrested the score from the sole domain of keyboard players. Though Bach's music is more ornament than not in the transfer from instrument to ensemble, the challenges of form and of communicating the intense focus of the Goldberg Variations are perhaps easier in human minds - rather than a single one with ten busy fingers!

The most commonly heard orchestral arrangement of the Variations was made by the Russian violinist Dmitry Sitkovetsky in 1985. Sitkovetsky was inspired by imposing interpretations of the piece laid down on record by the pianist Glenn Gould. In 2017, Henning Kraggerud and the Arctic Philharmonic Chamber Orchestra colleague Bernt Simen Lund completed their own arrangement following a successful orchestral recording of Edvard Grieg's Violin Sonatas. During a process first described by Kraggerud as 'quite puritanical', the two musicians returned to the trusted sources of the Goldberg Variations that exist: the printed version by the score's first protector and any additions in Bach's own hand (the arrangement on the CD does include a version of the Aria found written in Anna Magdalena Bach's notebook).

As a point of principle, Kraggerud and Lund have avoided both the addition of extraneous instruments and a simplification of certain passages found in other arrangements including Sitkovetsky's. 'All the notes and pitches are Bach's,' Kraggerud says. 'Occasionally we double up the melodic voices, consistent with the mechanism of the harpsichord. We could do that because Bach also promoted by the instrument of the organ. Kraggerud and Lund have frequently for pizzicato - plucked strings, as on a harpsichord - in combination with bowed strings. In a final textual nod that echoes the division of a harpsichord of two differently voiced keyboards, they employ a solo string quartet that steps out from within the string orchestra.

Consulting Bach's with hand corrected scores have allowed for purification on the level of detail. We believe interpretative inspiration on another level. Unlike many musicians have taken, in all variation endings but we think this one here ends in a sort of paradise of a kind that occurs after many of the variations, and often instead of a fermata, could just as well mean the opposite; that the new variation follows immediately - something that fits very well with our interpretation', says Kraggerud.

Clearly, the Goldberg Variations feel very different when their mysterious depths are being grasped with instruments under the control of 28 fingers (plus one pair of simple keyboard) than they do business inventions through the ten fingers of two hands. But the variations are still unmistakably Bach's. Sometimes in the Goldberg Variations there are two parts in Variations No 7 (a gigue) and No 24 (a two-part canon) while in the 'gigue' and the 'bar' - we become more aware of the 'basic' voice's origins; hence, occasionally string instruments emphasize a melodic phrase in a way that would be difficult. No 25 (an intense G minor song linked to a 'lost Passion aria' by Nicholas Kenyon). But the agile movement of a wrist-propelled bow can also prompt No 5 (where all Bach's rapid keyboard flights transfer to strings, as in No 5 arpeggios towards the high end of the violin spins the long line of semiquavers or No 13's cadenza-like upward spirals in rocket-like groups of sixes, or mixed triple and solo instrument to solo instrument.

The string-only sonotony preserves the coherence of the whole while allowing for greater expansiveness. Sometimes we are made more aware of the 'basic' voice's origins; hence, occasionally string instruments emphasize a melodic phrase in a way that would be difficult. In No 8 the viola on the third and has 15 in common with a melody from Gottfried von Einem's Fifth Overture of No 28 sounds with obvious gravity. And No 28 we feel the added complexity of the inner parts, and even Bach lays out the two following 'Ich bin so lang nicht bei dir gewest' and 'Kraut und Rüben haben mich vertrieben' (Cabbage and carrots have driven me away). The two canons on a bass line of No 55, we feel the inherent contrast of the major and minor. The reprise of Aria that follows appears transformed by experience - we've started out in but at a different time of day.

Context, of course, changes everything. So too does the ability of the orchestra to juxtapose Bach's Goldberg Variations with his own Topelius Variations on this recording as a little, frek. (the Norwegian word sits somewhere in a meaning between rude, cheeky and naughty). The Goldberg Variations stands perfectly on its own, but perhaps that's why you would you listen when it's up against something so new?

Zachris Topelius (1818-1898) wrote essays, poems, fairytales and children's stories, and served for a period as rector of the native Finland in the eyes of his countrymen. In his anthology of both children and adults, he can be viewed as a Nordic Hans Christian Andersen. But Christian Andersen who was just 13 years younger than Topelius, and whose poetry (some of his verses were set by both Sibelius and Aulin) stands here in a sophisticated tribute to his musical peers: Mozart and Beethoven.

Kraggerud composed Topelius Variations in 2017 following a commission from the Ostrobothnian Chamber Orchestra. Finnish composer Bernt Simen Lund's inspiration to the town of Topelius's birth in Western Finland. The work was first performed by the OCO in May of the same year. It's written as a 15-minute score as 'inspired by Topelius's life in poetry and music' and 'a famous musical commemoration of the late Ludvig Holberg'. Kraggerud sought to capture Topelius's time by recalling the sort of musical language that proliferated during it, seen through the filter of the intervening years.

As for the 'variations', they are built on not one but two themes: that first heard at the very start on a lone violin, folk-like in character, and a second devilish allegretto agitato derived in part from its predecessor and carried initially by low strings playing pizzicato. While each is subjected to variation, the themes alone reflect the duality of Topelius himself, a man torn between idealistic innocence and a more complicated and almost demonic side, while the modulating parts between describes his doubts and fears.', in Kraggerud's words.

The tension between those two themes comes to drive Kraggerud's work on. Like Bach's Goldberg Variations - albeit not occupying the same clear. Classical ground plan - the piece is propelled by contrast, opposition and daring energy. Following a slow preamble in the form of a musical 'Once Upon a Time'. Also like Bach, Kraggerud uses composed time signatures to deliver cumulative momentum; as in Kraggerud's arrangement of the Goldberg Variations, the latter settles into a diverse ensemble of individual instruments for its final break. But here, we end with a bright, open-ended, unresolved cadence the likes of which must have tortured Bach's ears. Along the way, Kraggerud alludes to those special matters adored by Topelius. But the piece tells its own story with an almost child-like clarity; born of the Topelius fairytale Kraggerud heard from his mother as a child. 'I always believe in storytelling in music, even in a piece like Goldberg Variations which seems like absolute music,' he says, recalling those early days. 'The moment you start performing you are storytelling, really.'

## About the Artists

**Henning Kraggerud** is Artistic Director of the Arctic Philharmonic Chamber Orchestra, International Chair in Violin and fellow at the Royal Northern College of Music in Manchester and Professor at the Barratt Due Institute of Music in Oslo.

Henning's extraordinary reach as an artist is a result of his versatility and passion for music, as well as the genuine quality to his playing and the beauty of his performances. His teaching and educational writings provide fascinating insights into his multi-faceted approach to music-making. In his composing, arranging and improvising, Kraggerud brings his own works into the concert hall - recall the spirit of the old masters such as Fritz Kreisler and Eugène Ysaÿe.

Henning is invited time and again to join many of the world's most significant orchestras, including the Hallé, BBC Symphony, Oslo Philharmonic, Danish National Symphony, Tonkünstler Orchestra, Baltimore Symphony, Los Angeles Philharmonic and Orpheus Chamber.

As a chamber musician, he performs frequently both on violin and viola at major festivals and venues such as BBC Proms, Wigmore Hall, Amsterdam Concertgebouw, Verbier Festival, Hollywood Bowl and Carnegie Hall.

Henning is a prolific composer whose works are performed by many prominent musicians and orchestras around the globe. His largest-scale work to date is entitled Equinox. 24 Preludes in All Keys for Violin and String Orchestra. Commissioned, performed and recorded by the Arctic Philharmonic Chamber Orchestra with Henning as soloist, the work was composed as a musical counterpart to a story specially written by world-famous author Jostein Gaarder, and has been hailed as 'endlessly resourceful and surprising' (BBC Music Magazine, five stars).

Henning's output as a composer also includes *Pregheira*, commissioned and performed by the Brodsky Quartet in 2012, and *The Last Leaf*, given its first performance in 2014 by the Britten Sinfonia, as well as cadenzas for two of Haydn's cello concertos commissioned by Clemens Hagen in 2015 and *Victimae Paschali* for choir and orchestra commissioned by the Trondheim Chamber Music Festival. In 2017, the Ostrobothnian Chamber Orchestra commissioned and premiered Topelius Variations for string orchestra (included on this disc), which went on to be performed again by Henning on a national tour with the Australian Chamber Orchestra, and with the Portuguese National Symphony Orchestra.

Based well north of the Arctic Circle, **The Arctic Philharmonic Orchestra** is the world's youngest and northernmost orchestra. Since its founding in 2009, the critically acclaimed orchestra has become one of Northern Norway's largest and most active cultural institutions, performing around 150-250 opera and concert productions in various formats each year.

The orchestra's home base is Northern Norway and the Arctic High North, but has also made its presence felt internationally. The orchestra has toured China and Russia among others, and has played at famous concert halls such as Mariinsky in St. Petersburg, Beethovenhalle in Bonn, Grosses Festspielhaus in Salzburg and Musikverein in Vienna.

The Arctic Philharmonic Chamber Orchestra and Henning Kraggerud have recorded Grieg's Three violin concertos and Kraggerud's own, stunning composition, Equinox, followed up with a recording of Vivaldi's Four Seasons.

The orchestra also performs opera productions and alternate between different ensemble formats on a regular basis. Henning Kraggerud is the Artistic Director of Arctic Philharmonic Chamber Orchestra.

The Arctic Philharmonic Orchestra is frequently collaborating with world renown artists and conductors, composers and institutions.
