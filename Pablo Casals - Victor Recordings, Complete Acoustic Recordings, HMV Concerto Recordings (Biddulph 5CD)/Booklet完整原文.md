## Liner Notes

### Introduction by Leo Black

It's paradoxical to say that someone was born just too early for the gramophone, when his records cover half a century; but in a sense it's true of Pablo Casals. At the start of his recording career (in 1915) he was nearing forty and long since a world celebrity. If only we could hear him at twenty and complete our picture of that amazing life! Or, hear how he played the major works of the repertoire in his early maturity. Nowadays the name Casals could mean first and foremost the great man who stood out against Franco, and whose old age produced records that show him grunting and groaning with the emotion he breathed into every note.

We need to think back from there to the proud young Catalan whose career began in Barcelona cafés during his early teens - the man who first realised that Bach's Cello Suites were for playing, as well as for teaching, and who persuaded a worldwide public that the cello was an instrument on a par with the piano or violin. In 1915 a Boston critic hinted that Casals might be too perfect a virtuoso, whose performances stunned but scarcely moved the listener. Ten years later, he was at a remarkable point of balance. The virtuosity was still at a peak, but the long march into the profundity of his later years had begun (for the musical argument on those difficult late records is usually incomparable: both stunning and moving). Casals made a further batch of acoustic recordings for Columbia in the early 1920s, and soon after, with the advent of electrical recording, did many of them again for the Victor Company. The tracks on this CD were recorded with pianist Nikolai Mednikoff in Camden, New Jersey, between 1926 and 1928 (Casals had made his first recordings for the Victor Company in 1925 with pianist Edouard Gendron). The repertoire could be called superficial: complete Bach suites had to wait another decade, and Casals' concerto records were still around the corner. There are none of the great sonatas, and just one major work, Bruch's *Kol Nidrei*, performed with piano. (For some reason this has never before been issued.) Such was the taste of the time, which Casals knew better than to despise. Instead, he showed how the cello can sing and speak, and that all music has a spark of the divine when the performer's timing and sound are so right.

### Acoustic Recordings History by Teri Noel Towe

Casals had only recently celebrated his 38th birthday when he made his first recordings for 'American' Columbia (CBS) in New York on 15th January 1915. (While he told several people in later years that he recalled making records in Paris about 1903, there are no test pressings, record company documentation, nor other corroborating evidence of any kind to verify that such discs were made. Although Casals was blessed with a spectacularly accurate memory, it is possible that these recordings were a private affair, perhaps made on a 'home' machine.)

The 1915 recordings were the first results of a series of sessions that took place over the course of almost ten years. They were to initiate a relationship with American Columbia which, though severed in the mid 1920s and then revived at the time of the Bach Festival in Prades in 1950, was to endure to the end of Casals's life.

Casals had come to the United States in March 1914, in part to get away from serious turmoil in his personal life that had fractured the familiar routine of his day-to-day existence. Both the painful disintegration of his relationship with the Portuguese cellist Guilhermina Suggia, and the unravelling of the social and political fabric that he worked with, and the factors in his decision to come to the United States, much more important, was his passionate relationship with a talented and socially prominent American Lieder singer named Susan Metcalfe, whom he married in a civil ceremony in New Rochelle, New York, a few days after his arrival. (As a result of his marriage to Metcalfe, Casals was listed in the Social Register for a number of years!)

Casals was not particularly enthusiastic about making records. He viewed the undertaking ambivalently as a necessary evil, an essential part of building his career and keeping his name before the public in the United States where he had not visited in a decade. In discussing Casals' acoustic recordings, Robert Baldock makes the telling observation that "Casals was content to record when asked, but was never ambitious for recording career. He saw recording as a marginal activity — a good deal less important than live performance." (*Pablo Casals*, London 1992, p.109)

In fact, Casals's attitude towards the recording process in the 78 rpm era was much less nonchalant than that. A half century later, the nonagenarian Casals told this writer in a private conversation that he did not enjoy making records because they lacked the intangible excitement of a live performance. He also recalled that he tried to compensate for this lack of 'excitement' by speeding up the tempo of the music.

Casals made at least eight visits to Columbia's New York studios between January and April 1915, and all the recordings which were commercially released are featured in this volume. (Two other pieces, Fauré's *Papillons* and the first half of Leon Boëllmann's *Variations symphoniques*, were also made during this period. Casals never re-recorded them.) The Gigue from Bach's solo Cello Suite No.3 in C, made in 1916, is included in this volume of 1915 recordings in order to complete the three other movements from the suite.

The 3rd CD opens with the recordings Casals made during a series of five sessions held in New York during an open-day period in late-April 1916. It was not a happy time for the cellist. Only a month earlier, his treasured friends Enrique and Amparo Granados had drowned in the English channel when their steamer was sunk by torpedoes from an Imperial German submarine. Casals became involved in the organization of a benefit concert for the Granados children, one of whom was his godchild. Furthermore, personal as well as cultural differences were already straining his marriage with Susan Metcalfe. Despite all this, the 1916 recordings are an tribute to Casals' astonishing powers of concentration for they betray not a hint of sadness and concern.

Although Casals continued to follow the practice of spending the summer with his family at his beachside villa at San Salvador near Barcelona and the concert season in the United States throughout the war years, for unknown reasons, he made no more records for several years. When he did finally return to the Columbia studios in the spring of 1920, he recorded twelve sides in a two-week period, following a strenuous concert tour throughout the United States. At that time, in part because of technological advances over the course of the five years since his first visit to the Columbia studios, Casals recorded several of the more 'popular' items.

Much of the repertoire selected for these early recordings would seem to many of us today as a waste of Casals's prodigious talents. However, the concentration on 'bon-bons' and 'lollipops' exploited an important and often forgotten aspect of Casals' multi-faceted talents. While a student of Jose Garcia at the conservatory, Casals had been a seasoned veteran of the Café Tost in Barcelona where he had performed nightly for several years in order to earn money to help support his mother and his two younger brothers. Robert Baldock's description of the regular evening musicales at the Café Tost makes it clear that Casals was every bit as much in his element playing 'salon pieces' as he was interpreting the acknowledged masterpieces of the cello repertoire: the performance constituted something between a formal recital and crush-bar background music, but standards were often very high. Among the popular tunes, mazurkas selections and waltzes, Casals and his fellow players inserted more serious pieces. Eventually they convinced the proprietor, Senor Tost, to devote one evening a week to a classical programme. Word spread, and patrons began to attend that evening specifically indeed they came to hear *El Nino del Tost*, the Tost boy-wonder'. (*Pablo Casals*, London 1992, p.31)

With the exception of 1921, when Susan Metcalfe Casals returned to the United States from San Salvador without her husband, provoking one of the most profound physical and emotional crises that Casals endured during his long life, the recording sessions throughout the 1920s were annual late winter/early spring events. After the complete and irrevocable disintegration of his marriage, there was no longer reason for the United States to be the locus centrum of Casals' existence, and he returned back to his native Catalonia. He was to make no further recordings in the United States until after he settled in Puerto Rico in the mid-1950s.

In the beginning of 1925 Casals ended his recording contract with Columbia, and began recording for the Victor Talking Machine Company. The reason for switching allegiances is no longer known, but over the next four seasons, the cellist was to make an impressive series of records for Columbia's major competitor. Many view these discs (on disc 1) as his finest solo recordings, and the two selections with which this CD ends represent Casals' first Victor records. With the advent of the electrical process of recording in the spring of 1925, these were to be the cellist's last acoustic recordings.

Assessed as a whole, Casals' acoustic recordings open a fascinating and tantalising window into the 'youth' of an incomparable artist. They not only firmly and undeniably contradict the often-heard canard that Casals 'had no technique', but also provide irrefutable confirmation of contemporary critical reports of his playing. The words of the legendary Olin Downes, reporting on a concert performance that Casals gave with the Boston Symphony Orchestra a few weeks before he made his first Columbia records, seem the perfect description of the performances preserved on these priceless discs: *annihilation, oblivion of technical difficulties, and the superb balance of hand and heart that only the great of great artists achieve.*

(quoted in H. L. Kirk: *Pablo Casals: A Biography*, New York 1974)

Notes by Teri Noel Towe.

### HMV Concerto Recordings - Notes by Wayne Kiley

At the end of November 1936 Pablo Casals came to London to record the Boccherini Concerto in B-flat and Bruch's Kol Nidrei with orchestra this time. The cellist had not played in front of a microphone since March 1930, and these two pieces would be his first electrical recordings as soloist with orchestra.

Besides these two works, Casals recorded a Beethoven Cello Sonata (with his long-time friend Mieczyslaw Horszowski) and two Bach solo suites.

HMV's artistic director Fred Gaisberg had been instrumental in reviving Casals' interest in making records. A long-time admirer of the cellist, Gaisberg had produced the famous Cortot-Thibaud-Casals trio recordings in the late 1920s, and had persuaded HMV to send engineers to Spain to record Casals as conductor of the Orquestra Pau Casals in 1932. As well as the Boccherini and Bruch, Gaisberg wanted to record two English works for cello and orchestra: the concertos by Edward Elgar and Donald Francis Tovey. However, only the former was eventually recorded for commercial release - and not until nearly a decade later. By the time Casals came to record Elgar's late masterpiece in October 1945, World War II had just ended and the cellist was nearly seventy years old.

Casals' interpretation of the Elgar Concerto was almost unanimously criticised at his first London performance in 1936. Following this disappointing reception, Casals was determined to make a success of this work in England. By the time he performed the concerto in London in the spring of 1945 the reviews of his performances were glowing. Indeed, one critic spoke of his having "reached to an Elgarian mood of wistfulness that few artists now understand". With the critics' seal of approval, it was fitting that Casals would record the Elgar. The recording sessions were scheduled a few days before the cellist was to make an extended tour throughout England. Fred Gaisberg who had initially planned the recording had retired, however, and his replacement was the young Walter Legge. As it turned out, the Elgar Concerto was to be Casals' last commercial recording with orchestra. (Casals's 1953 recorded performance of the Schumann Concerto was a 'live' taping of a concert.)

As well as the Elgar, Haydn's Concerto in D was also scheduled to be recorded. This popular work was one of Casals' favourites: he made his American debut with the work on 12 January 1904 (three days later he performed in the White House for Theodore Roosevelt), and had recorded the second movement twice acoustically (in 1916 and 1924). However, because the Elgar sessions took an entire day, there was only a morning session left the following day to record the Haydn (the orchestra was already booked elsewhere in the afternoon). In the limited time available only the first two movements were made, and a recording of the third movement had to be postponed for a later date. Sadly, this was not to be - these recording sessions would turn out to be Casals' last before his self-imposed exile from performing in public.

On 17 October 1945, two days after the recording sessions, Casals embarked on his concert tour. He had been hopeful that the British government would sympathize with his stand against the fascist regime of Franco in Spain. No action was taken, however, and by the end of the month Casals realised that the new Labour government's attitude towards Franco was conciliatory. Although he would honour his immediate performing commitments he decided not to play further in England after this tour. He cancelled all future performing and recording engagements, declined to attend a luncheon in his honour given by the Worshipful Company of Musicians, and turned down honorary degrees from both Oxford and Cambridge universities. Furthermore, after the tour he donated almost all the proceeds of his concerts to the RAF Benevolent Fund. Concerned about this State of affairs, the pianist Myra Hess arranged for Casals to meet with government officials at the House of Commons and Buckingham Palace, but these discussions could not ameliorate the situation. The cellist regarded Labour's stance as a betrayal of socialist causes, and wrote to his pupil Germaine Grottendieck: "This is politics without feeling and without honour - a worker's party that bases its electoral appeal on the cry 'Down with Franco' and then, having won, bolsters the very same dictator." Casals had formally resolved not to perform in any country which acknowledged the Franco regime, and by the end of 1946, decided not to play publicly while the despot remained in power, in the words of Casals' biographer Robert Baldock, "as a public symbol of opposition to Franco".

Although Casals did not live long enough to witness the eventual downfall of Franco and the end of fascism in his native land, the spiritual message of peace he fought so hard to preserve in his lifetime lives indelibly through his inspired recordings.
