## Biography

JOSEPH HISLOP (1884-1977)

From Edinburgh choirboy to apprentice engraver, from amateur "musical evenings" to principal tenor at Stockholm's Royal Opera House: that, in brief, is the early life of Joseph Hislop. From Stockholm to Covent Garden, Chicago, Milan, Buenos Aires, Australia, the world: that is the middle. And the end takes him to operetta, teaching and retirement, from Sweden to London and back to Scotland, with golf, a daily practice of scales (up to top C) and death at the age of 93. That is a remarkable man's life story in brief.

At length, it can be read, fully documented, in Michael Turnbull's biography, the first to be written in book form, appropriately by a Scotsman, about one of Scotland's most distinguished sons. At one time his name was a household word, and, in Britain at least, almost every record collection that represented classical singers would have its Hislop.

That was in 1921. The earliest recordings heard in this present collection date from two years later, and the last of them are from 1930. They enable us to follow him in various stages of his career, beyond that brief seven year span, and it is fitting that the recital opens with the aria from *Faust*. This was the opera of his début, at Stockholm in 1914. It was also the opera in which he last appeared at Covent Garden, and the one in which he took his farewell of the operatic stage, at Stockholm, in 1937. His Covent Garden *Faust* was given in two famous performances in 1928, when the Mephistopheles was Chaliapin at his most unruly. Excerpts from the first night, June 22, were recorded "live", and two of them, Hislop's *Salut, demeure* and Chaliapin's *Invocation*, are included in Volume IV of *Covent Garden on Record: a history* (GEMM CDS 9926).

In *Faust*'s solo it is interesting to compare Hislop's singing in the studio and in the theatre, where he makes an unusual and magical effect by drawing out the F natural on the last repetition of "innocente" in a finely sustained pianissimo; and before that comes an unforced top C that fairly rings round the house. The studio recording, reproduced here, was the subject of one of the most enthusiastic reviews written for *The Gramophone* magazine by their veteran opera critic Herman Klein: "The opening of 'Salut' is the loveliest example of mezza voce that has been heard from a British tenor since the palmy days of Edward Lloyd. The whole air is also superbly sung and the high C at the end is magnificent".

The *Lucia di Lammermoor* recording which follows recalls another important, and perhaps crucial, moment in Hislop's career. In 1923 he was called to La Scala, Milan. This was the summit of an Italian tenor's ambition, or perhaps if he were frank he might admit it was the last stage to what in those days seemed the summit, the Metropolitan Opera, New York. One wonders whether Hislop quite understood this. He was given a role that suited him perfectly; he followed directly after the company's leading tenor, Aureliano Pertile; and he sang opposite their prima donna, Toti Dal Monte. He had such a success that he was offered a contract for forty five further performances; but because he haggled over the fee the negotiations broke down. At that time, with the bravos of Venetians and Neapolitans (he had earlier sang at the San Carlo) mingled with the enthusiasm of audiences all over the United States and the devotion of his adopted homeland in Sweden, he must have felt that the world was at his feet. In retrospect the failure to reach agreement with the Scala management can be seen (as he himself acknowledged) as the mistake of a lifetime. Much may have been lost; the single enduring gain of the Scala episode is this splendid recording.

In the duet from *Rigoletto* heard here he is with Lotte Schoene, comparably exquisite contemporary, and it is good to find Hislop sensitively moderating his power to balance her delicate tones. He also shows himself a polished stylist in the verse of "E il sol dell'anima" with its gracefully poised tone and elegant gruppetti. In the recitative "Ella mi fu rapita" we see how strongly he could characterize with his voice (the bitterness and affection well caught), and in the aria which follows we note how in the opening bars he takes his own meditative tempo and not the mechanical one two-three set by the orchestra.

The Duke in *Rigoletto* was another of Hislop's regular and admired roles. He sang it in Chicago and at the Manhattan, New York, with the mighty Titta Ruffo in the name part. Hislop recalled him to the end of his days as the most awe-inspiring of voices. It was also one of his roles with Antonio Scotti's touring company in America, while at Covent Garden in 1924 his colleagues in the opera were Cesare Formichi, advertised in the press as having the most powerful voice in the world, and Maria Ivogin, whose voice was second to none.

In the duet from *La forza del destino* he copes with another voluminous baritone, Apollo Granforte, using the same method as with Chaliapin: remaining faithful to his own lyrical principles, never being betrayed by the spirit of competition into forcing the voice, and, in this instance, remembering that the dying man is of too noble and stoical a breed to indulge in the sobs and self pity favoured by some more plebeian tenors.

This *Lohengrin* is an opera he never sang on stage; nor did he sing *Die Meistersinger* though at least twice in his career acting prospects opened for his doing so. The solos from *Lohengrin* and *Die Meistersinger* he sung in English, which is none the less attractive for its Scottish accent. This same critic, A.P. Hatton, heard Hislop again in 1930 when he was singing in Lehar's operetta *Friederike* (Frederica in the English version). He found much to admire but noted that the strain of singing it one night after night in such a long 'run' had caused a certain hardness in the upper register.

Among the three items included here, "O Maiden, my maiden", shows how attractively he could sing this music, and the final items in the recital show him, as he was known in concerts all over the world, as the most winning exponent of the songs of his native Scotland. They are an endearing part of his legacy.

Another part is the list of his pupils, headed by Jussi Björling. The new biography testifies to the devotion inspired by his memory. But essentially Joseph Hislop lives in the corpus of his best work on records, and that in turn lives only when it is made generally available on compact disc and when there are listeners with time and sympathy to gather his singing into the quiet of their own minds: It is that kind of attention that he repays most amply.

**JOHN STEANE**

## Introduction

Joseph Hislop (1884-1977) was a great Scottish tenor who is not better remembered probably because he sang mostly in Sweden though he also enjoyed an international career which took in Covent Garden and the Chicago Opera. From his records we can appreciate his fine, ringing voice: unmannered, totally musical, and increasingly satisfying upon repeated hearing. (Hislop was, incidentally, the principal teacher of both Jussi Björling and Birgit Nilsson).
