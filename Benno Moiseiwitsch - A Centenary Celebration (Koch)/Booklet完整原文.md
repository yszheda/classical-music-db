## 内页文章
### Benno Moiseiwitsch: A Centenary Celebration

When Theodor Leschetizky died in Dresden on 14 Nov 1915, a generation of great pianists mourned. To name only those that might be recognized today -- Alexander Brailowsky, Ossip Gabrilowitsch, Mieczyslaw Horszowski, Elly Ney, Ignace Jan Paderewski, Artur Schnabel, Paul Wittgenstein -- is to realize that the little bearded man of Vienna had superior insights into what makes one great pianist unique from every other. Benno Moiseiwitsch was also a graduate of the so-called "Leschetizky Method" and provides a case study of how this great teacher worked.

Benjuma Moiseiwitsch (he used, but never liked, the anglicized "Benno") was born 22 Feb 1890 in Odessa, Imperial Russia's cosmopolitan Black Sea window on the world. When one week the small child was picking out tunes on the family piano and the next playing them correctly, his sensitive mother encouraged self-teaching and began to give him lessons. She convinced her level-headed husband, an Odessa horse-breeder, that the child should have formal instruction, but it was the prosaic father that counselled his strong-willed child (who argued over interpretation with his teacher) to play what he felt. Moiseiwitsch clearly recalled those early lessons, and the trouble he had with Mozart: "It's the technically easy things that are the hardest to get right...if a work is simple, watch out!" In this instance, the family listened for days as he worked out the shape and detail of one particular sonata. When he solved his problem, and his mother heard the result, she immediately took him to visit Odessa's famous Academy.

The Imperial School of Music had a well-deserved reputation in 1897, but did not awe the self-confident boy. When asked why he should be admitted, he said "let me play the piano for you." At age 9 he won the coveted Rubinstein Prize, awarded for overall excellence. His family resisted pressure to convert this into a child prodigy's career, for which the pianist was later grateful. Just before or after (the story and reason vary with the teller) the events of the aborted 1905 Revolution pictorialized in Sergei Eisenstein's *Battleship Potemkin*, the close-knit family began to seek freer societies. Benno opted for England. He intended to complete his schooling at the Royal Academy of Music, but after auditioning, was told that nobody in London had anything to teach him. Leschetizky of Vienna was suggested.

The famous teacher took only three or four pupils at a time, which allowed him to choose among the young elite of the keyboard. Moiseiwitsch was turned down: Leschetizky expected those who came to him to have mastered the physical aspects (technique) of keyboard music, and told the applicant "I can play this better with my left foot." Moiseiwitsch, who ruefully admitted to heartily disliking practice as a youngster, was sent to a coach for months of intensive remedial exercises, after which the master accepted him.

The "Leschetizky Method" (as others called it, inasmuch as it was a formal system) sought to realize the unique potential inherent in the temperament of each student. The high-spirited Moiseiwitsch was recognized as a natural-born romantic, and instructed in the discipline of restraint. A standard "lesson" was for the student to play through a piece without correction, occasionally before an audience, and then to dissect what had just transpired. Leschetizky could be caustic. Once he told Moiseiwitsch "Stop mincing. You play that piece with a rhythm like a harlot strutting." On a more constructive note, he told his pupil, "don't play to an audience. Play to yourself and listen to yourself seriously." Each graduate had a slightly different story to tell, but all agreed that he brought out their best. For Moiseiwitsch he added an intellectual component to instinctive romanticism.

Most Leschetizky students emerged from his care with an emphasis on expressive color and tonal shading, Moiseiwitsch decidedly so. "At my first lessons with Leschetizky I learned to use my hands as a painter uses a palette -- to apply different tonal shades to the keyboard. This was not merely a matter of dynamics, or graduation of tone, but the method of using the hand and arm so that a pure limpid tone could be produced by one set of fingers, while others, for instance, were playing with a different touch and different degree of tone. These might be called a new tendency, for prior to Leschetizky's time they were understood by few...he had an uncanny way of finding out just what was wrong with one's fingers and telling the pupil in the most practical matter possible how to produce the result." But more than simply encouraging Moiseiwitsch to sublime technique into emotion, the teacher insisted on a balance of these elements, with the understanding that some music (Bach, for example) went beyond emotion. In later years Moiseiwitsch would sadly comment that the newest generation of virtuosos considered proper musicianship to be a technique which allowed them to play "louder, faster and more glitteringly." He knew better.

One lesson learned well was the need for constant study. The great teacher often illustrated points to be corrected, but the pupil was expected to supply changes. A favorite ploy was to have the student leave the piano until a new phrasing suggested itself, a strategy Moiseiwitsch used as he rethought his interpretations. To facilitate this, and to keep his fingers supple, Moiseiwitsch travelled with a silent keyboard shaped like a long suitcase.

In 1908 the master told his acolyte that it was time to "climb to the mountaintop." England, where a good part of his family now lived, was chosen for the debut, given at Reading on October 1st. The next year he presented himself to the larger public at Queen's Hall in London, commencing a memorable career. His reputation in Britain was quickly established, and in 1919 he made the first of over 20 visits to the United States. New York *World* critic James Gibbons Huneker was present at what for many had been a Death Watch (where "many a rash intruder had been conducted to his own obsequies") and concluded that for this newcomer funeral services would have to be deferred: "He has brains, a soul as well as the fleetest of fingers" -- over-quickness was also the flaw that Richard Aldrich of the *Times* singled out in an otherwise laudatory review which spoke of "true piano tone, singing, delicately tinted, hardly emotional in quality, but controlled with an exquisite graduation of dynamics." The rest of the world would admire just these qualities for the next 40 years.

Benno Moiseiwitsch was an enthusiastic world traveller, performing in England, Canada, the United States, Japan, Australia, South Africa and South America. He became a naturalized British citizen in 1937. During World War II, when every musician of his adopted home was expected to support the cause, he nearly killed himself in giving 844 concerts anywhere he was wanted. The toll was a loss of repertoire. For a pianist who had performed as many as 21 different concertos in a given season it was agony to be reduced to five or six popular ones. He never seemed worried about exhaustion, but later complained that he needed a three-year sabbatical to recover his repertory. In compensation, he was made a Commander of the British Empire in 1946.

Although a superlative and popular artist, Moiseiwitsch was never offered the recording opportunities accorded Vladimir Horowitz. Yet he did leave a prime example of the "Grand Style" which so many Leschetizky pupils excelled at. He considered hard and fast stylistic differences between periods of music to be artificial. His use of rubato depended on analysis of the psychological values behind the notes. Often, as in the Beethoven concerto on this disc, the result led to a broad (albeit carefully controlled) sweep of music which intrigues and delights. This should not be confused with the bravura ("look what I can do!") style he brought to his first meeting with Leschetizky, which was converted into something more delicate, thoughtful, even transcendental. Since Moiseiwitsch chose not to teach (an exception was made for Malcolm Sargent, whom he advised when the young man was deciding between the piano and the baton) his recordings will have to be our only reminder of how another age made music. The 1916-1930 discs for His Master's Voice, and the material on this disc, and his remarkable 78's of the Rachmaninoff concertos (the Russian composer/pianist considered his close friend to be his heir) all remind us that glitter and speed (which were Moiseiwitsch's to command, if he chose) are only part of a satisfying musical experience. Chopin, Beethoven, Liszt, Schumann and Rachmaninoff were his specialties. He lived well without apology, travelled by steamship because he preferred to avoid the "hurry up!" of modern concertizing (which allowed him to indulge his passion for bridge and poker) and was noted for his sharp wit. When he suffered a stroke after returning from his final U.S. concert of February, and died in London on 4 April 1963, a saddened world mourned the loss of one of its last true romantics.

- Brendan Wehrung
