Igor Stravinsky was born in 1882 at Oranienbaum, near St. Petersburg, and died in New York in 1971. A pupil of Rimsky-Korsakov, some of his earlier works show the influence of this famous teacher. He became, however, the radiant teratorn of contemporary music with the ballets he wrote for that now almost legendary figure, Sergei Diaghilev (1872-1929), of which the best known are *The Firebird*, *Petrouchka*, and *The Rite of Spring*.

Having shocked the French capital with *The Firebird*, Diaghilev followed with *The Firebird*, introduced in Diaghilev's first season of Russian ballet in Paris in 1910. Stravinsky followed up the next year with *Petrouchka* and in that same year received Diaghilev's commission for what was to become his most important work to date and a landmark in the history of 20th century music: *The Rite of Spring*, subtitled "Pictures of Pagan Russia in Two Parts," and frequently referred to, even in English-speaking countries, by its French title, *Le Sacre du Printemps* (Stravinsky himself always referred to it by this title rather than in English). *The Rite* was composed and orchestrated between the fall of 1911 and early March 1913. The first performance of *The Rite* took place at the Champs Elysées Theater in Paris, on May 29 of that latter year, with choreography by Vaslav Nijinsky (who also danced in the performance) and decor by Nikolai Roerich, to whom Stravinsky dedicated the score. Pierre Monteux conducted, and the presiding deity, Diaghilev, was in his box, alone on April 5, 1914, at the Casino de Paris.

The Paris premiere of *The Rite* of Spring has been documented as one of the most sensational in the annals of music. Audiences — notably including a full complement of aesthetes — hissed and catcalls actually drowned out the huge orchestra during parts of the performance. Diaghilev, who did not leave in outrage over the first few bars exchanged blows with each other on the gallery. But it is said that Stravinsky, who, it is well known, was never one for such "demonstrations," ran from the theater. Von Vechten, who was in the audience, reported that a young man who had stood up behind him for a better view was so carried away that he began to beat out the rhythm on his head with his fists; and that he was himself so transfixed that he "did not feel the blows for some time." Somehow the dancers managed to dance, and the orchestra could be seen — if not always heard — playing diligently. By the end of the performance, Diaghilev, the choreographer, Leonide Massine, and the dancers already proclaim his music as the curtain falls.

**brutal, savage, aggressive, and apparently chaotic; it hit the public like a hurricane, like some uncontrolled elemental force.** *The Rite of Spring* is the very antithesis of all those saccharine "Spring" ones that come to expect from every musician, painter and writer under the sun. Here is a spring seen from within, from the very bowels of the pregnant earth, which writhes in the agony of birth and gives birth in dark and violent forces. What I was trying to convey," says Stravinsky, "was the surge of spring, the magnificent upsurge of nature reborn."

The ballet scenario depicts a series of rituals held in propitiation of Nature with the coming of spring, and the audience reacting its climax in the sacrifice of a maiden who literally dances herself to death.

Part I is titled *The Adoration of the Earth*, and its seven sections are: Introduction; Auguries of Spring; Dance of the Youths and Maidens; Dance of Abduction; Spring Rounds; Games of the Rival Towns; Entrance of the Sage; Kiss to the Earth — Dance to the Earth.

Part II is *The Sacrifice*, comprising an Introduction ("The Pagan Night"); Mystic Circle of the Adolescents; Dance to the Chosen One; Evocation of the Ancestors; Ritual Performances of the Ancestors; and the Sacrificial Dance.

Ever since Monteux persuaded Stravinsky to arrange a concert version in 1914, *The Rite* having the mass resources of the orchestra, not the veritable dance it is — after the decades that have passed now since the music was first heard, new generations have come along to whom the work was no longer revolutionary; some of today's listeners were actually introduced to music with this very work — Stravinsky's influence on Debussy, Ravel, and Brahms. The *Rite* of Spring man no longer set off riots, but it still packs a visceral wallop. But there is still nothing quite like it — Stravinsky himself recognized the uniqueness of the work, and, as Rollo H. Myers noted in his article on the composer for Grove V, "having achieved what he set out to do in the 'Rite' he wisely decided not to attempt anything further on these lines..." from this point onwards he altered his course."

Stravinsky's *Petrouchka* began to take shape in 1910, shortly after the composer had completed the score of *The Firebird*, and it is interesting to note that after the work was intended not as ballet music, but as a short concert piece for pianoforte and orchestra, called by the Russian name for that mythical tragic-comic figure of which another derivation is "Petrushka." This conception is retained in the score of the ballet Petrouchka as the music for the scene in the Second Tableau.

Eventually the larger concept of a story based on this sad and lonely figure was evolved, and Stravinsky added the introduction to the first tableau to the pianoforte music which now ends the First Tableau of Petrouchka. Finally, a whole ensemble is ballet, in which the ballet is named for the little wooden puppet with human attributes. In the title role to this day it remains one of the most satisfying of all ballets, with its brilliant orchestral yet timeless story.

The work is scored for a large orchestra, including a bold array of percussion instruments, while, of course, the pianoforte has a prominent part in the orchestration. The action takes place in the Admiralty Square, St. Petersburg, during the annual fair of the Shrovetide Festival, about the year 1830.

**First Tableau: The Shrovetide Fair.** All is bustle and excitement. Crowds of colorful characters pass to and fro, to the accompaniment of similar animation in the orchestra. Shrill, lively figures from the flutes begin and lead to a broader, more emphatic tune, based on the Russian Easter song, "Christ is risen." An organ grinder appears, and clarinets quietly introduce a fragment of a more lyrical theme. There is rude interruption by a drunken merchant — but the musicians persist with his theme. His lady companion strikes a triangle and begins to pirouette to a jolly little tune (which Stravinsky had heard played on a barrel-organ when he was working on the score of Petrouchka), which is suddenly drowned out by the brazen strains of a street trumpet. Now another street-musician appears, accompanied by a dancer; he plays on a musical box, and then both groups perform together, while their respective themes are heard simultaneously in the orchestra. All are joined by the crowd and they dance. Just then the lively rhythm recommence, and former themes are heard again, including the Easter song. For a big climax, two drummers give a loud roll on their drums as an old Showman appears.

Prokofiev's *Love for Three Oranges*, Op. 33, is a work of his early years in the United States, and the suite from it was recorded in New York and first released in 1954. Upon his arrival in 1918, he was penniless and in need of moral and financial support. The American press was unkind to his music in its first recitals, and things proceeded with difficulty. He finally found support in Chicago in the person of the noted Chicago Opera and the conductor Cleofonte Campanini, the latter who offered to stage a Prokofiev opera here. *Love for Three Oranges* was the result.

It was inspired by a theatrical magazine of the same name, which took its name from a play by Carlo Gozzi and which Prokofiev had bought with him from Russia. The magazine was the melody of fairy tale, comic, and satiric elements and had actually sketched out some sort of plan for an opera version of the play on his journey across the Pacific. Campanini had told Prokofiev what he wanted in the way of a comic opera, and the latter promptly produced during the winter and received a sorely needed advance. As he said: "The theatrical aspect of the opera interested me tremendously. The way in which the action develops on the stage during the performance was always in my mind. The Firebird itself is symbolized by the interval of an augmented fourth that emerges between the entries of the violas and violins; this is joined by an extended variant of the chromatic theme. *Ronde des Princesses* moves at an easy tempo — to which reference was made above — and its supplement, presented by the oboe to the harp's accompaniment, and the ensemble of muted trumpets, respectively. The piece is laid out in a comparatively simple pattern: there is a short canonic prelude for flutes, which disappears after the presentation of the following themes, and in the rhapsodic concluding section. In contrast to the preceding movement the orchestral layout is dominated by the pastel shades of dreary instrumental solos and their subtly more substantial complements.

The *Internal Dance of King Kashchei* snatches us back to earth, as it were: over a persistent note in the bass a short theme of sharp rhythmic definition is presented, which generates much of the thematic and dynamic material of the movement. The central episode, which consists of a folk-song inspired theme, subsequently embellished with the gorgeous colours of the full orchestra, is repeated in a more agitated version after a reference to the rhythmic theme at the movement's beginning; enriched here with fanfare-like figuration. This carries with it the appearance of the chromatic theme from the introduction and concludes with a sharp orchestral outburst.

The *Berceuse* is built over a persistent figure of four notes, and is memorable for the strikingly original use of the bassoon. Poignant, melancholy, and poetic, its conclusion vanishes into the shadowy shimmer of the end of the first Tableau, leaving the ghostly figure of Kashchei. This section, followed by a sustained bass solo and repeated a number of times in a successively in higher octaves, leads to the final section. *Ronde des Princesses* moves at an easy tempo — to which reference was made above — and its supplement, presented by the oboe to the harp's accompaniment, and the ensemble of muted trumpets, respectively. The *Internal Dance of King Kashchei* snatches us back to earth, as it were: over a persistent note in the bass a short theme of sharp rhythmic definition is presented, which generates much of the thematic and dynamic material of the movement.
