## LINER NOTES

### A Song Transcription Enables a Pianist

A song transcription enables a pianist to be both singer and accompanist – a liberating union of wills and skills at the service of some of the loveliest melodies ever written. So writes the pianist Stephen Hough, whose own piano transcriptions of songs constitute notable additions to the repertory. Vocal lines here can safely soar high above the keyboard, while the accompaniments can be fearlessly woven in complex and luxuriant textures.

The solo piano recital is a perfect oxymoron, for it requires the translation of an entire orchestra's worth of sound into perfect harmony; a percussion instrument that must nonetheless be made to sing.

This area of piano transcription began, to all intents and purposes, with Liszt. Bach, of course, had long transcribed transcriptions of the instrumental works of various composers of his own time. Mozart barely addressed the art (he made versions of three Bach fugues from *The Well-tempered Clavier*) and Beethoven limited himself to writing a transcription for piano and orchestra of his own violin concerto. But the Romantic age – Liszt, Schumann, Chopin – entirely eschewed transcription.

Liszt's arrival on the scene coincided both with the development of the piano in its increased power and sonority, and the growing popularity of the recital that offered pianists an ideal setting in which to display their particular gifts. Liszt's concerts were invariably virtuosity and shameless showmanship, though his genius also showed how the music of other composers' music was more egocentric indulgence. With few virtuosity came also increased access to music-making for the public of the 1850s and 1860s. One service provided by Liszt's myriad transcriptions was to introduce otherwise deprived piano to some of the piano (or, at least, fairly recent) masterpieces by Beethoven, including the symphonies.

Complete symphonies, overtures, themes from operas, organ works, chamber music and songs all furnished a vast body of work for solo piano, some of it transcriptions of well-loved originals, more often than not embellished by writing that reflected Liszt's transcendentally technical, imaginative, daring and idiosyncratic harmony.

If we dwell on Liszt's example, it is only because he was the greatest and wide-ranging practitioner of the art in history. A long succession of great names followed in his wake, the best known of whom include Alkan, Tausig, Balakirev, Busoni and Rachmaninov, though none of them made a habit of the piano transcriptions of songs (Liszt produced only 170 transcriptions, 84 of them songs). The arrival of the radio and gramophone obviated the need for a pianist to introduce masterpieces to audiences (though there were many notable exceptions, of course, not least Rachmaninov, Horowitz, and Prokofiev's transcription of his ballet music for piano).

While these changes in fashion and musical taste have kept pianists who kept transcriptions in their repertoire came back on pieces written decades earlier.

It was left to Leopold Godowsky (1870-1938) to contribute a significant (if small) body of song transcriptions involving by Schubert, Schumann, Brahms and Mahler. Still in on Blume (Schumann) and Sändchen (Richard Strauss). The subtle harmonisation and the supreme craftsmanship have been an inescapable influence on the few who followed in his footsteps. (Some European jazz pianists had meanwhile developed their own treatments of popular songs).

In 1971 I was preparing for the International Scriabin Piano Competition in Oslo (under Cor de Groot and Nikita Magaloff). It was then that I first became acquainted with the works of Leopold Godowsky when, in my native Holland, I heard Nelson Freire play Godowsky's *Symphonic Metamorphosis on Die Fledermaus*. For me the most fascinating aspect of Godowsky's music was (as for others) the harmonic and contrapuntal character, next to the mastery of the left hand for which Godowsky wrote such a large number of works.

Though I began my first transcription (*Kreisler's Schön Rosmarin*) as a result of hearing this recording, my subsequent recitals of Liebesleid and Liebesfreud, as well as other magnificent transcriptions, it was Godowsky who was largely responsible for my having been influenced the most, especially in the area of song transcriptions and writing for the left hand alone. Others whose work has had an influence on me were Fauré and Ignaz Friedman.

The harmonic aspects of music are to me so overwhelming (just look at jazz!) that in the case of many compositions, such as Johann Strauss II and Gershwin but also Schubert, Schumann and others it becomes, as a transcriber it is like swimming in a big harmonic pool.

At the same time there are many places which are hard to transcribe, unless they become a literal transcription without any change. For example, Rachmaninov's song *Vocalise* was transcribed by him for orchestra but without altering a single note. This is quite mysterious why some pieces almost offer themselves for transcribing, where other works seem to have a character that does not allow a modernization of the piece, for instance the Mahler songs. It is all interesting to note that Godowsky never transcribed many Schubert songs he did not arrange one Brahms song, even though Godowsky wrote many marvellous pieces.

The masterly way in which Godowsky managed to write for the left hand alone inspired me to transcribe some songs for the left hand as well. As Godowsky calls the piano an orchestra from the left hand side, what pieces can be transcribed, unless the stylistic aspect forbids it. It may be a song, a symphony or even a complete opera.

For this CD I have chosen these songs of the German repertoire which are most closest to my heart. Some are just literal transcriptions and others are more elaborate transcriptions, as these works permit free handling of the score, so that in these cases one may consider them as transcription as well as explorations of the possibilities of the piano as an instrument.

## About Frédéric Meinders

The Dutch pianist and composer **Frédéric Meinders** was born in 1940 and received his first piano lessons at the age of five from his parents. He later studied with Jan de Man at the Royal Conservatory in The Hague where he was awarded the Prix d'Excellence. In 1968 he won the first prize at the International Viana da Motta Competition in Oslo, leading to concerts all over Europe, North and South America and the Far East.

Frédéric Meinders has played with leading orchestras including the Concertgebouw, Vienna Symphony and Munich Chamber Orchestra under conductors such as Sir Colin Davis, Bernard Haitink, David Zinman and others.

He has also been active as a chamber musician and performed with Arleen Auger, Janos Starker, Nelson Freire and many more. From 1997-2003, Frédéric Meinders was visiting professor at the Federal University of Minas Gerais in Brazil.
