## Liner Notes by Richard Wigmore

Acclaimed by Herbert von Karajan, no less, as 'the Carmen of the century', Teresa Berganza was a singer who defied easy categorization. Her voice, pure and even throughout its two-and-a-half octave compass, was both distinctive and exceptionally beautiful: a creamy, subtly-coloured mezzo that soared and shone in the soprano range. Beginning her lengthy career in Monteverdi, Bach and arie antiche, Berganza first made her international reputation in Mozart and Rossini. Two decades later, her revelatory Carmen in Piero Faggioni's 1977 Edinburgh Festival production, conducted by Claudio Abbado, is still talked about by those whose heard it. 'Carmen involved a personal change in my character,' she later recalled. 'Until then I'd always played "nice" roles, with none of the sheer force of this one.'

Berganza was, of course, a natural in the operas and (in her early career) zarzuelas of her native Spain – say, as the miller's wife in Falla's *El sombrero de tres picos*, or as Salud in *La vida breve*. After her triumphant Carmen, and now with a richer, smokier lower register, she further extended her operatic repertoire to embrace such roles as Dulcinée (in Massenet's *Don Quichotte*), and her American debut as Isabella (L'italiana in Algeri) the following year. After her 1960 Covent Garden debut as Rosina in *Il barbiere di Siviglia*, this plum Rossini mezzo role became another of her calling cards, along with the title role in *La Cenerentola*, with which Berganza had delighted Glyndebourne audiences in 1959. In his history of Glyndebourne (Methuen, 1965), Spike Hughes admiringly noted 'that peculiar Spanish quality of voice and personality which had characterized Conchita Supervia's classic performance of the part'.

'Style, grace, technical assurance, and an ability to characterize the music' was Philip Hope-Wallace's spot-on summation in Gramophone of the Mozart recital Berganza recorded with the LSO under John Pritchard in December 1962. In Mozart's day, the distinction between soprano and mezzo-soprano was virtually non-existent. Dorabella in *Così fan tutte*, nowadays usually taken by a mezzo, was billed as a soprano by Mozart and first sung by Louise Villeneuve, whose repertoire included high-flying coloratura roles. Such versatility was much rarer two centuries later. Yet Berganza here encompasses with equal success the impetuous ardour of the priapic page in Figaro, the contrasting sisters in *Così fan tutte* and the vacillations of the lovelorn Sesto in *La clemenza di Tito*, goaded by the ruthless Vitellia into assassinating the Emperor. In the Cherubino arias, both taken rapidly, she combines breathless ardour with a vital sense of rhythm and a care for a true legato that were always Berganza hallmarks. Her Fiordiligi and Sesto solos both have a certain patrician restraint. Yet you would go far to hear the rapid triplets in Fiordiligi's opera seria parody aria 'Come scoglio' or Sesto's scena 'Parto, Parto' with clarinet obbligato (here flawlessly played by Gervase de Peyer) sung with such mingled grace and jeweled precision. Between the Fiordiligi arias, in *Così fan tutte* the following year, Berganza is delightfully blithe expounding the flighty Dorabella's carpe diem philosophy in 'E amore un ladroncello' – 'Love is a little thief'.

Mozart composed the beautiful scena for mezzo-soprano, piano and orchestra 'Ch'io mi scordi di te' as a farewell gift for his friend Nancy Storace, the English soprano who had created the role of Susanna in Figaro. The soft, sensuous orchestral colouring and close interweaving of voice and keyboard give it a chamber-musical intimacy unique among Mozart's concert arias. Singing with poise and elegance in a soprano tessitura, Berganza, in tandem with the ever-sensitive Geoffrey Parsons, catches the scena's peculiar tenderness and inwardness.

In 1981, when she recorded a clutch of mainly early Mozart concert arias with György Fischer, Berganza's voice had lost little of its early bloom. As John Steane noted in Gramophone, 'she is a singer who has kept herself in trim, so that the evenness of her scales and of each separate note remains virtually unflawed'. She now handles the Italian texts with an enhanced eloquence and intensity, as in Arsace's poignant farewell to his lover in 'Ombra felice ... lo ti lascio', written for the alto castrato Francesco Fortini. Of the other Mozart arias, three date from his precocious childhood: 'Conservatevi fedele', written in The Hague at the age of nine, and two settings of Metastasio texts, KV 77 and KV 82, the former a vivid expression of Timante's horrified (but unfounded) suspicion that his wife might also be his sister. Berganza's impeccable Mozart style, founded on a seamless legato, is likewise displayed in 'Al desio di chi t'adora', composed for the 1789 revival of Figaro. The new Susanna, Adriana Ferrarese, was a much grander and, evidently, less amenable singer than Nancy Storace. Mozart accordingly wrote for her an elaborate two-section rondo, apt for the singer who was to create the role of Fiordiligi in *Così fan tutte* the following year.

Except for Haydn's *Arianna*, the remaining 'Eighteenth-century Portraits' are taken from one of Berganza's earliest LPs, recorded in 1960 with the Covent Garden Orchestra under Alexander Gibson. Planned to display the singer's versatility, the program ranged from Handel's grieving Cleopatra and Gluck in plaintive or tragicheroic mode, via arias by Pergolesi (Serpina's waspish 'Stizzoso'), sung with guile and brio) and Paisiello, to the touching aria with bassoon obbligato in which Medea's confidante Neris bewails her mistress's fate. Berganza sounds distinctly cool in the solos from Orfeo (written for the famed castrato Guadagni), and lacks an ideal weight and grandeur of tone for Alceste's defiant address to the spirits of the underworld. But her mastery of bel canto pays rich dividends in the dulcet Paisiello aria, and her limpid, softly floated singing of Paris's yearning 'O del mio dolce ardor'.

Haydn's dramatic cantata of 1789, *Arianna a Naxos*, quickly became one his best-loved works, and is now a favourite recital opener for mezzos. With her first husband and regular accompanist Félix Lavilla, Berganza lives intensely each phase of this quasi-operatic scene, from Ariadne's voluptuous awakening, through her impatience for Theseus's return and the appalled realization of her abandonment, to her final aria of outrage and despair. And throughout the tortuous emotional journey, Berganza retains a classical nobility of style that never lets you forget that Ariadne is the daughter of Minos.

Arianna a Naxos was recorded in Vienna over two days in May 1977. A further two days were scheduled for Haydn songs, but the intended album was evidently not completed and the release of the cantata, as part of a Berganza portrait LP, was delayed ten years until February 1986.

Richard Wigmore
