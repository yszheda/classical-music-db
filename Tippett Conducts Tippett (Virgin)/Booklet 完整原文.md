The three works recorded here contain the ingredients for a miniature portrait of Britain's most distinguished living composer, Sir Michael Tippett. A youthful 83-year-old, he remains one of the most popular figures on the musical scene. Moreover, the plurality of his musical style, his intellectual breadth and his championship of humane values in a technologically dominated world seem ever more relevant today. His music oozes energy and freshness. With every work he seems to explore a new sound-world, something that will undoubtedly be reflected in the stylistic diversity of his output over the 60 or so years he has been composing.

Now, at 83, Tippett is as 'currently writing' *King Priam* and other pieces which he has in the pipeline. At the same time, Tippett's music is steeped in vernacular traditions – from bagpipe music and the blues, to the Concerto for Double String Orchestra to *A Midsummer Marriage* – but he is able to communicate with a wide audience. A bonus to this portrait of Tippett is Tippett's hit presence as conductor in a recording for the first time in about fifteen years.

Listening to the joyful, vital rhythms and melodies of Tippett's Concerto for Double String Orchestra, you wouldn't think that it was the end-product of a long and difficult apprenticeship. Born in London in 1905, Tippett spent his boyhood away from the musical scene in Suffolk. His parents weren't musical. He had heard an orchestra only once in his school-days in Leicester. He declared his intention of becoming a composer. They were totally baffled. In the end they enabled him to study at the Royal College of Music. As he was never able to afford to pay teachers, he more or less taught himself to compose; but later, dissatisfied with his compositions so far, he went back to his studies with a noted expert on sixteenth-century madrigals, R.O. Morris.

Instinctively, Tippett discovered he had a flair for music conceived in linear terms, with an emphasis on melodic line and counterpoint. Morris helped him develop a formidable range of contrapuntal skills. But Tippett also taught himself by including virtually every madrigal in the repertoire of a small choir he conducted in Oxted, Surrey. Tippett's earliest acknowledged compositions – his String Quartet No. 1 (1934), Piano Sonata No. 1 (1934-5) and his Concerto for Double Orchestra (1938-39) – are all a celebration of contrapuntal virtuosity. This is evident right from the start of the *Concerto*, where the two string orchestras set off like melodic line racing against each other at breakneck speed, with each soloist entering in turn, like voices in an exuberant, jazzy manner.

Mention of jazz brings us to another facet of Tippett's style – his love of the vernacular. Responding to all kinds of folk-music, to early jazz recordings by Louis Armstrong and others, and to the balletic music of Stravinsky and Bartók, Tippett found himself attracted to all sorts of irregular, unpredictable accents in his music. Much of the energy of the *Double Concerto* comes from its exciting play with additive rhythms. Bloomy harmonic inflexions (Tippett was already a great fan of Bessie Smith) also became a feature of his own harmonic idiom: listen to the opening melody of the middle movement of the *Concerto*, where they are quite unmistakable.

From student days, when Tippett went to the Proms to hear all the Beethoven symphonies, Beethoven was his god. He was impressed above all by Beethoven's ability to combine the widest expressive range with a strong control over the musical architecture. The *Concerto*'s two fast outer movements emulate the characteristic layout of Beethoven sonata-movements, while the shape of the central slow movement was derived directly from the slow movement of Beethoven's String Quartet in F minor Op. 95, consisting of a song-like opening section, a fugal episode and a return of the song.

However, thanks to Tippett's bubbling musical invention, the *Concerto* always sounds spontaneous rather than technically contrived. A memorable instance is the sudden appearance of a new theme near the end of the finale: one based on a Northumbrian bagpipe tune, full of 'Scotch snap' rhythms, but extended to bring the *Concerto* to a climactic surge of exultant, overlapping melodic lines.

Like many other twentieth-century composers, Tippett has been less intrigued by the romantic idea of a concerto – one in which a single soloist competes against a large orchestra – than with the early eighteenth-century variety, in which certain members of an instrumental ensemble were allowed opportunities to shine as soloists. Tippett's most overt tribute to this genre came when he was commissioned by the 1953 Edinburgh Festival to write a piece commemorating the tercentenary of the birth of the Italian master, Arcangelo Corelli (1653-1713). Tippett based his composition, the *Fantasia Concertante on a Theme of Corelli*, on two contrasting motifs from a Corelli concerto (Op. 6 No. 2 in F). He also recreated in his own way the tripartite layout of Corelli's string orchestra: the concertino, consisting of three soloists (two violins and cello), concerto grosso and concerto terzo – the rest of the strings, with a special group equivalent to the harpsichord continuo that Corelli would have used to add richness to the string sonority.

Nevertheless, the *Fantasia Concertante* isn't simply an academic imitation of past compositional methods. Tippett found himself relishing more to the Italian lyric tradition as a whole, from Monteverdi to Puccini. Having also just completed his first most radically lyrical opera, *The Midsummer Marriage*, Tippett acquired such creative self-confidence that he was able to write the *Fantasia* in three weeks flat – hence its intensity of expression.

The melodic lines here are indeed decorated with turns, trills and roulades to a far greater degree than in the *Double Concerto*. After the opening sequence of seven contrasting variations on the Corelli motif, fantasy takes over completely, as Tippett adds several layers of decorative counterpoint to a transcription by Corelli of a Bach organ fugue in B minor (itself based on Corelli material). Swirling and cascading, the music attains an almost erotic climax, calming down in a mournful slow instrumental extension, in fact, of the final down chorus music from *The Midsummer Marriage*.

Incredibly, both works were not at first well received. The *Double Concerto* was initially turned down by the BBC, several publishers and concert promoters, and was first played by an orchestra formed by Tippett mainly from unemployed slum musicians. The *Fantasia Concertante* too was denounced, even in advance of its première, by the conductor Sir Malcolm Sargent. Both works were championed, however, by the German conductor Hans Schmidt-Isserstedt, and later by other leading figures like Sir Colin Davis, and have now attained wide circulation.

While Tippett's concertos and symphonies show his skill as a 'pure' musician, pre-occupied with the craft of composition, his dramatic works – four operas so far and choral works for the concert hall like *A Child of Our Time* (1939-41) and *The Mask of Time* (1980-82) – are wonderfully idiosyncratic products of a creative mind ever open to the world outside. In the anguished and suffering of individuals and societies in a strait-jacket time, Tippett also manifest his delight in a wonder culture, in the contemporary theatre, film and television.

Quite typical of Tippett's later music is *Songs for Dov*, a song-cycle with orchestral accompaniment. Dov is the hero of Tippett's third, and most inventive opera *The Knot Garden* (1966-70) – a kind of updated version of Shakespeare's *The Tempest*, in which the characters often revert to their Shakespearean prototypes. Dov, a young musician this time a therapist, continuing outside the opera the career of one of its key personalities – Dov, a young musician this time a therapist.

### Dov finalement se met à méditer (comme Pasternak), se demandant s'il est possible de défendre les valeurs éternelles de la nature contre les valeurs éphémères de la ville décrites par deux morceaux de boogie-woogie.

La réponse n'est pas facile à trouver. Dov ne peut que commenter, ironiquement, 'Sure, Baby' (C'est sûr, mon chou). C'est là ce qui fait l'essentiel de cette œuvre: son caractère provocateur qui a toujours provoqué et provoquera, je l'espère, des réactions faciles et bienheureuses. Ce dialogue met la vérité à nu. Tout en partant au coeur, elle cherche aussi à provoquer, d'où la pérennité de sa valeur, de sa qualité et de sa signification.

The Compact Disc Digital Audio System offers many advantages over conventional analog recording systems:

- **Extended frequency response**: The system captures frequencies from DC to 20 kHz, the full range of human hearing.
- **Wide dynamic range**: With over 90 dB of dynamic range, the system can reproduce the softest pianissimo to the loudest fortissimo without distortion.
- **Low distortion**: Digital recording eliminates the harmonic distortion and tape hiss associated with analog systems.
- **Durability**: The disc is resistant to wear and tear, and will not degrade with repeated playback.
- **Random access**: Any track on the disc can be accessed instantly, without the need to rewind or fast-forward.
