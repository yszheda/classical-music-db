### Drama & Music in Le Vin Herbé (Pages 2-3, Image 7)

The great bulk of the desired effect is achieved through the simple narration of the protagonist's being in love even before the appearance of the love potion. In Richard Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde* (1859), the audience experience of love is conveyed by the tonal ambiguities and orchestral fabric. With Frank Martin's *Le vin herbé*, this effect is achieved through the music itself. One is centered on an almost constant state of dramatic reminiscence, the vocal lines - like the love duet in Wagner's *Tristan* - are the local functions. This observation describes the music of the work as it is concerned, as it is given this common ground, the musical structure of the two works could not be more different. It is not just that Martin's orchestra is smaller; Martin, in fact, could easily have written for an orchestra like Wagner's. Wagner writes in a virtual continuum of thematic reminiscence. Martin offers something different: a series of discrete dramatic vignettes in Martin's signature idiom, more beholden to Debussy than to Wagner, such are permeated in various inner-voices and the instrumental texture with thematic reminiscences. Martin achieves his most lush chromaticism, often impregnated with the intervals of perfect fourths and fifths - through the manipulation of the melodic material, for moments of compelling emotion, such as the lovers' first kiss. The lovers' music in the forest, in Part I Scene 3, is also rich. These are also set to music of compelling inner-voice writing. Much of Martin's compositional technique is rooted, in his own admission, in his lifelong love of J.S. Bach.

In the first act, Martin uses the ascending fourths of the guiding intervals, for both melodic and choral purposes. The thematic cells mentioned above in *Le Vin Herbé* are also found in Martin's late works. There are three: the first is the ascending fourth, heard at the opening of the work and at important moments, such as the first time the lovers are alone in Part I Scene 3.

The work is sung in a basically good poetic syllables with just one or two of those awkward diphthongs. The Notre Dame school of composers in the late twelfth century set Latin texts with great freedom and flexibility. Martin's declamation - the melodies and Bédier's subtle language.

### CONDUCTOR'S NOTE by Mark Shapiro (Pages 6-7, Image 5)

*Le vin herbé* holds a special place in the hearts and history of I Cantori di New York. We first performed it during our inaugural season as the group's conductor. I returned eight years later to celebrate I Cantori's fifteenth season. Many of us cherish these performances as highlights of our musical lives.

Frank Martin originally scored *Le vin herbé* for 7 strings, piano, and 12 singers. When not singing solos, the six women and six men constitute the chorus. Several of the solos are actually trios, and seem to call for a balanced and unified choral sound. In practice, this requirement may prove unwieldy, for if the soloists and unified choral sound. One lamentable result may be that the work isn't as well known as it should be.

Millénaires arrive having to do with the kind of voice best suited to each task, the solos want voices, while the chorus must work to project its transparency as possible. Martin's declamation - the melodies and Bédier's subtle language. One lamentable result may be that the work isn't as well known as it should be. In its purely musical merits warrant. It seldom figures on the programming horizon of volunteer choruses, which is perhaps a shame. It is also, however, that this miracle should have been committed to disc.

In this recording, the challenge was to honor Martin's intentions while remaining true to I Cantori's anachronical mission. Except for the roles of Isolde, Tristan and King Mark, everything is sung by members of I Cantori. In the case of Brangien and Duke Huel, the soloist also sings with the chorus. Interestingly, as on early LP recordings in which Martin participated in panels, the performance is somewhat at variance with the full score, in turn differs slightly from the piano-vocal score. Who is closer to Martin's intentions? We feel the music makes a marvelous effect with performing forces those on this recording. Other conductors and ensembles might choose different solutions. We would observe that Martin Frank Martin himself was a most pragmatic musician, and this piece - and failed the flexibility that has enabled us to remain committed to it. We would be delighted if this recording helped other choruses, especially volunteer choruses, to become more aware of this wonderful piece and joined them in this program. *Le vin herbé*, a work for the ages if ever there was one, merits a wider public.

## I Cantori di New York (Pages 30-31, Image 18)

In 2001, I Cantori di New York celebrated its twentieth anniversary under Artistic Director Mark Shapiro. I Cantori has concertized extensively, with critical acclaim. In April 1997, the group received the 1997 ASCAP/Chorus America Award for Adventurous Programming. It has been cited by the New York Times as "one of the most finely blended chamber choirs in New York" and praised for its "flexibility in dynamics and articulation" and "exquisite balance." I Cantori has given seven world premiere performances of works by some of America's most distinguished composers, including the group's three world premieres, in Tenth Anniversary concert at Alice Tully Hall, and its October 1995 Avery Fisher Hall debut in the Suite No. 2 from Ravel's "Daphnis et Chloé" with the Orchestre de la Suisse Romande.

I Cantori's repertoire includes well-known choral works as well as rarely performed works of merit. The group also has an active commissioning program: the 1997-1998 season saw the premiere of "The Four Seasons" by Matthew Harris and Shaun Ngui. I Cantori has participated in concert series at Trinity Church and the World Financial Center, and has performed as a guest of the New York Chamber Ensemble and the Concert Royal Baroque Orchestra.

By way of community outreach, the group has performed at homeless shelters and has donated a day of master classes and concert tickets to charitable organizations. In October 1997, I Cantori released its first CD, *Echoes and Shadows*, on the PCM Via label.

## Mark Shapiro (Pages 30-31, Image 18)

Mark Shapiro lives in New York City and is the Music Director of I Cantori di New York. He is on the faculty of the Mannes College of Music in New York, where he leads the choirs and teaches conducting. Mr. Shapiro is also a guest conductor with the National Orchestra of the Alliance at the Schola Cantorum in Paris, where he directs the Paris Choral Alliance.

A cum laude graduate of Yale College, he earned an honors diploma in orchestral conducting from the École Normale de Musique de Paris. Mr. Shapiro also studied at the American Conservatory in Fontainebleau, where he was subsequently appointed Assistant to the Director. In support of his conducting teachers, Mr. Shapiro has studied choral conducting with Nadia Boulanger. He has conducted well-received reviews by such composers as Lukas Foss, Frank Martin, Peter Mennin and Ethel Smyth, along with numerous premieres.

Mr. Shapiro is active as an opera conductor. He has conducted opera at Mannes and with Metro Lyric Opera of New Jersey and was Artistic Conductor to Mozart's "La Finta Giardiniera" at the Bard Center, and accompanies and coaches in New York. He was recently appointed the orchestra for the Bais Rivka Downtown on New York City, aired on PBS in the fall of 1999.

## A Note from the Producer (Pages 30-31, Image 18)

This Martin project afforded me both the opportunity to work for the first time with Mark Shapiro and his extraordinary I Cantori group and to revisit *Le vin herbé*, encountered as a young singer almost 20 years back when I sang the Tristan. Johannes Somary performed *Le vin herbé* in New York.

The Tristan legend, of course, touches anyone remotely attracted to music. As a legend and a source of inspiration for composers as diverse as rules away from my own incandescent memories of Birgit Nilsson and the Met of my youth, the lure of that love potion, of the deepest passions, of the forbidden is very strong in Frank Martin's hands as well. I believe the work - liberally, carefully prepared and generously performed by Mark Shapiro and his cast - gives a presence and a sensuous color to this subject that has been Germanically and heavily scored in the past. The resulting experience is intense, while Tharp and Whyte proved particularly excellent young colleagues, and conductor Shapiro handled it all expertly. My job was easy.

I extend our best wishes to all who supported the project: John Andrew, Charles and Betsy Boes, James Finley, Abe the Bear, William Fischer, Henry M. Lewis, Malcolm Macdonald, Lawrence, Pamela M. Reich, Theodore C. Rogers, Mark Shapiro, The Jeffrey Silver Fund, Steven M. Skinner, Roger W. Steel.

Recorded Spring 1999 at Holy Trinity Church, New York. This recording was made using a Steinway piano.
