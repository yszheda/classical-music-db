## Foreword by Dominique Meyer, Director of the Wiener Staatsoper

It is characteristic of theatre - of music theatre - that it always unleashes its greatest impact in an unadulterated, unaltered, unbroken flow. Here, the great moments of opera take wing: those individual moments that become history. When it is live, we see the truest expression of the artist, the human being on stage. It is precisely in the character of these live moments that lies the special power of music theatre in the midst of a world where an easy perfection seems often to dominate.

The CD series *Wiener Staatsoper Live*, which presents unforgettable historical recordings from the house on the Ring, is committed completely to this art of direct expression. At regular intervals, live recordings are published that were captured by Austrian Radio at the Vienna State Opera. Thus a large audience gains access to memorable performances that have taken place there since the reopening of the house in 1955. Those evenings at the Vienna State Opera that have become legend in the history of the art can now be experienced again anew.

In the course of the years, the number of recordings published has grown considerably. The overview of these performances from recent and not so-recent history has created a kind of musical map of the Vienna State Opera that allows the observer to recognize much of its unique tradition and musical culture.

And above all, it offers impressive proof that the Opera's unique orchestra, its greatest conductors, its unique singers and its irreplaceable ensemble are what link the State Opera of the past with the State Opera of today, giving it an incomparable position among the great opera houses of the world.

**Dominique Meyer**
Director of the Wiener Staatsoper

## Introduction from the French Press

Karajan's legendary return to the Vienna State Opera. An exciting, subtly nuanced and superbly cast mad day. This is Karajan!

The legendary return of Karajan to the Vienna Opera, an exhilarating crazy day, full of nuance and ideally distributed.

This performance from May 1977 in Vienna was an immense success, as this publication attests, fully accomplished to a degree of completion that satisfies our highest expectations. The manner in which he conducts the orchestra and singers reaches a level of refinement and balance that cannot be surpassed...

These *Figaro's were, in any case, among the most beautiful ever offered by Karajan. Herbert von Karajan, who in the past conducted *The Marriage of Figaro* with a precision close to fanaticism of the perfectionist, is today considerably far from that. He makes the orchestra the partner of the scenic action, not its commander. He imprints certain accents, dares certain variations of tempo, as if they resulted from a spontaneous inspiration, and adheres, with an infallible instinct, to the dramatic situations. Here is a colored Mozart, overflowing with life, sometimes beating speed records, celebrating, when necessary, moments of pure lyricism - upon arrival, a genuine theatre music for a dazzling piece. Karajan presents a Mozart that is one hundred percent his, not a piece from a museum. These *Noces de Figaro* were not a musical homage, but a powerful and intense moment of theatre.

*From Wiener Zeitung, Norbert Mathis - as cited by Karl Lobl in Kurier*

## Synopsis

### Act One (CD 1)

In Count Almaviva's palace his valet Figaro is busy measuring the room which his master has allotted him for after his marriage to Susanna, the Countess's maid.

Susanna is not happy with the choice of room: she fears the advances of the Count, whom she reports to have good mind to try out again with her the feudal *droit du seigneur* of the "first night" which he himself had relinquished. Figaro swears revenge on his master.

Marcellina, the elderly housekeeper, seeks legal advice from Doctor Bartolo: she once, on a promise of marriage, lent money to Figaro and would like to prevent his wedding. Bartolo knows all about legal tricks - to the delight of Marcellina. She and Susanna meet and mock each other.

The Count comes looking for Susanna, who quickly hides Cherubino the adolescent page in an armchair. Basilio, the music teacher, enters: now the Count has to conceal himself. When Basilio speaks of how Cherubino is casting soulful looks at the Countess, the Count can no longer stay hidden. When he now discovers Cherubino in Susanna's room he can scarcely contain his fury. Only the fact that Cherubino has overheard his wooing Barbarina, the daughter of the gardener Antonio, prevents him from exploding. The love-obsessed Cherubino would for preference like to declare his love to every woman in the palace. The Count names Cherubino an officer in his regiment in Seville and gives him orders to set out immediately.

### Act Two (CD 2)

The Countess deplores the infidelity of her husband. Susanna comforts her. Figaro makes clear his plan: Cherubino must, dressed in the clothes of the Countess, make pretence of receiving a lover in the park at nightfall; the Count will take him, the trick will ridicule him by his jealousy. The Countess, that this stratagem works, accepts nonetheless, out of curiosity, to be taken in by the game.

Conscious that Cherubino has just been witness to his advances toward Susanna the Count checks himself from raging. Cherubino confesses his love to the Countess - before Susanna gets the page to dress in a morning gown of the Countess to be hidden. When the Count knocks on the door, Cherubino must hide in the next room. The Count surprises the Countess, who is a little flustered, accepts however, out of curiosity, to be taken in by the game.

### Act Three (CD 3)

The Countess awaits Susanna - and laments her lost happiness. Marcellina and Bartolo present to the judge Don Curzio to pronounce a sentence against Figaro. But it refuses, precising that being a foundling, Figaro must, to be able to marry, obtain the consent of his parents. But surprisingly a few words exchanged by Susanna and Figaro, Marcellina recognizes in Figaro the son she had with Bartolo. Figaro discovers a mother and embraces, full of joy, the one he had until then feared. Barbarina and Cherubino find each other. The Count expects Susanna - and nourishes his anger against the intrigues directed at him. Susanna tries to calm him down, by now apparently willing to agree to his wishes against Figaro, yet this is done in haste. But an unexpected surprise is born: Figaro overhears this exchange and is filled with suspicion and anger.

### Act Four (CD 3)

Barbarina searches desperately in the dark for a lost pin which, on the Count's instructions, she is to give to Susanna as answer to the note she had secretly slipped him during the ceremony. Her innocent confession of remorse and her generous forgiveness resolve the confusions of the day, which now can find a festive end.

## About this Recording

This is the legendary live performance from 10 May 1977 at the Vienna State Opera, marking Herbert von Karajan's return to the Vienna State Opera pit. Karajan conducts his legendary Vienna ensemble with a cast that includes Anna Tomowa-Sintow as the Countess, Ileana Cotrubas as Susanna, Frederica von Stade as Cherubino, José van Dam as Figaro, and Tom Krause as Count Almaviva. This was one of the most celebrated *Figaro* productions of the 1970s, captured live in performance and released by Orfeo in 2012 as part of the Wiener Staatsoper Live historic series.
