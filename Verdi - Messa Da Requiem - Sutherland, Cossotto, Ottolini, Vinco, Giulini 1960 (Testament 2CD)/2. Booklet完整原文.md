(Photos: Joan Sutherland, Fiorenza Cossotto, Luigi Ottolini, Ivo Vinco)

**Photos: The Tully Potter Collection**

It was the deaths of two great Italians, Gioachino Rossini (1792-1868) and the novelist Alessandro Manzoni (1785-1873), which created the circumstance in which Verdi wrote his *Messa da Requiem*. Agnostic though he was, Verdi had never been much interested in writing sacred music but the death of Rossini in Paris in November 1868 caused the Italian nation to think that it might best commemorate the great man's passing. Rossini's home town of Pesaro proposed a performance of Cherubini's D minor *Requiem* as the centrepiece of its memorial offering. In Bologna, however, an altogether more ambitious plot was afoot. Verdi, who had been an admirer of Rossini, almost grumblingly and at a distance, proposed the creation of a composite *Requiem Mass* by a group of senior Italian composers. Though the work was completed, it was not performed, as administrators and musicians bickered over money and personnel.

Verdi's contribution to the *Messa per Rossini* was the setting of the *Libera me*. Those who read the music lavishly praised it. 'Your remarks would almost have planted in me the desire to set the Mass in its entirety at some later date,' he told his publisher Tito Ricordi. 'But don't worry. It's a temptation that will pass like many others. There are so many Requiem Masses, there is no point in adding another.'

Not even this foxy reply could disguise the fact that the idea of a complete *Messa da Requiem* was already taking hold in Verdi's imagination. All that was missing was an occasion significant enough to justify its completion. Manzoni's death on 22 May, 1873 provided just such an occasion. Verdi visited Manzoni's grave and thought the novel I promessi sposi ('The Betrothed') to be 'one of the finest of all ages'. 'Besides being a book,' he added, 'it is also a lesson of Christianity to humanity.' Verdi visited Manzoni's grave and determined to complete the *Requiem* in time for the first anniversary of his death.

Carlo Maria Giulini (1914-2005) first conducted the work in public at the 1957 Holland Festival. Later that same year he directed it in Venice with a solo team which included Fiorenza Cossotto and Ivo Vinco. Over the next four decades the *Requiem* became one of his signature works. He conducted more performances in London during that time than anywhere else (there were also performances at no fewer than three Edinburgh Festivals) but the work also became his musical visiting card in places as diverse as Paris, Berlin, Stockholm, Chicago, Los Angeles and New York. His final performances, in January 1998, took place in Turin and Paris.

Giulini twice recorded the *Requiem*, first in London in 1963 and secondly in Berlin in 1989. Despite a number of technical niggles, the 1963 London recording has long been regarded by many as hors concours. Yet even that celebrated account rather pales into comparison when set alongside the performance with which Giulini opened the Edinburgh Festival on 21 August 1960. This Edinburgh performance has an electric charge to it, a potent mix of danger and devotion which reflects dramatic precedents set by Arturo Toscanini, and Victor de Sabata in the *Requiem*, whilst marrying those insights with the work's spiritual dimension which Giulini would make peculiarly his own.

At the time of the work's first Viennese performance in 1874, the conductor Hans von Bülow described it as 'an opera in ecclesiastical robes', a slur which was more widely repeated in the Protestant north than it was in the Catholic south (including southern Germany and Austria) where the work was revered from the outset. Giulini himself was a committed Catholic. Those who worked with him believe, in a nutshell, that he could not conceive of conducting a work such as Verdi's *Requiem* without faith. He replied: 'I cannot tell you how that would be, because it is impossible for me to understand how it is possible to give life without faith. I think that inside the human body there is no "soul". I can only tell you that I have this gift of faith, I believe.'

Both as a man and as a musician Giulini was often thought to be the very embodiment of elegance, not in the superficial sense of the term (though he was that tool) but in the sense of someone to whom mannerliness, cultivated taste, and moral integrity mattered. This was true, yet as a rounded

### Page 6-7

view of Giulini the man it takes insufficient account of the force of the fires banked up within. In his 1983 autobiography *My First Forty Years* Placido Domingo wrote: 'It would be difficult to find another musician with Giulini's combination of gentleness and intensity. His music-making is extremely refined. Yet in [Verdi's] *Dies irae* he seemed to be personally God the Father on the Day of Judgment. Not that he overacted or made pompous gestures: he simply became the music to a frightening degree. It was shocking to see someone who was so good and gentle demonstrate such power.'

It is said that in the *Dies irae* Victor de Sabata would take the baton in both hands and bring it down like an executioner's axe. Giulini's baton motion in the *Dies irae* was more like a scythe. In other places the beat was unusually expressive, a skill honed in the opera house where Giulini had worked with distinction during the 1950s. We can hear this in the way in which the singing of the tenor Luigi Ottolini (1925-2002) is so beautifully nuzzed, the phrasing exquisitely moulded. An accomplished tenor but never a big 'name', Ottolini was precisely the kind of soloist with whom Giulini liked to work in the *Requiem*. The bass Ivo Vinco (b.1927) and the mezzo-soprano Fiorenza Cosotto (b.1935) were also young singers near the start of their careers. (They were in fact husband and wife. They had married in 1958.)

Cossotto, who would become the leading Italian mezzo-soprano of her generation, had made her professional debut at La Scala, Milan in 1957 in Les Dialogues des Carmelites, Francis Poulenc's opera about the martyrdom of Carmelite nuns during the French Revolution. The following year she made her international debut singing the role of Jane Seymour in Donizetti's *Anna Bolena* at the Edinburgh Festival. Hers was an astonishing voice, well knitted across a range that extended from low A to high C. It was also scrupulously used. From the start of her career, she was known for the close attention she paid to the written score – to dynamic markings in particular – and her skill in transferring these from the page to the voice. Her singing of Verdi's characteristically demanding introductory bars to the

'Lux aeterna' in this live Edinburgh performance is a fair indication of just how good she was.

Cossotto also had a particular way with the *Requiem*, the tone specially rounded and intent. This was important, since it is the mezzo-soprano who is the work's spiritual protagonist. Cossomo would almost certainly have been Giulini's choice of mezzo-soprano for his 1963 EMI recording had the not already recorded the role for the same company under Tullio Serafin four years earlier (unavailable on Testament SBT 2140). She later went on to record it with Karajan la celebrated filmed performance directed in La Scala, Milan by Henri-Georges Clouzot) and with Barbirolli.

The pairing of Cossotto and Joan Sutherland was another successful musical marriage. Sutherland had not exactly appeared from nowhere when she gave her now famous performances of the title role in *Lucia di Lammermoor* at Covent Garden in February 1959, London. audiences had known how good she was since the mid-1950s. Like Cossotto, she had sung in Poulenc's *Les Dialogues des Carmeltes*. She first worked with Giulini in the autumn of 1959 when she gave a superbly sung yet at the same time dramatically vital performance of *Donna Anna* in Giulini's now legendary EMI recording of Mozart's *Don Giovanni*. Her singing has a similar beauty and focus in this Edinburgh performance of the *Requiem*, as we can hear at her very first entry where Verdi's marking been legato, largo, pesante is keenly registered.

The Edinburgh performance was also a triumph for the Philharmonia Chorus and its director the celebrated German-born chorus-master Wilhelm Pitz. Walter Legge had founded the chorus in 1957 as the choral complement to his already prestigious Philharmonia Orchestra. It had made its international festival debut the previous September singing Handel's *Messiah* under Sir Thomas Beecham in Lucerne. Now it found itself in Edinburgh making its own distinctive contribution to a performance which one writer described as being 'as near to perfection as I ever hope to experience'. Half a century on, that's a judgement with which it would be difficult to disagree.
