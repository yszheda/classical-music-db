## Introduction

Victorien Sardou was one of the most popular playwrights in late 19th century France. Several of his dramas were made into operas, including *Madame Sans Gene* and *Fedora* by Giordano, and *La Tosca* by Puccini. The play *La Tosca* was written in 1887 for the famous actress Sarah Bernhardt, who personally urged Puccini to write music for it. The libretto adaptation he used was one originally prepared for the now forgotten composer Alberto Franchetti, who was talked out of writing it by Puccini's wily publisher, Ricordi. The premiere in Rome, on January 14, 1900, was disrupted by an anti-Puccini claque and sniffed at by critics, and indeed critical disapproval has continued to the present day. This has not in the slightest affected the public, who have made *Tosca* one of the most consistently successful operas in history. It is among a handful of the most frequently performed and enthusiastically attended operas in our time, a fact accounted for by its extraordinarily dramatic and vivid music, its effective text, its varied and interesting plot, and above all, its three sharply drawn principals: a woman of dubious morals with an inner toughness and heart of gold; a dashing and brave, yet sensitive, tenor hero; and the blackest of baritone villains whom we all love to hate. A "shoddy little shocker" it may be, as critic Joseph Kerman famously called it, but audiences have responded just as Puccini, master manipulator of emotions, knew they would.

## Synopsis

### Act One

Although the characters of *Tosca* are fictional, the locales are all real places in Rome. Angelotti, an escaped political prisoner, is hiding in a chapel of the Church of Sant' Andrea della Valle with the surreptitious help of his sister. In the course of pretending to pray every day, she has unwittingly become the model for a painting of Mary Magdalene by the artist and revolutionary Mario Cavaradossi. The singer Floria Tosca, who is in love with Cavaradossi, is unaware of the truth and vehemently jealous of this blonde woman. The chief of police, Baron Scarpia, has tracked Angelotti to the church and, finding the sister's fan on the floor, uses it to incite further jealousy in Tosca's heart in order to pry her away from Cavaradossi.

### Act Two

Angry and restless, Scarpia dines in his palace, sending a summons to Tosca to appear before him after her concert.

### Act Three

At dawn in his cell in the Castel Sant'Angelo, Cavaradossi bids a tearful farewell to life, but is soon informed that all that has happened. Tosca tells him the bullets will be blanks and that he is to "play dead" and not arise until she gives the all clear. Elated, Cavaradossi marches bravely out, even refusing a blindfold. The soldiers fire and Cavaradossi falls. The executioners leave, and Cavaradossi is brought in bleeding, just in time to hear a message that Napoleon is now winning the battle. The young idealist bursts out in a joyful cry of victory, provoking Scarpia's wrath. Horrified, Tosca realizes that Scarpia has cheated her: the bullets were real. Just then Spoletta and the police come in to arrest Tosca, having discovered Scarpia's corpse. Crying "Scarpia, we meet before God!" she foils them by leaping from a parapet to her death.

## About this Recording

This is the complete live recording of Puccini's *Tosca* from Mexico City 1 July 1952, with Maria Callas in the title role, Giuseppe Di Stefano as Cavaradossi, Piero Campolonghi as Scarpia, conducted by Guido Picco. This was one of the great early Callas collaborations with Di Stefano, one of the most celebrated operatic partnerships of the 20th century. Issued in 2000 by Opera D'Oro, this historic live performance captures the iconic Callas-Di Stefano partnership in one of their most famous roles.
