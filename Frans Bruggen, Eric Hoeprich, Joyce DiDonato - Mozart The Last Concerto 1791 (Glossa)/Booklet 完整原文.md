## Mozart: The Last Concerto, 1791
### Liner Notes by Eric Hoeprich

---

## The Last Concerto

In 1781, Mozart left Vienna for Munich, where his opera *Idomeneo* was enjoying a triumphant reception. He had just been appointed to a position in the court orchestra of Archbishop Colloredo, but his heart was in Vienna. In 1781, he broke with the Archbishop and settled permanently in Vienna, where he composed his greatest works during the last decade of his life.

During this period, Mozart developed a close friendship with Anton Stadler (1753-1812), a clarinetist and basset horn player in the court orchestra. Stadler was known for his exceptional technique and expressive playing. Mozart wrote several works specifically for Stadler, including the Clarinet Quintet K.581 (1789), the Clarinet Concerto K.622 (1791), and the two arias from *La Clemenza di Tito*: "Parto, ma tu ben mio" and "Non più di fiori."

The Clarinet Concerto in A major, K.622, was completed in October 1791, just two months before Mozart's death on December 5, 1791. It was first performed in Prague on October 16, 1791, with Stadler as the soloist. The concerto was written for Stadler's basset clarinet, an instrument with an extended lower range that allowed Mozart to explore the full chromatic capabilities of the instrument.

---

## Anton Stadler and the Basset Clarinet

Anton Stadler was born in Graz, Austria, and studied with Michael Mayrhofer. He joined the court orchestra in Vienna in 1787 and remained there until his death in 1812. Stadler was renowned for his beautiful tone and technical mastery. He and his brother Johann were both accomplished clarinetists and basset horn players.

The basset clarinet was a special instrument developed by Stadler in collaboration with the instrument maker Theodor Lotz. It extended the range of the standard clarinet by four semitones downward, allowing for the performance of Mozart's concerto in its original form. After Stadler's death, the instrument fell into obscurity, and the concerto was traditionally performed on the standard B-flat or A clarinet, with the lower passages transposed up an octave.

In 1992, musicologist Pamela Poulin discovered important documents in the Latvian State Archive in Riga that provided new information about Stadler's tour and the reception of his performances. These documents revealed that Stadler was well-received by audiences and that his innovative instrument was highly regarded.

## The Design of the Basset Clarinet

The basset clarinet posed particular challenges for instrument makers. Stadler possessed a clarinet with an extended compass, as corroborated by several sources. In his article in *Berlinische Musikalische Korrespondenz*, Bertuch described the instrument: "His instrument, which, as is usual, has straight keys from the bell... but the last quarter of its length is fitted with a transverse pipe over which the projecting bell flares out." This description suggests that the instrument's lower joint was modified to accommodate the extended range.

Stadler's basset clarinet was fitted in flat, and the basset horn was fitted with diatonic thumb keys. The basset clarinet in this recording is a modern reconstruction based on historical instruments, allowing Eric Hoeprich to perform Mozart's concerto as it was originally intended.

---

## The Last Concerto's Legacy

Mozart's Clarinet Concerto stands as one of the greatest works in the clarinet repertoire. Its three movements—Allegro, Adagio, and Rondo: Allegro—showcase the full range of the instrument's expressive capabilities. The Adagio, in particular, is renowned for its lyrical beauty and emotional depth.

The two arias from *La Clemenza di Tito*—"Parto, ma tu ben mio" and "Non più di fiori"—also feature prominent basset clarinet obbligati. These arias demonstrate the intimate connection between Mozart's operatic and instrumental writing for the clarinet.

This recording, with Eric Hoeprich on basset clarinet, Joyce DiDonato as soprano, and the Orchestra of the 18th Century conducted by Frans Brüggen, presents Mozart's last concerto as it was originally conceived, using period instruments and historically informed performance practice.

## Additional Notes from the Booklet

### On Stadler's Tour

Stadler undertook a lengthy concert tour in 1791, visiting Riga, Warsaw, Reval, Vilnius, St. Petersburg, Lübeck, Hamburg, and Hanover. Documents show that he indeed gave a solo concert on the 18th of October 1791, though it is not known for certain where he played. Stadler stopped for several days in each city on his return journey.

### On the Basset Horn

The basset horn was also an important instrument in Mozart's works. It appears in *Die Zauberflöte*, *La Clemenza di Tito*, and the *Gran Partita* K.361. Stadler and his brother Johann were both accomplished basset horn players.

### On Mozart and Stadler's Friendship

Mozart and Stadler shared a warm friendship. Mozart's letters reveal his affection for Stadler, whom he nicknamed "Stodla." The two men shared a sense of humor and a love of music that transcended their professional relationship.
