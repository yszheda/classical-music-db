## 内页序言（Piotr Kaminski 撰文）
'Made-to-measure': this was the maxim which governed Mozart - with Handel, the greatest vocal composer of his century - when fashioning arias for his singers. He would write only after having listened to the artist, 'fitting' their solos with an unerring hand. In this way his music provides us with portraits of some of the most illustrious singers of his time.

Amongst these are Dorothea and Elisabeth Wendling, the first Ilia and Elettra in Idomeneo (1781). Sisters-in-law, married to two virtuosi from the celebrated Mannheim orchestra, they moved to Munich in 1778 together with the entire court of Karl Theodor, Elector Palatine, who had commissioned the work. If we are to go by their respective roles, Dorothea was sweetness incarnate, whereas Elisabeth possessed exceptional resources. In Idomeneo, she sings one aria amoroso - 'Idol mio' in the second act - and two arias furioso, in the first and third acts. The latter, where Elettra's madness is precipitated by disappointment in love, posed serious dramatic problems for Mozart. In a letter to his father dated 3 January 1781, a month before the opening, he remarks on the incongruity of the situation in which he is placed: should he have the entire assembly - soloists and chorus - leave the stage 'for no better reason than to allow Madame Elettra to be alone?...' Notwithstanding its qualities, climaxing in lethal syncopations and staccati rising to a top C, he ended by cutting the number and replacing it with a single recitative as brief as it was stormy. Posterity is accustomed to replace the recitative which originally preceded the aria with its successor, whilst preserving the aria itself. For want of being 'original', the result is irresistible.

One of Mozart's most complex roles - that of Donna Anna in Don Giovanni (1787), a target for much psychoanalytical scrutiny - was composed for Teresa Saporiti, an uncommon artist, if only for her longevity: 1763-1869! Her two sisters were also singers; the younger, Caterina, married the Prague impresario Bondini and created the role of Zerlina in the opera. The aria in the second act, culminating in vocalising which provoked the wrath of Berlioz, gives voice to the equivocal feelings of Donna Anna for her fiancé Don Ottavio: the more she exerts herself in 'calming the anguish' of the young man, the more the agitated orchestral accompaniment betrays her distress.

Undoubtedly one of the loveliest vocal personalities with whom Mozart was associated, although he was unable to extol her virtues in opera, was Josefa Dušek (1754-1824), the wife of the composer and pianist from Prague, František Xaver. She was the dedicatee of one of the composer's first lyrical masterpieces, the concert aria Ah! lo previdi, K 272. At the time of Don Giovanni, while Mozart was staying at the Dušeks' villa on the outskirts of Prague, he favoured the singer with a heartrending farewell scene, Bella mia fiamma, on a text from Cerere placata by Jommelli. Josefa, so the story goes, locked him in his room in order to wrest from him this long-promised aria: by way of retaliation, Mozart wrote a piece bristling with pitfalls, threatening to destroy it if the singer failed to sight-read it perfectly... One presumes that Josefa proved herself equal to the task. It was also for her that Beethoven composed his only Italian aria, Ah, perfido!, in 1796.

Sisters, sisters-in-law and counterfeit sisters peopled Mozart's compositions: in Così fan tutte (1790), the roles of Fiordiligi and Dorabella, two sisters of fragile loyalty, were taken respectively by Adriana Ferraresi del Bene (at that time mistress of the librettist Lorenzo da Ponte) and Louise Villeneuve, dedicatee of three marvellous concert arias (K 578, 582 and 583). Louise, who for a long time - erroneously - passed for Adriana's real sister, seems to have captivated Mozart more than the latter, whose formidable vocal means he exploited; but also her absence of humour and naturalness, which he deplored, to create a vain and affected character whose unexpected discovery of true love destroys all artifice. Her two arias, the first heroic-comic, the second charged with contrasting emotions, underline her metamorphosis.

Adriana Ferraresi constitutes the link between Così fan tutte and The Marriage of Figaro (1786): she it was who sang Susanna in the latter's revival in 1789. For this occasion, however, Mozart replaced the celebrated Act Four aria with another, giving pre-eminence to the diva's low register. (That of Nancy Storace, who created the role, must nevertheless have been impressive: indeed, contrary to the perception of 'soprano-soubrette' which attaches to the role, she was what today would be termed a mezzo-soprano.) It is neither Susanna nor Figaro, however, who are the main protagonists of Figaro, but the Countess. She was created by Luisa Laschi, future wife of the celebrated tenor Domenico Morbelli. Contrary to the other characters in the opera, the role evolves during the course of the action: from the passive melancholy expressed by her entrance aria, through the trials of the second act, the Countess reaches full maturity in the third, her scena (accompanied recitative and aria in two movements) moving from nostalgia to resolution. She who at the beginning was no more than a submissive and terrorised wife, now draws together the strands of a plot which, as the opera ends, produces the anticipated result: the contrition of the errant husband.

Piotr Kaminski
Translation: Mari Prackauskas

## 艺术家介绍
### Barbara Frittoli
In December 2000 Barbara Frittoli made a triumphant return to the city of her birth with a series of highly successful performances at La Scala Milan as Leonora in Verdi's Il trovatore. This was one of the great moments in a career that began after she graduated with the highest honours at the Giuseppe Verdi Conservatory in Milan, where she studied with Giovanna Canetti, going on to win several international competitions.

One of the leading Mozart sopranos of her generation, she has made a number of roles her own in a series of acclaimed productions. These have included the Countess in The Marriage of Figaro at the Wiener Staatsoper (1994) and La Scala (1997), both under Riccardo Muti; Fiordiligi in Così fan tutte at the Wiener Staatsoper (1994 and 1997), the Ravenna Festival under Riccardo Muti (1997), the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden with Colin Davis (1998) and the Opéra Bastille in Paris with Sir Charles Mackerras (2000); Donna Elvira in Don Giovanni at the Wiener Staatsoper (1997 and 1998), the Opéra Bastille under James Conlon and the Salzburg Festival with Lorin Maazel (both 1999); and Sifare in Mitridate at the Teatro Regio in Turin (1994) and in Paris with Christophe Rousset (2000). She also recently appeared in Glyndebourne Festival Opera's production of Don Giovanni.

Barbara Frittoli has made outstanding appearances in the Romantic opera repertoire as well. As Desdemona in Verdi's Otello she has received some of the most glowing reviews of her career, which has included performances at the Salzburg Easter Festival (1996), the Teatro Regio in Turin under Claudio Abbado (1997), Brussels with Antonio Pappano (1994) and more recently with James Levine at the Metropolitan Opera and Zubin Mehta in Munich. Other roles for which she is widely celebrated include Alice in Verdi's Falstaff (1999, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, with Bernard Haitink), Liù in Puccini's Turandot (a 1998 tour in China with Zubin Mehta and the Maggio Musicale Fiorentino), Mimi in La Bohème, Micaëla in Carmen, Antonia in Les Contes d'Hoffmann and Marguerite in Faust.

Barbara Frittoli's extensive concert repertoire includes Handel's La Resurrezione, Verdi's Messa da Requiem (in Milan with Riccardo Muti in 2001), Brahms' Ein deutsches Requiem, Strauss' Four Last Songs, Mozart's C minor Mass K 427 (conducted by Colin Davis with the London Symphony Orchestra in 1996), Pergolesi's Stabat Mater (with Riccardo Muti at the Wiener Musikverein in 1996) and Rossini's Stabat Mater (with Riccardo Chailly and the Royal Concertgebouw Orchestra in 1997).

### Sir Charles Mackerras
Born in the USA of Australian parents in 1925, Sir Charles Mackerras studied in Sydney and Prague and made his debut as an opera conductor at Sadler's Wells. He was First Conductor of the Hamburg Opera (1966-1969) and Musical Director of both Sadler's Wells Opera (1970-1977) and Welsh National Opera (1987-1992), where his notable Janáček productions - among many others - won great acclaim. Sir Charles is Conductor Laureate of the Scottish Chamber Orchestra, Conductor Emeritus of the Welsh National Opera, Principal Guest Conductor of the San Francisco Opera and Music Director of the Orchestra of St Luke's, New York. A specialist in the Czech repertoire, Sir Charles is Principal Guest Conductor of the Czech Philharmonic Orchestra, following his life-long association with both the orchestra and many aspects of Czech musical life.

Sir Charles has undertaken much research into the performance practices of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. One of the highlights of the 1991 season was the re-opening of the Estates Theatre in Prague, scene of the original premiere of Don Giovanni, when Sir Charles conducted a new production of that opera to mark the bicentenary of Mozart's death. His large discography includes an award-winning cycle of Janáček operas with the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra, Benjamin Britten's Gloriana and Dvořák's Rusalka with the Czech Philharmonic Orchestra.

Sir Charles has for many years been associated with the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, and in addition to his many appearances with the San Francisco Opera, he conducts regularly at the Metropolitan Opera, where his recent appearances include The Makropulos Case, Katya Kabanova, The Magic Flute and Lucia di Lammermoor. In 1998 he made his debut at the Salzburg Festival, conducting highly successful performances of The Marriage of Figaro with the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra. In 2000 he returned to the Salzburg Festival for performances of Cherubini's Médée, also with the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra.

Sir Charles received a CBE in 1974 and was knighted for his services to music in 1979. In 1996 he received The Medal of Merit from the Czech Republic and in 1997 he was made a Companion of the Order of Australia. He has been awarded honorary doctorates by the universities of Hull, York, Nottingham, Brno, Griffith in Brisbane, and Oxford. Sir Charles recently celebrated his seventy-fifth birthday with the BBC Symphony Orchestra, Welsh National Opera and the San Francisco Opera.
