## Liner Notes by Robert Sherman

### Page 4-5

**Nadia Reisenberg** • **Erick Friedman**
piano | violin

**In Performance & Conversation**
**Beethoven • Brahms • Strauss**

Although Mother (a.k.a. Nadia Reisenberg) made her early mark as a recitalist and concerto soloist, by the time I was old enough to know what was going on, she had contentedly settled into her other two great loves: teaching (at Curtis College, Mannes and later Juilliard), and collaborative performances. I remember glorious sonata recitals with violist Milton Katims and violinist Mischa Mischakoff, both principals in the NBC Symphony back then, and with Simeon Bellison and Joseph Schuster, first clarinetist and cellist, respectively, of The New York Philharmonic.

Mother's first major recording was a Columbia 78 rpm album of the Brahms E-Flat Sonata for clarinetist Benny Goodman, and among her LP recordings for Westminster were both Brahms sonatas with the violist Paul Doktor. Every year on the anniversary broadcasts of my WQXR Listening Room series, Mother came to perform with such esteemed artists as violinist Oscar Shumsky, cellist Leonard Rose, pianist Rudolf Firkušný, and the Juilliard Quartet, and I remember with particular delight a program in 1975 with my Mannes colleagues David Glazer, William Kroll and Claus Adam. Unfortunately, the Budapest Quartet was disbanded by the time my broadcasts began in 1970, but I vividly remember the many earlier rehearsals in my living room, one of which I recorded for my favorite listening space under the piano – what a pleasure it was." Mother recalled on another radio occasion, "How beautifully we (the members of the Budapest) blended together, how wonderfully the phrases came out when each player gladly gave way to another's voice. It was his time to shine. I know what she meant, because starting in the mid-1940's, I was her designated page-turner.

I don't recall how Mother and the violinist Erick Friedman got together, but their meeting in 1977 changed both of their lives. She was 73, and had been gradually easing away from concert appearances; Erick was not yet 40, and at the peak of his soloistic powers. His energy and excitement somehow galvanized Mother, even as her musical wisdom helped soften and shape his character playing. Their first performance together was at the 92nd Street Y concert, documented on this album, launching a professional and personal friendship that continued until Mother's death six years later. They had short tours, and most notably, celebrated at Mother's 75th anniversary year (1979) with recitals at Caramoor and Carnegie Hall.

---

### Page 6-7

"We've had a wonderful association," Mother told me in 1979, when I was preparing a article for The New York Times about the Caramoor program. "Erick is an especially flexible artist. We love trading ideas at rehearsal, and he takes precisely the same joy in chamber music that I do."

Erick's comments came at a memorial concert for Mother in 1983: "I was so blessed to perform with such an artist," he said. "It was more meaningful than I can really say. It was an inspiration. Their relationship transcended pianism, and certainly violinism: her musical knowledge of so many forms was breathtaking. It made each rehearsal an event – indeed I couldn't wait until the next one. Nadia had that rare quality given to so few, whereby her inspired musicianship became enlightened performance. She loved music, and she loved people, and she loved to play for them – and they knew it. It was an honor for me to know and be close to her, and I cherish her memory. For me, that did not pass away, but gone forward into musical history." That, sadly, is true now for Erick as well: he died in March of 2004 at the age of 65.

The three pieces on the Y program took up what I (as an unrepentant LP lover) considered the first three sides of an album. I thought of filling that fourth side with excerpts from the Caramoor concert, but there was a steady rain that day, and the sound quality of the recording suffered accordingly. That's when I realized that just a couple of weeks before the Y recital, Erick and Mother had come on my Listening Room show, talking about how they had so quickly formed a deep musical partnership, and in the course of events, playing two movements of the Strauss Sonata and one of the Brahms. I decided to spare you the news bulletins, weather reports, and commercials, but otherwise the program is unedited, reproduced here precisely as it aired on WQXR on the morning of September 27, 1977.

One final, and sad footnote. Towards the end of the broadcast conversation, Erick remarked that following the Y concert, Musical Heritage was going to record all three of the sonatas. I don't know what went wrong or why, but the project never actually came to be. Ironically, and even more sadly, the same thing happened with the Carnegie Hall program two years later: we didn't record the sonatas (Franck, Grieg and Strauss) in the hall, because they were scheduled to get studio tapings shortly afterwards. But for reasons once more buried in the mists of time, the sessions were canceled. The present album then, along with two movements of the Franck from another Listening Room broadcast (released on "Music In And On the Air," Roméo Records #7286) remain the only commercially available samplings of Erick's and Mother's superb duo performances.

~ Robert Sherman

### Page 8-9

**~ Erick Friedman ~**

A child prodigy, Erick Friedman studied with Ivan Galamian at the Juilliard School and made his New York debut at age 14. Three years later, he began coaching with Jascha Heifetz, going on to record Bach's Double Violin Concerto with his renowned mentor.

Among Friedman's later recordings were concertos with the Chicago, Boston, and London Symphonies, as well as two Grammy-nominated albums, the Franck and Debussy Sonatas, and the Bach Sonatas with keyboard. In 1996, he won the Grammy for Best Historical Album, for his participation in the Heifetz Collection. Another notable recognition came in 2000 when he received the Paderewski Award for Distinguished Contributions to Society and Culture.

A serious auto accident derailed his violin performances for several years, during which time he became conductor and music director of the Garrett Lakes Festival in Maryland, and in 1989, joined the faculty of Yale University's School of Music. Friedman resumed his concert career in 1992, touring in Europe, South America and the Far East, and appearing as a regular guest artist at the Norfolk, Round Top, Newport and other major music festivals. He died of cancer on March 30, 2004.

---

**~ Nadia Reisenberg ~**

Born in Vilna, Russia (now Vilnius, Lithuania) on July 14, 1904, Nadia Reisenberg studied at the St. Petersburg Conservatory with Leonid Nikolayev, in the U.S. with Alexander Lambert (himself a student of Liszt) and Josef Hofmann. She later became Hofmann's teaching assistant at Curtis.

---

### Page 10-11

In 1939, she began her historic weekly series of nationwide broadcasts devoted to all 27 Mozart Piano Concertos (with Alfred Wallenstein conducting), and in the 1940's, Reisenberg became the only pianist to have been engaged twice in the same season by the New York Philharmonic. Her first (78 rpm) recording was of the Brahms Clarinet Sonata No. 2 with clarinetist Benny Goodman, and most of her distinguished solo LPs for Westminster have been reissued on CD by Roméo, Ivory Classics and Bridge (which also released for the first time her much acclaimed 1947 Carnegie Hall recital).

Nadia Reisenberg celebrated her 75th birthday playing recitals with violinist Erick Friedman, both in Carnegie Hall and at the Caramoor Festival, and the next year joined members of the Juilliard Quartet for chamber music of Mendelssohn, Rachmaninoff and Fauré for a memorable concert issued in 2012 on a Roméo label. While on the faculty of Juilliard, Mannes and Queens College, she passed away peacefully at her home on June 10, 1983, a Liszt Étude open on the piano.
