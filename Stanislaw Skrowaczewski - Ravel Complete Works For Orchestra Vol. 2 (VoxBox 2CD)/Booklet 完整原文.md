## Une Barque sur L'Océan • Alborada del gracioso (both from "Miroirs")

*"If music can describe an object without first revealing the artist, then Ravel's Miroirs virtually better this ideal."* — André Suarès

In 1905 Ravel wrote his first extended works for piano (having composed only brief individual pieces up to that time). The *Sonatine* and a suite of five pieces he called *Miroirs*, the latter work, as Roland-Manuel observes, "Ravel made of it a completely new instrument. The Mirror (real is a treasure no less dear because it is a plant. The rhythmical solidity is broken up. The range of modulation is extended. The harmony, especially, is enriched thereby; it is thrown into high relief by a single line of color. The same notes clearly related, ... 'Difficult as they are to play, these pieces at first seem to be equally difficult to understand,... Even today numerous are uninitiated rather than charmed by their many mirrors, only keepers in their repertory the Alborada del gracioso, in which the dry and twinkling virtuosity is contrasted, Spanish-wise, with the swooning flow of the love-lorn melodic line which interrupts the angry buzzing of guitars.' It is an altogether admirable piece of work, and doubly successful in the brilliantly orchestrated version."

Ravel orchestrated one other piece from the *Miroirs* in 1906, the year after the piano suite was completed; this was the third piece in the suite, *Une Barque sur l'océan*, dedicated to the painter Paul Sordes. (One can understand the composer's early interest in setting this piece for orchestra, for as Stuckenschmidt remarks, it "contains perfumelike directions that point to orchestral models. Harp and wind sonorities are evoked by every means at the disposal of the composer. The keyboard is treated in a way that is well-tested in the most modern chemistry of sound.") Although the orchestral version was performed at a Colonne concert under the composer on February 1, 1907, Ravel decided not to publish it, and it is only in the last forty years or so that it has begun to be performed again. Its delicate and subtle loveliness provides a stunning contrast to the brilliant and voluptuous *Alborada*.

**ABBEY SIMON** wrote the *previously Numberless composition* in 1948, and he says: "I've been running ever since." Running to Simon means performing between 40 and 100 concerts each year, and making recordings. In his 1988 cover note, New York Times critic Harold Schonberg referred to Simon as a "super virtuoso." He also mentioned the fact that when Abbey Simon gives a recital, the audience is full of professionals trying to figure out how he does those incredible things with a hand, why he gets the kind of color he does, and what he does with the rhythms that make them so flexible.

The **MINNESOTA ORCHESTRA's** long tradition of musical eminence reaches back to its founding in 1903 as the Minneapolis Symphony, eighth major symphony orchestra in America. A succession of great conductors and years of exceptional artistry have placed the Orchestra in its present position as one of America's foremost ensembles. The men who have shaped the Orchestra in its development are found music directors **Eugene Ormandy** (1931-36), **Dimitri Mitropoulos** (1937-49), and **Antal Doráti** (1949-60). Maestro Stanislaw Skrowaczewski was music director from 1960 to 1985, a 25-year period of important growth and achievement for the Orchestra.

## Ravel and His Concertos

Both of Ravel's piano concertos were written at the same time, begun in 1930 and completed in the fall of 1931. Except for the *Concerto for the Left Hand*, which follows the very year, this project was a curious assignment for a firm score offered to several composers simultaneously and ultimately awarded to Jacques Ibert, they were his last completed works of any form. The Concerto in G was begun first, but the *Concerto for the left hand* was finished and performed first. At age 56, the composer, who until then had been a man of leisure, now found himself in a new role.

"It was an unusual experience," Ravel spoke of them in an interview with a correspondent for the *Daily Telegraph* of London:

"I was an inexperienced exponent of the two forms and had to study their mechanism thoroughly. For the first time I propose to play myself, a concerto in the strict sense, written in the spirit of Mozart and Saint-Saëns. I believe in the value of tradition and without having been previously brought up or aiming to do so, I have acquired a liking for it. I consider that a concerto can be gay and brilliant without being profound or aiming at dramatic effects. It has been said that the music of the great masters is a form of prayer for me. But the piano has been written against it. And I think that this criterion is quite justified."

"In the beginning I meant it to be entitled *Divertissement*, but I realized that this would not have been unnecessary, as the name *Concerto* adequately describes the kind of music it contains. In some ways my *Concerto in G* differs from my earlier ones in being more free from jazz, but only in some ways."

"The *Concerto for left hand* alone is quite different, and has only one movement. It is in the nature of an introduction followed by a slow movement and finale, which are linked by episodes that serve as bridges between the two. At first, it is of several such works commissioned from various composers by the Viennese pianist Paul Wittgenstein, who lost his right arm in World War I. (Among the others were concertos by Erich Wolfgang Korngold, Paul Hindemith, Richard Strauss, and Sergei Prokofiev, whose *Concerto No. 4* was completed in 1931 but not performed until 1956, Wittgenstein gave the premier performances of the D major in Vienna on November 28, 1931). In his uniquely authoritative little book on Ravel, Alexis Roland-Manuel (1891-1966), who was his pupil, friend and confidant, and eventually became his literary executor and the author of an authorized biography, wrote the following about the two concertos:

"Nothing shatters the impression of unity that dominates the complete dissimilarity of these two works conceived and written at the same time; the disparity between their styles corresponds to differences of intention. As an interpreter of the *Concerto in D* had only one hand, his composer is not content simply to make this adaptation by a transcription; he creates the occasion to create the concentrated richness of the *Concerto in G* major with the tragic solemnity of the *Concerto for the left hand*."

"This is one of Ravel's most masterly works, and certainly the most beautiful he had written since the war. Apart from its value as a *tour de force*, this great composition strangely transcends its object, as if manipulating, accumulating, transforming and outraging nature, demanded of the artist that unknown power which bears one of his last songs."

The Ravel had death of this concerto gave it the force of an incantation. In order to weave new spells it transplanted exotic materials and distant echoes of *Une Barque sur l'océan* and *La Valse*, and of the Hispano arabesques. The orchestra has quite a new sound, alternately heavy and gloomy, and the piano emerges at times as a dazzling solo instrument, at times as part of a somber whole. The writing is alternately supple and opposed by the magical left hand whose waves the most extraordinary arabesques on the piano."

"In its breadth and perfect form, the *Concerto in D major* is a complete whole. The strict unity of the composition only makes the nervous character of the work more obvious, flooding the right-world with scintillating images that sparkle and fade in the feverish atmosphere of a dream world." (Quotations translated by Cynthia Jolly. Published in paperback by Publications, Inc., through arrangement with Dobrows, Ltd., London.)

## Ravel and the Orchestra

No one extends into any time with the same needs the orchestra of a century as a reminder of the genius of Ravel, who was consummate in his own terms both as orchestrator and as creator of orchestral works, only grows with the passing of time. His music continues to enjoy both popularity and respect, which is so many composers can boast in any generation.

Ravel is especially revered as a master of orchestral writing, though his output was by no means large. He preferred the polish and joy-point of a few carefully finished productions to a casual (many) and very little of what he wrote was conceived originally in orchestral terms. In addition to the three concertos, Ravel wrote for orchestra in the (two concertos for piano, in the short opera *L'Heure espagnole* and *L'Enfant et les sortilèges*, in a ballet and in symphonic poems). Ravel also made many effective orchestrations/transcriptions of piano music by other composers.

One of his earliest efforts was to orchestrate two works, the concert overture *Shéhérazade* (for an unknown source, untitled to the song-cycle) which he composed in 1898 and suppressed after a single experimental performance the following year. The first composition he completed and published as an orchestral work was *Pavane pour une Infante défunte* (1899), originally a piano piece that he orchestrated in 1910, and an earlier piece for piano solos. ## Ma Mère L'Oye

*"Mother Goose" — Complete ballet (Ravel)*

*"Because of it atmosphere of happiness, tender emotion, and refined poetry, it is a work whose charm is equal to all the children of all ages."* — André Suarès

"Ravel was never more at his ease," says Roland-Manuel, "than in the children's kingdom of Charles Perrault, Marie d'Aulnoy and Leprince de Beaumont. By virtue of a privilege which he shared with the greatest creative artist, the composer never lost, as his obstinate determination to acquire technical mastery that fresh sensibility of ear, the privilege of childhood and is eternally true to it." Ravel retained almost intact that freedom of imagination and artless power which in the adult generally gives way to the tyranny of formalist fashion. Having arrived at that time of life when the capacities of youth delight in exercise, when power and knowledge compete in the privilege of childish games, Ravel was still a child.

To appreciate the child in Ravel, to discover the secret of his creative personality, one must look into the past, where one finds the key to his genius, as well as to his limitations. Ravel was a child of the 18th century, and his music reflects the grace and elegance of that era.

Cyprian ("Cip") and Ida Godebski, close friends of Ravel's, had two young children when he adopted the piano-duet version of *Ma Mère L'Oye* in 1910. Cyprian, a Polish writer and pianist who had settled in Paris in 1892, and his wife Ida, were part of the artistic circle that included Ravel, Debussy, and many others. The five pieces that make up the original piano suite were composed between 1908 and 1910, and were dedicated to the Godebski children, Jean ("Jean") and Mimi ("Mimi").

The original piano-duet version of *Ma Mère L'Oye* comprises five sections: *Pavane de la Belle au bois dormant* ("Pavane of the Sleeping Beauty"), *Petit Poucet* ("Hop-o'-My-Thumb"), *Laideronnette, Impératrice des Pagodes* ("Laideronnette, Empress of the Pagodas"), *Les Entretiens de la Belle et de la Bête* ("Conversations of Beauty and the Beast"), and *Le Jardin féerique* ("The Fairy Garden"). While the suite sequence is frequently performed as written, Ravel later expanded it into a full ballet by adding three movements: *Prélude*, *Danse du rouet et scène*, and *Apothéose*.

One may also gather from the titles, the ballet is based on the Sleeping Beauty story, with other "Mother Goose" favorites interwoven. One of the titles, however, stands by itself: *Laideronnette, Impératrice des Pagodes*.

## La Valse, poème chorégraphique

*"I feel that this work is a kind of apotheosis of the Viennese waltz, linked, in my mind, with the impression of a fantastic and fateful whirl of life."* — Ravel

*La Valse* is one of the few works Ravel conceived in orchestral terms from the beginning; in this case a choreographic transcription prepared as staging for a ballet was an afterthought. That fact has kept it from becoming as widely performed as the orchestral version. (Abbey Simon has recorded the piano version in the course of the integral survey of Ravel's music for Vox; it is a virtuoso showpiece, but that has nothing of this "apotheosis of the Viennese waltz" even before he composed the *Rapsodie espagnole* in 1907, and, indeed, a fragment of *La Valse* appeared as early as 1911, as the penultimate section of the ballet *Adélaïde ou le langage des fleurs*, staged at the Théâtre du Châtelet on December 19, 1920. The Royal Flemish Ballet gave the first danced performance of the work on October 2, 1928, in Antwerp; and Ida Rubinstein presented it in Paris several times, using a different choreography and sets on each occasion; a choreographic version by George Balanchine was introduced by the New York City Ballet in 1951 and it is that company's current repertory.)

Ravel's original title for *La Valse* was simply *Wien*, the German name for Vienna. At one time he spoke of "a piece in the style of the earlier Strauss, not Richard," and it may be noted that one of the themes in *La Valse* is quite similar to one in Johann Strauss Jr.'s *Tales from the Vienna Woods*. Since that is one of the Strauss family's best-known pieces (based on themes from the operetta *Prinz Methusalem*), the similarity may have been unintentional; in any event, there are other resemblances as well, as Roland-Manuel noted: "Echoes of waltzes and more obviously Johann Strauss resound throughout the score and the rhythmic movement of (Chabrier's) *Le Roi malgré lui* and its *Fête polonaise*. But in the most successful parts, reminiscentness of the Valences of *Gaspard de la nuit*, of *Ma Mère L'Oye*, and even of *Daphnis et Chloé*."

It has been suggested that the diversity of influences reflected here persuaded Ravel that *Wien* was too delimiting a title. Commentators with a historico-sociological orientation have conjectured that by the time Ravel finished this composition he simply felt that the original concept had been altered by his postwar disillusionment, and perhaps "destiny's fantastic whirl" had made too grim changes in Wien's character to retain its specific title. (Ravel was acutely aware of them, for his objection to a stage version of *La Valse* in 1929 was based on the choreographer's having disregarded his specific time and place indicated in the preface to the score.)

"Movement of an ascending spiral, rising and falling, but without purpose. The images may be seen. One by one the clouds disperse and we see an immense room filled with a whirling crowd. As the movement becomes clearer we see more than one illumination, until the light of the chandeliers bursts forth. An Imperial Court, about 1855..."

The second suite, which was not performed until after the premiere of the ballet, is also in three sections — *Lever du jour*, *Pantomime*, *Danse générale* — corresponding to the entire "third movement" of Ravel's choreographic symphony, which takes place after the rescue of Chloe. The score of the Suite No. 2 bears a prefatory note which, in the now-standard translation of Philip Hale, has become almost as familiar as the music itself:

"No sound; but the murmur of rivulets fed by the dew trickles from the rocks. Daphnis lies stretched before the grotto of the nymphs. Little by little the day dawns. The songs of birds are heard. ... Herdsman enter, scouring the plain. ... Daphnis, awakening, stretches and rises. He perceives Chloe stretched out near him. ... They embrace. ... Nymphs appear and encircle Daphnis and Chloe. ... Daphnis and Chloe come toward us. He, the intrepid youth, falls on his knees before Chloe. ... Two old men, their hair crowned with ivy, enter solemnly. ... The music grows more agitated. ... Daphnis and Chloe unite in a passionate embrace. ... The curtain falls."

*Daphnis et Chloé* must rate among the finest of ballet scores. The music is Ravel's largest and most ambitious work, yet it never loses its clarity and refinement. The orchestration is sumptuous without ever becoming heavy, and the melodic invention is constant. The work requires a large orchestra, including saxophone, celesta, and wind machine, as well as a mixed chorus that is used wordlessly to create atmospheric effects.

## Le Tombeau de Couperin

*"Choose a music; minute him. If you have nothing to say, all you can say to it is to say. If you have something to say, you will not have to say it."* — Ravel

François Couperin (le Grand; 1668-1733) is revered as the embodiment of the clarity, grace and refinement which distinguish the musical tradition characterized by Roland-Manuel as "the true French line." When Ravel began Le Tombeau de Couperin in 1914, he could scarcely have foreseen that the conflict would be so terrible that tradition in a suite of pieces in forms used in Couperin's time — "a tribute," as he remarked, "not to Couperin himself but to the spirit of French music that Couperin represents."
