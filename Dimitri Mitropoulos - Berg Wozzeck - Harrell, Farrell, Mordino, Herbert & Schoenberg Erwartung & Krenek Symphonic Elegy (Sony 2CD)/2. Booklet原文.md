## 专辑介绍
```
WOZZECK

From the start, Wozzeck was a cause—to Berg, maintaining contact throughout the Great War with an opera he started in 1914 and finished in 1921; to the composer's friends and his publisher, who campaigned to have it staged; to Erich Kleiber who with infinite patience prepared it in Berlin, revived it seven years later and gave it its London stage premiere; to believers who worked to re-launch it after the Second World War and the Nazi ban; and not least to eminent conductors who took a risk with it in the U.S.A.

Stokowski started that in 1931 in Philadelphia and then New York, and Mitropoulos conducted it in concert, first in Minneapolis, and then with the New York Philharmonic in 1951 (the performance preserved on this recording). Was it a cause with him too? I'm tempted to think so, and cannot help wondering whether he had worked on the score already in Berlin, where he was on the music staff of the Berliner Staatsoper until 1924, at precisely the time Kleiber was securing the work for performance. The premiere was announced in January 1924.

Because of its nature, haphazard, ill-prepared performances of Wozzeck are rare. My own first acquaintance with the opera came in 1949 with a concert performance under Adrian Boult: It was meticulously rehearsed, strongly cast with Suzanne Danco as an extraordinary Marie, and it made an overwhelming impression. The 1951-52 season was the annus mirabilis for me as far as Wozzeck is concerned. In the space of 12 months, I heard performances in three different languages: one conducted by Karl Böhm in Salzburg, another by Erich Kleiber in London, and a third by Mitropoulos in Milan. Kleiber, whose commitment to Wozzeck at that point dated back nearly 30 years, maintained that his job was to make the opera appear as natural to the other performers and to the audience as it was to him. Mitropoulos must have felt rather similarly. By 1952, his American concert performances were behind him, La Scala's orchestra was the third with whom he had rehearsed the score, and he had a strong cast headed by Tito Gobbi (tackling the title role for the third time) and including Hugues Cuénod, who sang the Captain without any vestige of vocal caricature. The whole performance reminded one of Berg's comment, "It needs singing as if it were Trovatore."

Wozzeck is still not easy to perform, though it is a lot easier today than when Mitropoulos conducted it at Carnegie Hall in 1951, only a quarter of a century after its Berlin premiere, but now nearly half a century ago. Berg provided an annotated scheme by the way of sound-bite analysis, but insisted nobody listening to the work should be tempted to perform a mental analysis during performance. Would he additionally have accepted that the spirit behind an approach to the notes was more important than mere accuracy? My instinct says he would and, if I am right, then he would have been enthusiastic for what Mitropoulos achieved in New York, accuracy in any performance being only half the story. The degree to which performers have understood the work and can pass this on to the audience—this is a more telling ingredient. It is the conviction evident in this recording that makes it much more than a souvenir of a great occasion.

There is quite a lot of approximation in the notes, particularly in passages of Sprechstimme—that invention of Schoenberg's for Pierrot Lunaire, loosely described as "musically defined speech," or, more explicitly: "Rhythm and intonation are exactly prescribed but in execution each note is only defined in the moment when it is articulated; immediately afterwards the voice drops or rises as in natural speech." Rhythms in the Carnegie Hall performance were plainly rehearsed until right, but of the whole cast only Mack Harrell takes the notated pitch as more than a rough guide.

Harrell is not only accurate but sings beautifully, with character and warmth of tone; his is a performance by which others may be judged. Eileen Farrell has a God-given voice, more splendid than most who have tackled the role of Marie—she deserves credit for doing so—and it is easy to forgive a rather cavalier approach to pitch for the real beauty of her singing. John Mordino catches the near hysteria of the Captain to perfection.

Everything in this performance is made to sound easy—because a work is difficult to perform does not mean it should sound that way—and in such great moments as the D-minor threnody before the last scene, Mitropoulos makes his point unerringly, with the sort of control that creates tension rather than rigidity. It is a shame that Mitropoulos never conducted Wozzeck at the Metropolitan Opera; Böhm conducted the Met premiere, and Mitropoulos was dead before it was revived.

This recording is a landmark: Not only does it enshrine a performance that re-launched this crucial score in the United States, but it also became a benchmark for later versions of Wozzeck. (The first studio recording did not come until fourteen years later.) Especially considering that there are no native German-speakers in the cast, and that even the New York Philharmonic was new to the score, it is a truly remarkable achievement.

The Earl of Harewood
(Lord Harewood is the former artistic director of the Edinburgh Festival and former managing director of English National Opera.)
```

```

SYNOPSIS

Johann Franz Wozzeck, "thirty years and seven months old, militiaman and fusilier in the second regiment, second battalion, fourth company, uneducated, uncomprehending," is the regimental servant of the Captain of his outfit. To earn a little for the support of Marie and the child she has had by him he has placed himself at the disposal of the Doctor, who laughs at him to his face but uses him for the purposes of scientific experiment, demonstration to medical and psychology students.

### ACT I
(Scene 1) Wozzeck is seen shaving his talkative Captain in the barracks. The Captain is speculating on the passage of time—now it is an eternity, he muses, now only a moment, and yet he reflects with a shudder that the earth turns on its axis once every twenty-four hours. To his superior's meditations the orderly's reply is a laconic "Jawohl, Herr Hauptmann!" ("Yes, Captain!") The Captain is amused by his servant's simplicity and tells Wozzeck that he has no morals. Wozzeck at length answers: "Herr Hauptmann, the good God will not ask my poor little brat whether the Amen was said over it before it was made....We poor people! Money, Money! What it is to have none! If I were a gentleman and had a watch and a hat and could speak properly I would be virtuous, all right, but our kind is unlucky in this world and the next. No doubt if we got to heaven we would have to lend a hand with the thunder!" The Captain feels that Wozzeck is not a bad sort but that he "thinks too much," which is a mistake.

(Scene 2) In a field at sunset Wozzeck is seen with Andres, another soldier, who is cutting sticks. Wozzeck is haunted by a fear of the unknown and reflects on the uncanny nature of the place; he sees strange lights and imagines a head moving along the ground by night and, when someone picks it up, turning into a hedgehog. The stolid Andres in reply sings a simple hunting song. But Wozzeck's fancies grow wilder, he mumbles something about Freemasons, imagines the ground opening beneath his feet and, in the end, unnerves Andres by wildly imploring him to fly this haunted spot.

(Scene 3) Marie is shown in her room, playing with the child. A regiment passes under the window, a band led by the Drum Major at the head. He waves to Marie, who returns the greeting. Margret, a neighbor, comments maliciously on Marie's apparent interest in soldiers, till the two women fall to berating each other. Marie noisily slams her window, takes the child in her arms, and sings it to sleep. She is soon aroused from her thoughts by a knock on the window. Wozzeck is outside and tells that he is due at the barracks and cannot come to her now. His confused talk worries her and she fears that something sinister impends. She holds out the child to him; he does not even look at it and leaves hurriedly. Marie puts the sleeping boy back into his crib, and moans: "Ah, we poor people" and "I cannot much longer endure it."

(Scene 4) The Doctor's study the next day. The physician is busy with his unfeeling experiments, says that Wozzeck is a victim of mental aberrations, and outlines a new schedule of nourishment for him, in conformity with the science of dietetics which he hopes to revolutionize. Wozzeck rebels at first, then grows more and more confused and incoherent. The Doctor warns that, with his fixed ideas, he is shaping for the madhouse, and, delighted with the poor fellow's interesting case, rubs his hands and exclaims: "Oh my theory, my fame! I shall be immortal! immortal! immortal!"

(Scene 5) A street in front of Marie's house. At evening Marie stands admiring the Drum Major who proudly postures and assures her that he is handsomer still on Sunday parade, wearing his white gloves and his great plumes. Unspeakably pleased that he has even condescended to notice her, Marie flings herself into his arms and the two disappear into the house.

### ACT II
(Scene 1) Marie preens herself before her mirror. She is wearing earrings. Wozzeck enters and is at once surprised to see her instinctively put her hands to her ears. To his question where the earrings came from she replies that she found them. Despite his obvious suspicions he turns to the sleeping child, whose forehead is moist. "Wir arme Leut!" he laments; "nothing under the sun but work, and even in our sleep we sweat!" He gives Marie his earnings and leaves her musing on her wickedness.

(Scene 2) The Captain and the Doctor meet in the street. The latter is in a hurry but the Captain detains him for a moment, whereupon the physician terrifies his friend by brutally calling his attention to his flushed, unhealthy appearance and warning him of a probably paralyzing, if not fatal apoplexy. Wozzeck enters and the two torment him with their innuendos about Marie's fidelity. When he frees himself from his tormentors the Doctor looks after him with the reflection: "A phenomenon, this Wozzeck."

(Scene 3) Meeting Marie in front of her house Wozzeck talks confusedly about "sin" and asks if "he" stood there, where he stands now. Marie says that many come and go through the street and that she "cannot forbid it to anyone." Then she fears that Wozzeck is about to strike her. "Better a knife in me than a hand on me!" she cries; "when I was ten years old my father did not dare that." She enters her house while Wozzeck murmurs to himself: "Better a knife! My head is swimming!"

(Scene 4) A beer garden crowded with soldiers, many of them drunk. Suddenly Wozzeck catches sight of Marie dancing a waltz with the Drum Major; maddened by jealousy he is about to rush at them when the dance stops. Andres and others begin a lusty song, and someone begins a mock sermon. More dancing, then a Fool approaches the moody Wozzeck, says he "smells blood," and starts Wozzeck's wild fancies afresh.

(Scene 5) At night in the barracks. Wozzeck tosses restlessly on his cot. Snoring soldiers surround him and his moaning awakens Andres. Visions of a knife flash before him. The Drum Major enters, tells of his conquests, makes insulting remarks, and, suddenly turning upon Wozzeck, hurls himself upon him. Infuriated, the latter fights back and is beaten bloody. The aroused soldiers, after a cynical remark or two, go back to sleep.

### ACT III
(Scene 1) In her room Marie reads by candlelight the biblical story of Mary Magdalene and laments her own frailty. After turning to the child she takes up her Bible once more, then cries: "Savior, have pity on me as Thou hadst pity on her!"

(Scene 2) A pond in the wood. Wozzeck appears with Marie. Although she wishes to return home he forces her to sit down and assures her "her feet will not hurt her much longer." It is growing dark. He reflects on how long they have known each other. Presently madness again overcomes him. The moon rises and Marie mentions its redness, which evokes in Wozzeck the idea of blood. He draws his knife, plunges it into her throat, stoops over her, and exclaims: "dead!" Then he rushes away.

(Scene 3) At a tavern Wozzeck struggles desperately to forget his deed. He dances with Margret and for a moment even makes love to her. She sees blood on his hands and her shrieks attract the other dancers. After explaining irrationally that he had cut himself, he dashes out with the cry: "Am I a murderer?"

(Scene 4) He comes back to the pond in search of the knife, afraid it may lead to his detection. He throws it into the pond, catches sight, in the moonlight, of the Doctor and the Captain, walks into the water to recover the blade, loses his footing and drowns. The pair hear a groan, are unnerved, and hurry away.

(Scene 5) In the street before Marie's house the child is playing with other children. One of them shouts to him that his mother is dead. The youngsters rush off to see the body. Marie's child does not understand, continues playing with his hobby horse, riding it faster and faster, with cries of "Hopp! Hopp!" Then, after a moment, he too follows, still calling "Hopp!"

(from original LP notes, SL-118, released 1951)

## 勋伯格与克热内克作品介绍
```
ARNOLD SCHOENBERG
ERWARTUNG

Erwartung, Schoenberg's one-act monodrama, is based on a poem by Richard Dehmel, the very poet whose work had earlier inspired a song by the same name as well as one of Schoenberg's greatest chamber works, Verklärte Nacht. Marie Pappenheim's libretto presents one unnamed female character, aptly called "Woman," who goes into a dimly lit forest to keep a tryst with her lover. The action, as far as it can be surmised, unfolds in four scenes.

Scene I: As the woman gropes down a roadway, the natural world around her takes on frightening shapes in the penumbra. Though she is afraid, she resolves to enter the woods to find her lover.

Scene II: Venturing deeper into the forest, though still terrified, she stumbles against a shape she mistakes for a body. It is only a tree trunk.

Scene III: The woman imagines hearing and seeing things that further feed her fears. Now in a state that hovers somewhere between waking and dreaming, that teeters between rationality and irrationality, the woman goes deeper into the forest.

Scene IV: This scene constitutes more than half of the opera's length. Here, too, the woman's emotions fluctuate, between dread and desire, anxiety and serenity. As she drags herself to a clump of trees to rest, her foot strikes against something. It is the body of her lover, murdered, she imagines, by the rival she has alluded to throughout the opera. By turns wild with grief, tender, nostalgic, insanely jealous, and incredulous, the woman sinks down. As dawn breaks, she rises abruptly, distractedly mumbling as she walks away, "I was looking for..."

Richard Haney-Jardine

ERNST KRENEK
SYMPHONIC ELEGY

On September 15, 1945, while taking an evening walk in Mittersill, Austria, the composer Anton Webern was mistakenly shot dead by an American soldier. Some time later, when the news finally reached him, composer Ernst Krenek, sharing the shock and sorrow felt world-wide by the music community, sat down to compose his Symphonic Elegy, Op. 105, which bears the subtitle "In Memoriam Anton Webern."

Krenek's earlier musical style ranged from dissonant and atonal works to music that he himself characterized as "tonal... with the condiments of jazz." Early on he had reacted against the extreme modernism epitomized by the twelve-tone music of Schoenberg and his renowned disciples (notably Webern), claiming that their music willfully irritated and alienated audiences. Indeed, with a series of disparaging remarks about the movement, Krenek had cast himself defiantly outside Schoenberg's milieu. However, from around the 1930s on, Krenek gradually turned to twelve-tone music in a move that to many appeared paradoxical. By the time Webern programmed a Krenek piece for radio broadcast in 1930, Krenek's compositions were in fact increasingly viewed as in the same league. That broadcast marked the beginning of a close association between the two composers. Soon the names of Webern and Krenek were uttered in the same breath, and Webern's influence could be felt in the younger composer's thought and music.

Not surprisingly, the short, single-movement Elegy, scored for string orchestra, lovingly embraces Webern's aesthetic, especially in its treatment of the twelve-tone row: a disciple's moving and elegant tribute to a beloved mentor.

Richard Haney-Jardine

WOZZECK

Die Geschichte des Wozzeck ist reich an Menschen, denen die Durchsetzung dieser Oper ein persönliches Anliegen war. Nachdem Alban Berg das Werk über die Jahre des Ersten Weltkriegs zwischen 1914 und 1921 komponiert hatte, mußten seine Freunde und sein Verlag erst einige Kämpfe ausfechten, ehe Wozzeck aus der Taufe gehoben werden konnte. Der Dirigent der Uraufführung war Erich Kleiber. Er zeigte bei der Einstudierung eine grenzenlose Geduld und dirigierte auch sieben Jahre später die erste Londoner Bühnenaufführung. Weitere Fürsprecher waren jene Anhänger Bergs, die die Oper nach dem Zweiten Weltkrieg und dem Aufführungsverbot durch die Nationalsozialisten neu etablierten, und nicht zuletzt die bedeutenden amerikanischen Dirigenten, die die ersten Aufführungen in den USA leiteten – was damals ein durchaus riskantes Unterfangen war.

Zunächst dirigierte Leopold Stokowski die Oper 1931 in Philadelphia und später in New York. Dimitri Mitropoulos leitete konzertante Aufführungen – erst in Minneapolis und 1951 dann ebenfalls in New York mit den New Yorker Philharmonikern (letztere Aufführung ist auf der vorliegenden CD festgehalten). Ich denke, daß auch Mitropoulos sich diesem Werk besonders verbunden fühlte. Möglicherweise beschäftigte er sich mit der Partitur bereits in Berlin, wo er bis 1924 als Korrepetitor an der Staatsoper arbeitete – zu jener Zeit also, als Kleiber sich entschloß, das Werk auf die Bühne zu bringen. Die Uraufführung wurde für den Januar 1924 angesetzt.

Es liegt in der Natur der Sache, daß man dieser Oper meist in durchdachten und sorgfältig vorbereiteten Aufführungen begegnet. Ich selbst lernte Wozzeck 1949 in einer konzertanten Aufführung unter Adrian Boult kennen, der eine außerordentlich intensive Probenarbeit vorausgegangen war. Der Gesamteindruck war überwältigend, nicht zuletzt wegen des hervorragenden Ensembles mit einer beeindrucken Suzanne Danco als Marie. Vor allem die Saison 1951/52 war für mich reich an Wozzeck-Erlebnissen. Innerhalb von zwölf Monaten hörte ich Aufführungen in drei verschiedenen Sprachen: eine mit Karl Böhm in Salzburg, eine mit Erich Kleiber in London und eine mit Dimitri Mitropoulos in Mailand. Kleiber, der sich damals seit fast dreißig Jahren mit dem Wozzeck beschäftigte, wollte vor allem erreichen, daß die Oper den Ausführenden wie dem Publikum so ungekünstelt erschien wie ihm selbst. Mitropoulos verfolgte offenbar ähnliche Ziele. 1952 kannte er das Stück durch seine konzertanten Aufführungen in den USA in allen Einzelheiten. Das Orchester der Mailänder Scala war bereits das dritte, mit dem er die Partitur erarbeitete. Außerdem verfügte er über ein Ensemble ersten Ranges, angeführt von Tito Gobbi (der die Titelpartie zum dritten Mal in Angriff nahm) und mit Hugues Cuénod, der den Hauptmann ohne jede karrikatureske Überzeichnung sang. Die ganze Aufführung ließ an jene Bemerkung Bergs denken, wonach die Oper wie der Troubadour zu singen sei.

Wozzeck ist bis heute nicht leicht aufzuführen. 1951 jedoch, als Mitropoulos das Stück in der Carnegie Hall dirigierte, waren die Schwierigkeiten noch ungleich größer – schließlich waren seit der Berliner Uraufführung erst zweieinhalb Jahrzehnte vergangen. Berg hat seiner Oper ein Schema zur Unterteilung in klangliche Einheiten beigefügt. Er war jedoch dagegen, daß man sich eine Aufführung entsprechend analytisch anhörte. Es fragt sich, ob er darüber hinaus wohl der Ansicht zugestimmt hätte, daß es wichtiger ist, einen Notentext in seinem Gehalt zu erfassen, als ihn bloß penibel umzusetzen. Ich bin in der Tat zuversichtlich, daß Berg diese Meinung geteilt hätte. In diesem Fall wäre er ohne Zweifel von Mitropoulos' New Yorker Aufführung begeistert gewesen, in der sich einmal mehr zeigte, daß Genauigkeit nur die eine Seite einer Interpretation sein kann. Viel entscheidender war hier, daß die Musiker das Werk ganz durchdrungen hatten und in der Lage waren, dem Publikum ihre Einsichten zu vermitteln. All dies wird auch in dem vorliegenden Mitschnitt deutlich, der somit weit mehr ist als die Erinnerung an einen großen Abend.
