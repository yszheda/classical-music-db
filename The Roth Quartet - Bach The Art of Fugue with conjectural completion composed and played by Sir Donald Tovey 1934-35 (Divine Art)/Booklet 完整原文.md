### The Art of Fugue - The Enigmatic Masterpiece

This short essay, it must be made clear at the outset, will not in any detailed way expound or examine the structure or form of The Art of Fugue, as any technical account, that is, the reader is likely to find many and useful books and articles of interest. For now, let us be content with some general comments. As we now know it, the Art (the title was not given by Bach, but possibly by his publisher or his son-in-law) consists of sixteen movements in its most complete form, as follows:

- 4 simple fugues (two regular-subject and two inverted-subject) each for four voices;
- 3 counter-fugues each for four voices;
- 4 compound fugues (two double fugues for four voices, one treble for three voices and one triple for four voices);
- 4 canons for two voices;
- 3 mirror fugues (one for three voices and two for four voices)
- 1 (incomplete) quadruple fugue.

Each section employs the same basic and quite simple subject (a "head" theme). Bach therefore faced the problem of monotony, and whilst to the casual listener the music can still be "hard work", interest (and fascination) is maintained by the constant transformation of the subject in quite subtle ways, both rhythmic and melodic. But even so, it is a most remarkable work - perhaps unique - for as a work as a whole its rhythmic unity is definitely an uphill gradient of simpler forms leading to ever-more complex ones, in something like 24 or so evolutions.

Some editions (including the first printed score and current publications) include the four canons together; this was the decision of the composer's son C.P.E. Bach, who supervised the publication of the work, but evidently contrary to his father's intentions. Bach's working papers suggest a more sophisticated order, with the canons acting as "dividers" between the groups of fugues, reflecting their increasing complexity; thus, the simple fugues are followed by a simple canon at the octave; the counter-fugues by a canon at the tenth; the double fugues by a canon at the twelfth; and the mirror fugues by a "canon in augmentation and contrary motion".

The final movement, intended to be an immense quadruple fugue in four voices - it starts out at bar 239 in the middle of what should be the third section. While we return to this subject later, we may add here that many commentators believe the original concept called for four examples in each set, rounded off by the great quadruple fugue which would encapsulate and summarise all of the foregoing. It also apparently there are pieces missing - it should also be mentioned that the first printed score of 1751 also includes a mirror fugue of the opening piece, thus forming both a perfect quadruple fugue, and a masterful summation of the whole work.

## 内页 4 (HIPPO_20210829_0004.jpg 右侧)

The Art of Fugue is a work that has given rise to endless speculation and argument. Some have seen it as a purely intellectual exercise, others as a deeply spiritual statement. The truth probably lies somewhere in between. What is certain is that it represents the culmination of Bach's lifelong exploration of counterpoint, and stands as a testament to his unparalleled mastery of the art.

1. There are no tempo markings or performance indications, the music being placed on the page in its absolute form.

2. There is (or has been) substantial controversy over the intended instrumentation, even over questions of form. Some have suggested that the original, partial edition issued by Bach himself was intended as a sort of musical notebook, though this is disputed. However, the music was written in open score, with each voice on a separate stave. This was not at all unusual in Bach's time, when composers and copyists were accustomed to writing in open score, and has nothing whatever to do with questions of instrumentation. It is true that the title page of the first printed score of 1751 also includes a mirror fugue of the opening piece, thus forming both a perfect quadruple fugue, and a masterful summation of the whole work.

3. The work as a whole is an easy to listen to its moving innocence and beautiful and ordered perfection: a joy to appreciate. There is no concession to "hardness" such as may be found within even some of Bach's other more difficult works, including both The Well-Tempered Clavier and Mass in B minor. The music takes the listener to its limits of emotional conviction and vigour. (On the other hand it is not suggested that the above, the work does not provide the listener with a stimulating experience, indeed it is quite quite absorbing).

4. There are no tempo markings or performance indications, the music being placed on the page in its absolute form.

5. Bach may have composed the piece for one academy or another. He had been accepted as a member of the Correspondierende Societat der musicalischen Wissenschaften (Societe des musikwissenschaftlichen Korrespondenten) in June of 1746 or 1747 (depending on one's source of information). He had been founded in 1738 by Bach's pupil and close friend Johann Adolph Scheibe, and whose membership (by invitation) included Telemann and Society's members (which included Telemann and Handel - Mozart joined later) was obliged each year to submit a scientific paper on an aspect of music. Bach submitted his Kunst der Fuge in 1747/8, and it is believed that he intended to submit The Art of Fugue in 1749 but deteriorating health prevented him from in time. It is further speculated that his intention may have been to surpass the demonstration of the philosophiae naturalis principia mathematica of Sir Isaac Newton, and thus offer a work on musical theory, if, as was a long task of devotion for Bach, as he probably had started work on the composition in 1738, or even earlier.

6. Bach's music has been the subject of countless books, articles and essays over the years. Nowadays, it seems that everyone is searching for esoteric arcane and hidden meanings and symbolism in the music. In recent decades an entire subculture has sprung up, which has adopted an almost universal trend to see numerology and mysticism in almost everything - numbers, star-signs, extra-terrestrial encounters, secret societies, hidden codes, ancient civilisations, sacred geometry, and so on. It is a trend that is, to be sure, not only absurd, but also highly offensive. By far, the most important of these are those who believe that Bach's music is encoded with all kinds of hidden messages. Much of this has been attributed to the work of Albert Schweitzer, who, in his famous study of Bach, suggested that the composer was a mystic who encoded his religious beliefs in his music. This theory has been thoroughly discredited by modern scholarship, but it continues to have a certain appeal for those who wish to see Bach as something more than a mere craftsman.

Bach died on 28 July 1750. His death was caused by complications following eye surgery performed by the English oculist John Taylor (the same surgeon who operated on Handel). Bach had been suffering from diabetes and high blood pressure, and the surgery proved unsuccessful. He died in Leipzig, and was buried in the cemetery of St. John's Church. His remains were later moved to the Thomaskirche, where they rest today.

### Sir Donald Francis Tovey

Sir Donald Francis Tovey was born at Eton on 17 July 1875. His father had been a master at Eton College and a Church minister. He very quickly displayed a skilled piano and composition, with a special interest in harmony and counterpoint. He entered Balliol College, Oxford, from where he graduated in 1898 with an honours degree in Philosophy and Music. He achieved early fame as a brilliant pianist, composer and concert organiser. He met and corresponded with many of the major musicians of his day, by whom he was held in high regard. From 1901-1902 he gave a series of concerts in London, analysing several of Beethoven's works, especially on his work on Beethoven symphonies in particular. It was here that Tovey, as before, made particular reference to his philosophies of music; for example he wrote that the "innocent ear", which is possessed by children, is the only organ capable of appreciating music in its purest form, and that all musical training should be directed towards the preservation of this innocence.

Tovey's analyses are now thought by many academics to be faultless by his ever-fondness for Germanic romanticism, and by the deep depth of his grasp of the philosophy of music as a whole work. The sheer number of his researches and their conclusions is staggering. It is no exaggeration to say that he was before the acceptance of his views. For example, Peter Sternberg, pianist and music scholar, finds Tovey's analyses and comments wrong many times.

### Roy Harris

Roy Harris is perhaps America's most acclaimed symphonist. He was born in a log cabin on 12 February 1898, near Chandler, Oklahoma. One of the most important figures in American music of the first half of the 20th century. Harris was largely self-taught as a composer, studying briefly with Arthur Farwell in Los Angeles and being appointed as the director of the music division of the Los Angeles Public Library. He studied at the University of California, Berkeley, and later in Paris with Nadia Boulanger (1926-1929). He taught at Mills College, Juilliard, and UCLA. He died in 1979.

Harris is perhaps best known for his Third Symphony (1939), which was premiered by the Boston Symphony Orchestra under Serge Koussevitzky. He wrote thirteen symphonies in all, as well as numerous chamber works, songs, and piano pieces. His music is characterised by its use of American folk melodies and irregular rhythms. He is also known for his pedagogical works, including Counterpoint and Harmony.

### Technical note by Andrew Rose:

"This recording of The Art of Fugue presented a couple of specific problems for the restorer. The overall sound on these American-recorded discs was quite 'boomy' and unnatural, a big contrast to some London-recorded quarters of the 1930s (the quartet was working on at the same time.) However, the latter did give me a few pointers for how best to go about the sound of this recording, and with the careful use of specially tailored filters I was able to create a far more natural overall timbre. The other big challenge in remastering this recording came from two of the side changes. Most of the tracks on this CD originally came from a single 12" LP set, but where they overlap I had to contend with the quartet's tendency to slow down just past the half-way point of the first side. Of course this isn't so bad if you're listening on 78, side at a time, but if you're hoping to join side changes and produce music more continuous, a number of digital tricks have to be employed to minimise noise and speed up tempo. Whilst this can never substitute for a recording made at the proper speed, I hope that the joins I've produced will be undetectable under normal listening conditions, and thus not detract from the enjoyment of the music."

The original recording was made by Columbia Records (USA), apart from the final side (Tovey's piano piece), which was recorded in London. The set was issued in the UK in a limited edition of 1000 copies, on 12" Columbia discs with a special cream and brown label. It was not accompanied by a concise but highly detailed analysis by Tovey. It was issued in February 1936 and remained in the catalogue for exactly 19 years.

### Feri Roth

Feri Roth (1891-1969) was born on 18 July 1891 at Gyongyos. He graduated in 1909 from the Hungarian Academy of Music in Budapest in 1917. Founding the Roth Quartet in 1920, he led the group until his death in 1969. He was a soloist and set up an ensemble with former members of the Hungarian Quartet. In 1947 he spent a long summer at Tanglewood teaching chamber music as a lecturer, and becoming Professor in 1950. During his years at UCLA the quartet sponsored chamber music concerts for hundreds of local ensembles and engaged in all types of music from Bach to contemporary music. His extensive collection of string quartet parts (some 10,000 volumes) and Beethoven attracted thousands of students. Roth received the Award of Merit from the National Association of Schools of Music. He was decorated by Queen Juliana of Holland for outstanding service to American music and was awarded an honorary doctorate from the University of San Francisco. He died in Los Angeles on 7 May 1969.

### Jeno Antal

On this recording, the second violinist is **Jeno Antal**, one of the many talented musicians who played with the quartet for a relatively short period of time at the same time as **Ferenc Molnar** (viola) who is not to be confused with the great Hungarian playwright and journalist.

### Janos Scholz

Janos Scholz (1903-1970) was a renowned cellist and collector who amassed an enormous collection of photographs, donated in batches between 1978 and 1993 to the Fogg Art Museum of Harvard University. The collection is still famous and exhibited in museums and galleries throughout the USA. He also set up a substantial collection of cello music, left to the University of North Carolina where he died in 1990. His music collection also includes pictures for viola da gamba, violas, keyboard and harmonium.
