## Sir Charles Mackerras Biography Page

**Sir Charles Mackerras**
PHOTO DECCA / MARY ROBERTS

Eighteenth-century music had been a passion for Charles Mackerras ever since childhood. 'What I particularly like is the beautiful symmetry of it,' he said in 1977, 'and the extremely florid decoration which you find in the Baroque art of central Europe. That's why I'm so interested in making the music of the eighteenth century florid, because it all ties up with the general artistic impulses of the period. I've tried hard not only to be a scholar and a musicologist but to try and interpret old music in the old way, in the way that the composers imagined it, but also trying to put my expertise and knowledge of conducting music of all eras into what I do. I passionately believe in trying to recreate the sound, the actual noise that is made by an orchestra of Baroque dimensions. The right sound is a little raw – and it is true that I am particularly interested in the music of Janáček, whose sound is also a little raw.'

Mackerras was speaking shortly before his departure from the musical directorship of English National Opera. He had held the post for eight years, during which time he had continued to revolutionise wider understanding of both the areas of music discussed above, with both first London productions of Janáček rarities such as *The Makropoulos Case* and *From the House of the Dead*, as well as landmark productions of Handel, Gluck and Mozart at both of London's opera houses.

This album marked the first (and so far only) occasion on which the London Symphony Orchestra, 'the world's most recorded orchestra', had addressed itself to the music of Jean-Philippe Rameau. The sessions took place over four days in January 1965, produced by the Italian musicologist and conductor Vittorio Negri, who himself made extensive contributions to the Philips catalogue with comprehensive recordings of Vivaldi's sacred music. The fruits of those sessions – at least the suites of Gluck and Rameau – were released without much fanfare by Philips in the autumn of 1965. So little fanfare, in fact, that the first reference to the LP in the pages of Gramophone comes not in the reviews section or any new-release advertisement placed by Philips but in a round-up of 'titles from current stocks' at London's legendary Gramophone Exchange, known to all as Gramex, and then operating from premises from Wardour Street ("Don't miss the Collector's items at startlingly low prices in 'Dead Man's Corner"). Neither did this highly unusual album – of rare and appealing repertoire, played by one of the world's acknowledged great orchestras and conducted by an expert in the field – attract much attention elsewhere.

The Wembley Town Hall sessions did not end there. Mackerras and the LSO also recorded two overtures by Johann Strauss the younger, to *Die Fledermaus* and *Der Zigeunerbaron*, which achieved limited circulation as part of compilations of popular overtures. These have now been reissued by Eloquence within a complementary Mackerras collection which focuses on the 'Kaleidoscope' album of orchestral favourites he had recorded with the LSO in July 1961. Two further overtures, by Cherubini and Cimarosa, were also taped at the same January 1965 sessions, but until now have never been released.

The critical neglect of Rameau may seem surprising to us now, but even twenty years into the period-instrument revolution he lagged far behind Bach and Handel – as to a lesser extent he still does, though he is worthy of comparison with them. Mackerras was doing more than anyone else in England to bring his name, and his harmonically rich and original idiom, to a new audience. In November 1964 he had conducted a complete studio performance of Rameau's *Castor et Pollux*, for a BBC Third Programme broadcast. He had produced a new edition of the score in conjunction with Basil Lam, and *Opera* magazine took due note: 'It demonstrated beyond question both Rameau's genius and his historical importance, and raised the question why we never encounter him in the theatre.'

For the broadcast Mackerras worked with the English Chamber Orchestra, a crack ensemble of London freelancers who were evidently contoured as a period band for the occasion, replete with recorders and a continuo section of viola da gamba, harpsichord and chamber organ. The Wembley sessions afforded no such historical luxuries, yet the results on record hardly sound their age, with Mackerras streamlining the strings, bringing the woodwinds forward and most crucially coaxing the musicians into a style of ornamentation roughly approximating what Rameau might have known and without which his music quickly dies on the wing, fatally plain or ponderous.

During the last couple of decades, adventurous stage productions of *Castor et Pollux* have established it in the front rank of Rameau's tragédies lyriques. The third of his operas after *Hippolyte et Ancie* and *Les Indes galantes*, he composed it for the Paris Opéra, where it was first produced in October 1737. Audiences initially compared it unfavourably with its predecessors, and perhaps they were taken aback by a great deal of the score's paradoxical extravagance and austerity, for much of the music within it is coloured by a fierce, sometimes harshly lit intensity. Yet by the time it was revived for the second time in 1764, shortly before the composer's death, Castor had come to be seen as his crowning glory.

The story concerns the Dioscuri twins of the title, one mortal – Castor – the other immortal, and how Pollux resolves to take the place of his lately dead brother in Hades despite the imprecations of their father Jupiter. Their story is entwined with the fates of their lovers Telaira and Phoebe, with Phoebe eventually killing herself at the tragedy's appalling climax. The suite gathers dances from throughout the opera, reaching a powerful climax with the severe grandeur of the "Air des Démons, immediately followed by the airy sublimity of the 'Air pour le Soleil et les Heures' – a conceit of high contrasts typical of Rameau – and concluding, as the opera itself does, with a magnificent Chaconne.

With the Suite from Gluck's *Orfeo ed Euridice* on Side B of the Philips LP, audiences at least were on more familiar ground, if not the LSO (another first recording of his music). Or should we say *Orphée et Euridice*? Like Rameau, Gluck had begun his musical career in the teeth of parental opposition. Born in the Upper Palatinate region of Austria, he run away to Prague to escape following in his father's footsteps as a forester, and quickly earned his way as a self-made musician of parts. Posts in Vienna, London and across Europe established his name among the foremost musical dramatists of his time. In 1761 he was introduced to Ranieri de' Calzabigi, a true Renaissance man: poet, librettist, businessman and man of theatre who proposed an artistic collaboration on the perennially popular subject of Orpheus.

Written and sung as *Orfeo ed Euridice*, the work was a great success at its first performance, staged at the Burgtheater in Vienna in 1762 with the celebrated castrato Guadagni singing Orpheus. Two years later the score was published in Paris, but the conventions of the Opéra – local language, more dancing – demanded that for a first performance in Paris, Gluck should thoroughly recast the opera. Following the triumph of *Iphigénie en Aulide* in 1774, Gluck quickly revised *Orfeo ed Euridice* and made a French version of the text with a translation by Pierre-Louis Moline. He also adapted the role of Orpheus for the haute-contre Joseph Legros. Gluck dedicated *Orphée* to Marie Antoinette, who rewarded him with an annual pension of 6000 livres. But Vienna was competing for Gluck's talents, and when he returned home later that year he became a court composer there with a salary of 2000 gulden.

The Overture, the Pantomime at Eurydice's grave, and the first part of the Dance of the Blessed Spirits belong to the Vienna score of 1762, while the rest of the suite recorded here was added in the Paris version. This was the portmanteau practice also followed by Mackerras in his edition of the complete opera, which he recorded for Vanguard for 1969. In fact other, older conductors also used his edition in performance; among them Georg Solti for a new production by the Royal Opera in the same year. Critics noticed the contrast between the double-dotted rhythms of Mackerras's edition replete with appoggiaturas and ornate cadenzas, and Solti's driven lyric style, essentially moulded by central-European performing traditions from earlier in the twentieth century. However, Solti at least showed himself open to a lively awareness of historically appropriate performance innovations. The following clerihew was submitted to *Opera* magazine by one 'A. Jay' – harsh but fair, perhaps:

*To make a performance secure,*
*Sprinkle well with appoggiatura.*
*Though Davis and Solti*
*Declare it is faulty;*
*Mackerras is sure it is purer.*

Peter Quantrill
