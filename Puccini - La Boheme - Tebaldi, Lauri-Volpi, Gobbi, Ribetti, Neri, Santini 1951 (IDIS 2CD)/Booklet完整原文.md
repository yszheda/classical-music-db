# Article: Puccini - La Bohème

## Alessandro Trovato

**IDIS 6437/8**
**PUCCINI. La Bohème**

*"La Bohème will not leave any trace in the history of opera":* thus predicted the reviewer of *La Stampa* after the work's premiere, which took place to a mild response on 1st February 1896 with twenty-nine-year-old Arturo Toscanini on the podium. That hasty assessment, however, would soon be proven wrong, as audiences around the world became more and more enthusiastic, silencing the critics' arrogance. *La Bohème*, in fact, was the opera that gave Puccini definite international renown, confirming the success the composer from Lucca had already achieved three years previously, with *Manon Lescaut*.

Puccini worked at *Bohème*, on and off, for more than three years. For a long time the project of writing this opera, based on a subject taken from French novelist Henri Murger's *Scènes de la vie de bohème*, progressed side by side with that of another work, *La Lupa*, based on a novel by Giovanni Verga; then the latter was abandoned and Puccini was free to dedicate himself entirely to *Bohème*. Giuseppe Giacosa, who had drafted the final libretto of *Manon*, now was plagued with two problems from the start: in the final libretto of Bohème, the journalist got into frequent disagreements with Puccini, this caused some friction and disagreements between them; the difficulties were, however, always overcome thanks to the mediation of the editor Giulio Ricordi, who, with his guidance and pacifying attitude, was crucial to the clearing up of many problems Puccini completed the score on 10th December 1895. Even after the Turin premiere, however, the fussy composer urged himself further modifications, while he himself introduced changes to the score.

While he was working at *Bohème*, Puccini was also involved in an unpleasant dispute with Ruggero Leoncavallo, who also began an opera based on Murger's novel and who claimed priority rights on the subject. That controversy, however, did not end in victory for any one side: both operas were premiered within a year of one another, in 1845 and 1848 respectively, and both found success with the public, with Puccini's work ultimately achieving greater popularity. The characters described by Henri Murger (1822-1869) later decided to develop them into a five-act drama (*La vie de bohème*); in 1849 this met with just as much success after serialization in the magazine *Le Corsaire* between 1845 and 1849. Puccini, who had based his opera on Murger's novel and in this way it ended up beating his rival, can rightly be praised for such subtlety and refinement in characterizing the characters, with such subtlety and refinement in the varying emotions of Mimi and Rodolfo, as the young penniless writer and the frail seamstress.
