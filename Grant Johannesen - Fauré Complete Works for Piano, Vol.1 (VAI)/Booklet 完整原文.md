### Notes by Robert W. Trumble, Mus. Bac.

**Eight "Pièces Brèves," Op. 84 (1898-1902)** — When first published, the "Eight Short Pieces" had no titles, the ones now associated with the separate items being added later, possibly by the publisher. The titles do, however, convey a fair enough indication of the character of each piece. No. 8 is a Nocturne and is sometimes known separately as "Eighth Nocturne." The others are as follows:

1. **Capriccio** (1:36) — quite simple in style and not at all complex in its technicalities.
2. **Fantaisie** (1:41) — a short and pleasant "Pièce d'occasion."
3. **Fugue** (2:17) — of interest, for Fauré did not often write in the stricter forms.
4. **Adagietto** (3:04) — a piece of considerable beauty, more serious in style than the preceding.
5. **Improvisation** (1:38) — originally designed as a sight-reading test for Fauré's classes at the Conservatory, but of infinitely greater musical value than the usual run of such pieces.
6. **Fugue** (1:53) — another attractive and highly effective excursion into the field of more academic writing.
7. **Allégresse** (1:06) — a piece filled with expressive gaiety and exhilaration.
8. **Nocturne** (2:16) — deserves to rank with the others of the great series of nocturnes. Of interest to students is the rapid and effective transition from the opening key of D flat to the Phrygian mode of C.

**Nine Preludes, Op. 103 (1910-1911)** — The Nine Preludes have been compared, in the scheme of Fauré's art, to the place of the famous 24 Preludes in that of Chopin — short, concentrated masterpieces displaying, within the limited perimeter of each, a world of color and imagination and also a many-varied and ingeniously contrasted to pianoforte technique.

1. An ardent melody (2:22)
2. A rapid moto perpetuo (1:32)
3. In the barcarolle style (2:55)
4. Pastoral in character (1:56)
5. More forceful in nature (1:57)
6. Kokowski style (1:57)
7. A quiet, although quite intense opening leading to an effractive climax (2:29)
8. A study in repeated notes in scherzo style (1:02)
9. Perhaps the most expressive and deepest of the preludes. The change to the major mode at bar 8, simple enough in itself, is unforgetable, as is also the descending sequence leading to the final cadence. (2:26)

**Valse Caprice No. 1 in A major, Op. 30 (1883)** — Although the Valse Caprices have been regarded by students of Fauré as being a relatively unimportant portion of his musical output, as one writer has stated, from the more superficial Valses Brillantes of Chopin they are, nonetheless, bright, animated, and full of the play of sunlight. Alfred Cortot once remarked that it has never been imagined, before Fauré wrote them, that such music, whose worldly nature is never glossed over, could have retained so much sensuous charm, distinction and imagination.

The first of the Valse Caprices, with its sweep of melody and harmonic interest, captures one's imagination and makes an effective gateway to a series full of tenderness and considerable individuality.

**Barcarolle No. 3 in G-flat major, Op. 42 (1885)** — Here there is some resemblance in character to the well-known Barcarolle of Chopin. The pianist Alfred Cortot, an ardent admirer of Fauré's art, has noted its strong feeling of introspection, and the technical excellence of its writing.

**Nocturne No. 3 in A-flat major, Op. 33, No. 3 (1883)** — Here, the opening bars proclaim a mood similar to that of the second Nocturne. There is, however, a more sustained feeling of tenderness, a truer interpretation of the original scheme and purpose of the nocturne form — of music written to portray the charm and magic of the twilight and the soft pervading atmosphere of night.

**Barcarolle No. 9 in A minor, Op. 101 (1909)** — Gone now is the earlier approach to the Barcarolle form — so readily redolent of the *Venetianische Gondellieder* and of Fauré's own happy visits to the Adriatic. There is here to be found a nostalgic remembrance of things past. There is, in the ninth Barcarolle, a noticeable absence of the chromatic manner of his earlier writing. The writing is spare, there is in fact mainly diatonic and gives the impression of economy to the point of barrenness.

**Impromptu No. 1 in E-flat major, Op. 25 (1883)** — As in Opus 17 (Three Songs Without Words), the first impromptu possesses a certain rhythmic monotony which is quite characteristic. Here, there is no slavish imitation of Chopin's individual treatment — already in the E-flat Impromptu it is easy to catch a glimpse of the curious and almost sensual approach to the keyboard coloring — an approach that was to become stronger and more individual as Fauré approached maturity.

**Impromptu No. 2 in F minor, Op. 31 (1883)** — This brilliant and exhilarating work, in the rhythm of a tarantella, is a highly characteristic. In ternary form, its expressive middle section is marked a *mouvt mouvement* with a descending chords, giving an effect of great beauty and elegance, and one which stays long in the mind of the sensitive listener.

**Impromptu No. 3 in A-flat major, Op. 34 (1883)** — One of the finest examples of Fauré's work in this form, a composition of grace and proportion in which Fauré, the craftsman, reveals his impeccable taste and skill.

**Impromptu No. 4 in D-flat major, Op. 91 (1906)** — Dedicated to the pianist Marguerite Long. Here again the form is ternary, but with a departure from Fauré's normal procedure in that the middle section is marked "andante" as against the allegro tempo of the first and last.

**Impromptu No. 5 in F-sharp minor, Op. 102 (1909)** — Alfred Cortot once compared the prevailing mood of this remarkable work to the "pulsating hallucinations rare in Fauré's work, of which the schema is the same as that of the Valse Caprice in its two possible examples." The use of the whole-tone scale, a favorite device at one time of his compatriot Debussy, is worthy of notice. It was Fauré's only experiment with this style, and it evoked harsh criticism from the staunch classicist Saint-Saëns.

### About the Artist

Among the important American pianists and pedagogues of our time, **GRANT JOHANNESEN** is in a class by himself. Renowned for his interpretations of the French piano repertoire, he caught the attention of the great French pianist Robert Casadesus at the age of sixteen. At the latter's urging he went to Paris, where he studied at Princeton and the American Foundation at Fontainebleau. Piano studies followed with Edon Petri and, composition courses with Nadia Boulanger and Roger Sessions. He made his New York recital début in 1944 and his debut with the New York Philharmonic in 1949. He was awarded London's coveted Harriet Cohen International Award for outstanding artistry in 1957. In 1978 he was decorated Chevalier dans l'Ordre des Palmes Académiques and is also an honor of Chevalier des Arts et Lettres by the French government. Johannesen has also distinguished himself in the field of pedagogy. He taught at the Aspen Music School from 1956 to 1961, and served on the faculty of the Cincinnati Conservatory of Music from 1961 to 1978, eight of those years serving as the institute's president. He also founded the Casadesus International Piano Competition and has served on the panel of the National Endowment for the Arts.

As a recording artist, Johannesen has given special attention to works which few pianists have explored, especially music by French and American composers. The present set, recorded in 1961, was the first recording of the complete piano works of Gabriel Fauré which has now been reissued through the courtesy of French Heritage, Ltd. Johannesen's huge discography also includes concertos by Saint-Saëns and Milhaud, solo works by virtually every major French composer of the last 100 years, music by American composers, works for two pianos by American composers, selected works of Beethoven, Chopin, and the Schubert B-Flat Sonata. Of his recordings, *The New York Times* has written, "the Johannesen achievement is to play these works with what might be described as a French touch to his playing that gives the listener a fine feeling of security regardless of the musical style or technical difficulty involved. Mr. Johannesen reveals through his apt performances why these pieces should be played more frequently than they are."

This is the first time that Grant Johannesen's recordings have ever been re-issued on the VAI label. VAI is proud to bring these definitive interpretations back into circulation. They are a testament both to the artist and the music he has championed.
