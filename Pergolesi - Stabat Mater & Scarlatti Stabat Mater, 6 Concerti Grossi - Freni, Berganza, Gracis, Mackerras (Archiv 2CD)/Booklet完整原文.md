# BETWEEN TRADITION AND PROGRESS
## Music by Alessandro Scarlatti and Giovanni Battista Pergolesi

Alessandro Scarlatti was born in Palermo in 1660. Several of his brothers and sisters were musicians, and three of his sons - Pietro, Domenico and Giuseppe - made a name for themselves as composers. Alessandro arrived in Rome at a very early age; in 1679 he made an extremely successful debut there as an opera composer, and became musical director to Queen Christina of Sweden. In 1684 he took up the same post at the viceregal court in Naples, and during the next two decades he composed more than 80 operas, thus laying the foundations of the city's reputation as "la capitale du monde musicien" in the 18th century. In 1702 he left his post, and from then onwards he spent most of his time in Rome. Together with Arcangelo Corelli, he was admitted to the Arcadian Academy there, which was devoted to the cultivation of literature, the arts and sciences. He remained its official composer and musical adviser until his death. He continued to train himself in musical theory and counterpoint, and wrote a cappella works alla Palestrina. In 1703 he was appointed as sistant maestro di cappella at the church of Santa Maria Maggiore, becoming maestro there four years later. In 1708 Cardinal Grimani, the new Viceroy (who also engaged Handel) appointed him to the same position at the court in Naples again. But by this time Scarlatti was not turning out a whole series of works for everyday musical needs; instead, and this reflected a new feeling of artistic responsibility, he tended to give each of his compositions its own special character. He died in 1725 in Naples.

"VI Concertos in Seven Parts for two Violins and Violoncello Obligate with two Violins more a Tenor and Thorough Bass, Compos'd by Sigr Alexander Scarlatti" is the title of the works recorded here in the extremely rare edition which was printed in London by Benjamin Cooke, a publisher and music dealer of New Street, near Covent Garden. The date of publication is unknown, but it was probably around 1740 or shortly after that. This date is suggested by a manuscript score of the 6 Concerti grossi in the British Museum. This score bears the following message written by Cooke on the last page: "Dear B. Jonny, these compositions are by none other than Alessandro Scarlatti, and I desire you to tell no one that things of the greatest worth are concerned. London, 18.10.1740."

It is difficult to establish what the original scoring of the 6 Concerti for strings may have been. Was this primarily a case of a "quartet" for soloists or of an orchestral score in four parts? In the manuscript of the famous Santini collection the 4 Concertos in minor keys (Nos. 1, 2, 4 and 5) are brought together under the title "Sonate a quattro", the term "Concerti grossi" applied to them by Cooke thus appearing in the light of a concession to the preference of audiences for concertos with solo parts. The Third Concerto contains a solo for the first violin, while the Second and Sixth both feature a genuine concertino group in the concluding dance.

Burney was fully aware of the solemn character of the Concertos when he wrote "that they are possibly too severe to be played anywhere except in a church; but the fugues, the harmonies and modulations are very fine" - a stylistic judgement to be understood primarily in the context of the history of such works; as Dem emphasized, the influence of Corelli, above all that of his Concerti da chiesa, extended to all the orchestral works of Scarlatti's maturity. The differences of artistic purpose which kept Scarlatti at a distance from the orchestral innovations of his time led him to adhere generally to the traditions of the first masters of the concerto grosso, with whom he had become acquainted in Rome during his youth: Stradella, Legrenzi and Vitali. We have in mind particularly the concertos in minor keys, Nos. 1, 2, 4 and 5, which derived from four-part sonatas, and which may have been composed before Concertos Nos. 3 and 6 in the major. These begin not with fugal movements but with Allegros in concertante style, so that in this respect, too - especially in the Third Concerto (F major) - they are akin to the instrumental works of Corelli. Concerto No. 4 in G minor is most marked by archaic features, in particular the final movement in ⅜ time with its syncopated accents such as often occurred in 17th-century Venetian ballets. The Second Concerto (C minor) also consists of three movements (Allegro moderato - Grave - Minuet), among which the introductory Fugue and the charming Minuet are especially worthy of mention; in the latter there is a striking interpolation of a gently modulating middle section in the major, which interrupts the symmetrical eight-bar periods of the main part of the movement.

The First Concerto in F minor approaches closer than the others to the strict style of the Sonata da chiesa. Its polyphonic strictness is relaxed in the scansion of the Allemanda, after the Venetian fashion. Especially rich in contrasts are Concertos Nos. 3, 5 and 6 in F major, D minor and E major respectively. The final movement of the Third Concerto (F major) is highly individual - a kind of Tarantella, full of Baroque tonal splendour, and breathtaking in its animation. The Fifth Concerto opens with a Fugue which owes its unusual expressiveness to the character of its theme and of its harmonic development. The Sixth Concerto, to, like the Third, begins with a brief Allegro in concertante style. This is followed by a Fugue (⅘, alla breve), marked by a counter-subject which attains ever-increasing autonomy as the movement develops. A boldly modulating Largo of 17 bars leads to an ingratiating dance movement, the particularly attractive Finale to what is probably the most "galant" Concerto in this set, whose characteristics point especially to three stylistic attributes: polyphonic part-writing, tonal definition, and harmonic clarity.

### 页码 6 (左侧)

The Stabat Mater had its origins in the commemoration of the Passion and of the suffering of the Holy Mother in Central Italy between the 12th and 14th centuries. The author is generally supposed to have been Jacopone da Todi, a Franciscan lay brother who died in 1306. The text very soon became known outside Italy, and played a part in the development of Passion plays. From the end of the 15th century onwards, the Stabat Mater also won a place in the official liturgy, but it was used primarily during the private devotions and offices of religious communities.

Both the first two settings for several voices, by Giacomo van Weerbeke and Josquin Desprez, have survived in a choir-book compiled for Philip of Habsburg, Archduke of the Netherlands, who died in 1506, and are motets designed to accompany meditation. It was not until 1727 that the Stabat Mater was included as a sequence for the festival of the Seven Sorrows of the Blessed Virgin Mary in the Roman Catholic Missal, and a cento of it became an office hymn in the Breviary.

According to reliable accounts, Scarlatti composed his Stabat Mater in response to a commission from the Neapolitan brotherhood of the Cavalieri della Vergine de' dolori. The brotherhood ordered it for performance during the devotion for which they assembled every year on Fridays during March at the Franciscan church of San Luigi near the royal palace. Scarlatti's Stabat Mater is a late work. The parsimony of the noble brotherhood involved him in a very unusual task: for compositions to texts of this size it was customary to have soloists for all four parts, and in sacred works, to have a choir. Confined as he was to two soloists, Scarlatti saw himself obliged to write arias and duets, more especially as the instrumental forces available precluded arias with accompanying instrumental solos. In both sacred and secular works with arias and duets, however, it was usual for musical numbers to alternate with recitatives featuring action or description, and the verses of the Stabat Mater were not suitable for arias. In other words, it was not only in the choice of his musical effect that Scarlatti was extraordinarily restricted. He was unable to compose within the framework of any of the accepted forms, or even affo Pafratrir, but had to tackle a text entirely as he himself chose. Moreover, this text was none too suitable as a match for his own aesthetic concept of chiaro-scuro, which was based on contrast.

The most conspicuous characteristic of the style of his Stabat Mater are extensive chromatic passages, and which he expounds in the introductory movement, and to which he constantly reverts. These express the mood of pain and mourning which dominates the entire work. Scarlatti treats the verses of the Stabat Mater as musical numbers and - except in the first movement and in the recitative movements at the end of the work - he gives these the two-section form of the aria da chiesa. In the solo passages he employs the whole range of forms of expression accessible to the solo voice, from the freely accompanied solo of the Psalms and the Mass, up to the oratorio or operatic aria of new types featuring new devices (as, for example, sighing figurations, iambic rhythm used as the expression of painful confusion). The declamation of the text and the phrasing are polished to the utmost degree, as if he was composing in accordance with the rules of the old style. Superficial virtuosity, however, is avoided absolutely: coloraturas are scrupulously employed as a means of presenting and accentuating emotions. Scarlatti did not compose the two-part movements as duets in a real sense - which would have required an element of dialogue in the texts - but with a technique of virtuoso counterpoint which had its origins in his studies of the old style; they have their parallels in the duet passages of his great St. Cecilia Mass of 1720.

### 页码 7 (右侧)

By alternating solo and duet numbers and by giving them different characteristics, Scarlatti divided his composition into four sections: the first (Nos. 1-4) describes the anguish of the Holy Mother in her sor- row. The second (Nos. 5-8), quickening with the emphatically aria-like Quis est homo, is reflective, and its relationship with the first part is like that of an aria to a recitative. The third section (Nos. 9-13) leads on from contemplation of Mary to contemplation of the passion of Jesus. At this point Scarlatti interfered with the original text and discarded the parallel order of the verses in the interest of continual progress and intensification: verses 10/11 and 13/14 have been changed around. The duet Tui nati vulnerati and the undisguisedly operatic arias on either side of it should form the climax of the whole work: in the aria Fac, ut ardeat cor meum, Scarlatti uses the Aria agitata form which customarily served to portray the most extreme emotions, while in the aria Fuat crucem tectum stans he reflects the most profound pain in the Aria parlante form, at the same time making it seem like a popular canzona by giving it two verses. In the final section (Nos. 14-18), Scarlatti makes use of recitative - something which was against all the traditions of church music in Italy - and the prayers to the Holy Mother thus become a form of direct appeal.

There is scarcely any composer whose life and work were so intermingled with legend as Pergolesi. His early death - he was granted no more than five years for creative work - made him a romantic figure in the eyes of later generations as a darling of the gods, and the remarkable success which his compositions began to enjoy in all the centres of the musical world soon after his death aroused the interest of the public everywhere. Consequently the young musician who had died unknown outside his own circle became a European celebrity.

The little which we know about Pergolesi's life also leaves us in ignorance of many important facts. We do, however, know that on 4 January 1710 Giovanni Battista Draghi was born at Jesi, a little town in the Marches, a region on the Adriatic coast; later he called himself "Pergolesi" after Pergola, the home town of his ancestors. Following his first musical studies at Jesi, aristocratic benefactors enabled the delicate, sickly boy to obtain admission to the Naples Conservatory. There, in the musical metropolis of that time, he received instruction as a violinist and composer from leading members of the Neapolitan School. After completing his studies - probably in 1731, although we do not know the year for certain - he entered the service of various families and also, as a compositor scritturato, received commissions from elsewhere for operas and sacred works. Suffering from a severe illness, he sought relief at the baths of Pozzuoli near Naples, staying as a guest at the Franciscan monastery. There he died, barely 26 years of age, and was buried on 17 March 1736. Pergolesi's music marks a stylistic and historical transition. On the one hand the late-Baroque Neapolitan School reigned supreme; it had created rigid formal patterns for the overture, aria and other forms, within each of which a musical entity always gives expres- sion to a single emotion. On the other hand - as a result of the wish for subtler characterization, espe- cially in opera - there was a growing desire to ex- plore further the possibilities of expressing a wider range of feelings in detail. We find the fruits of this new musical attitude most clearly evident in La serva padrona, the first opera buffa, whose delightful freshness was to influence post-Baroque opera right through to the time of Mozart.

In Pergolesi's Stabat Mater sections in this new, per- sonal language stand side by side with others in the old, stricter style. The latter no doubt occur because church music has always tended towards conser- vatism, and is less amenable to innovations than other classes of music, opera above all.

According to the commission of the brotherhood the work was to employ the following forces: soprano and alto soli with string orchestra and basso con- tinuo. Pergolesi divided the 20 verses into twelve sections, presented as separate numbers, either solos or duets. He intended this work for the powerful, brilliant voices of two castrati. The addition of a cho- rus in many performances (at the Fac, ut ardeat and the final Amen) is not authentic.

### 页码 9 (右侧)

The reaction to the new, "fashionable" elements in Pergolesi's Stabat Mater was divided. Many musi- cians were enthusiastic, but there was also strong criticism, such as that of J. G. Sulzer, who wrote that "on hearing it ... the flesh creeps" (Theorie der schönen Künste, 1794). German audiences in particular, who always tended to regard Infinitù in music as superficial and unsuited to sacred words, refused - according to E. T. A. Hoffmann - "to carry worldly adornments into the sanctuary". Today, however, we value especially Pergolesi's personal touches which carry his music away from the conventions of his time, such as the duet Inflammatus et accensus (which could be from La serva padrona), the graphic setting of the words "Fac me vere tecum flere" in the duet Sancta Mater, istud agas, or the "cutting" trills at "Pertransivit gladius" in the soprano aria Cuius animam gementem. By contrast, archaic-sounding sec- tions such as the Fac, ut ardeat cor meum are relics of the strict church music tradition.

It may perhaps be said today that this "stylistic dis- union" (Alfred Einstein) is a particularly attractive feature of the Stabat Mater, and is, indeed, the prin- cipal reason for its importance. It is at points where the new suddenly takes over from the old, with flashes of brilliant originality, that Pergolesi's greatness is most clearly apparent.

The Stabat Mater had its origins in the commemoration of the Passion and of the suffering of the Holy Mother in Central Italy between the 12th and 14th centuries. The author is generally supposed to have been Jacopone da Todi, a Franciscan lay brother who died in 1306. The text very soon became known outside Italy, and played a part in the development of Passion plays. From the end of the 15th century onwards, the Stabat Mater also won a place in the official liturgy, but it was used primarily during the private devotions and offices of religious communities.

Both the first two settings for several voices, by Giacomo van Weerbeke and Josquin Desprez, have survived in a choir-book compiled for Philip of Habsburg, Archduke of Habsburg, who died in 1506. It was not until 1727 that the Stabat Mater was included as a sequence for the festival of the Seven Sorrows of the Blessed Virgin Mary in the Roman Catholic Missal, and a cento of it became an office hymn in the Breviary.

According to reliable accounts, Scarlatti composed his Stabat Mater in response to a commission from the Neapolitan brotherhood of the Cavalieri della Vergine de' dolori. The brotherhood ordered it for performance during the devotion for which they assembled every year on Fridays during March at the Franciscan church of San Luigi near the royal palace. Scarlatti's Stabat Mater is a late work. The parsimony of the noble brotherhood involved him in a very unusual task: for compositions to texts of this size it was customary to have soloists for all four parts, and in sacred works, to have a choir. Confined as he was to two soloists, Scarlatti saw himself obliged to write arias and duets, more especially as the instrumental forces available precluded arias with accompanying instrumental solos. In both sacred and secular works with arias and duets, however, it was usual for musical numbers to alternate with recitatives featuring action or description, and

Dans le Stabat Mater de Pergolesi, les éléments de ce nouveau langage personnel se tiennent à côté d'éléments de l'ancien style plus sévère. La raison en est qu'une partie que la musique d'église fut de tout temps conservatrice et les innovations s'y incor- porent moins facilement que dans d'autres genres, notamment dans l'opéra.

Conformément à la commission de la confrérie, l'œuvre devait suivre extérieurement le modèle de Scarlatti: deux voix solistes avec orchestre à cordes et basse continue. Pergolèse divisa les vingt strophes en douze sections séparées, présentées comme séparés numéros. Il a écrit son œuvre pour les fortes et éclatantes de deux castrats. Le renforcement par un choeur n'est pas authentique.

### 页码 19 (右侧) - 法语

La réaction aux éléments nouveaux, à ce qui était à la mode dans le Stabat Mater de Pergolesi ne fut pas unanime: à côté de l'approbation enthousiaste il y avait beaucoup de critiques comme celles de J. G. Sulzer qui «frisonne en entendant cette musique» (Theorie der schönen Künste, 1794). Dans les pays de langue allemande, this a toujours été le cas que, Imitation in the public law, according to E. T. A. Hoffmann, refused "to carry worldly adornments into the sanctuary". Today, however, we appreciate especially Pergolesi's personal touches which carry his music away from the conventions of his time, such as the duet Inflammatus et accents which could be from La serva padrona, the graphic setting of the words "Fac me vere tecum flere" in the duet Sancta Mater, istud agas, or the "cutting" trills at "Pertransivit gladius" in the soprano aria Cuius animam gementem. A l'opposé de cela, les parties archaïsantes comme le Fac, ut ardeat cor meum are relics of the strict church music tradition.

It may perhaps be said today that this "stylistic disunion" (Alfred Einstein) constitutes the particularly attractive feature of the Stabat Mater and is, indeed, the principal reason for its importance. It is at points where the new suddenly takes over from the old, with flashes of brilliant originality, that Pergolesi's greatness is most clearly apparent.
