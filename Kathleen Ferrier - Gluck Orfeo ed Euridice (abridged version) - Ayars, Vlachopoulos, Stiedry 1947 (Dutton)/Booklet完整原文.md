## Inside Notes

Kathleen Ferrier's name is synonymous in many older opera-lover's memory with the role of Orpheus in Gluck's opera. It was in the part that she made her last stage appearances when mortally ill at Covent Garden in 1953. Earlier she had performed the role at Glyndebourne in 1947, source of this recording, and in Holland. Although the Glyndebourne staging was not much admired by contemporary critics, who particularly disdained the treatment of the ballet, Ferrier's portrayal was singled out for special praise. As the singer's sister Winifred Ferrier pointed out in *The Life of Kathleen Ferrier* she learned the role in Italian for the occasion and worked hard, as was her custom, on the part beforehand. She had her hair cut for this male role and the eminent producer Carl Ebert inspired her to identify with the role of Orfeo, which she succeeded in doing as her intense accents on this recording confirm.

Ferrier had been invited back to Glyndebourne following her success in the titular part of Britten's *The Rape of Lucretia* the previous summer. She had often sung Orfeo's "Che farò" in recitals in the English translation "What is Life?" and recorded it for Decca, a disc that became a bestseller. I recall a performance with piano at a school recital that made me rush out for a recording (at that time Ferrier herself had not committed it to disc) so closely did she identify herself with the character's loss.

When she came to study the complete part for Glyndebourne she averred that it "lies in the fat of my voice", but there were difficulties. It seems she didn't always see eye to eye with the conductor, the Viennese-born Fritz Stiedry who was not convinced by her acting, perhaps understandably considering her inexperience on stage. He was also critical of her Italian and tended to alter the text as they went along to her acute annoyance. Ferrier was aware that there was some truth in his strictures and she sometimes spent whole evenings trying to improve her stage deportment and her accent, but she became so irritated by Stiedry that she threatened to hit him over the head with her plywood lyre. On the other hand photos of the rehearsals suggest a good rapport with Ebert. Throughout the run she sought to improve her performance, accepting and following frank advice given by a friend, the conductor Harry Vincent.

On the first night she took sixteen curtain-calls, and received the plaudits of the Press. The *Observer* commented: "Timorous epithets will not suffice. Kathleen Ferrier's is a noble voice", *The Listener* wrote of "an ideal Orfeo", and Winifred Ferrier quotes a letter from Sir Steuart Wilson, a former singer and at the time music director of the newly formed Arts Council who declared that

he had heard the role sung "as near my dreams as I could ever want".

The microphones catch much of the personal magnetism achieved by Ferrier on stage, I did not see her in this role, but on many other occasions, when her extraordinary gifts in the matter of communication were made manifest. The nobility and compassion evinced in her voice and demeanour were ideally suited to Gluck's ideas about his Orfeo. In the hero's sorrowful accents in her first scene, desperate appeal to the Furies ("Deh placatevi"... "mile pene"), wonder at the serenity of the heavenly scene ("Che puro ciel"), urgency in the extended duet with Euridice and, of course, in the desperation of "Che farò", Ferrier fulfilled virtually ever aspect of the demanding part.

Spike Hughes in his history of Glyndebourne wrote that the "nine performances of Gluck's opera did not rate among the most inspiring in Glyndebourne's history" but that Ferrier's performance itself was "historic". Ebert and Stiedry had worked together successfully in prewar Berlin, but on this occasion their cooperation wasn't so productive. At the time Stiedry's conducting was considered fast and peremptory, but in the light of how Gluck is approached today, his speeds seem right, avoiding the over-romanticisation of the composer's music prevalent at the time but now frowned on.

The other leading singers were Ann Ayars, a young American soprano who undertook Euridice, which she sings with a deal of urgent resolve in her tone as she pleads with her Orfeo to look upon her. The Amor was the Greek soprano Zoë Vlachopoulos, who catches the lively character of the role without resort to the coyness that can afflict its interpreters.

The first night of the production was on June 19, Decca, to whom Ferrier was contracted, had already decided to record the work, albeit with foreshortenings, although the main solos are given complete. The recording took place on 22, 23 and 29 June, in other words during the run of the opera at Glyndebourne (I am indebted to Paul Campion's invaluable "Ferrier: A Career Recorded" for this information). The recording first appeared on fourteen sides of 78rpm records. In its various reissues, it has been further cut so this is the first complete reissue of the original set - and it can also be heard in greatly improved sound compared with what has gone before, proving that the recording itself was good for its time.
