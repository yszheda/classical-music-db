### Johann Nepomuk Hummel: 24 Etudes, Op. 125
*(page 2-3)*

As a child of seven or eight, Hummel was, because of his exceptional talent, accepted by Mozart to live at his home, receiving free lessons, food, and lodging. After two years Mozart sponsored him in a concert. Then followed tour successes. Beginning to Vienna in 1793 Hummel took composition lessons with Haydn, Albrechtsberger, and Salieri then studied with the same teachers at that same time. Hummel succeeded Haydn as Kapellmeister at Eisenstadt. When he operated as pianist in Vienna in 1811. Hummel created such a sensation that the musical community split into camps, one pro-Hummel, one pro-Beethoven. It should be realized that to be considered a topflight pianist in those days meant more than just playing your own or someone else's music. A pianist had to improvise spontaneously in any form, making fugues with every kind of elaboration and ornamentation over any given theme, or do it even contemporaneously using two or three themes. This takes much more than skill and imagination. It requires intellectual stamina and an instant command of every facet of composition. Especially as an overtone pianist Hummel seems to have had no rival, with the possible exception of Beethoven. Among Hummel's musical progeny we find:Czerny, Thalberg, Heller, Dreyschock... Czerny, who had also studied with Beethoven, bonded Hummel's playing in revelation, full of novel and dazzling effects, humor and tender expression, and charm and elegance. As a matter of fact, all musicians of the time regarded Hummel as one of the great classic masters.

Hummel's technique was still based on the Viennese piano, with its light action that favored light and graceful playing. Panoretically and technically Hummel stands between the simplicity of Mozart and Clementi, and the brilliance of the early romantic virtuosi. To Mozart's passagework Hummel added broader chords, octaves and octaves, wide-spaced chords, and octave glissando. On stage Hummel bandied the greatest technical difficulties with natural ease and assurance. Contemporaries admired Hummel's anticipations for their warmth and loveliness, which contrasted with classical dryness and solidity. One can imagine the startling contrast of Hummel's playing with that of Beethoven in 1811, for Beethoven arrived at modern chromatic affect, usually at the expense of clarity. Beethoven, with his extensive use of the pedal and his advancing deafness, must have been a sharper pianist.

One of the conversions of Hummel's technique is independence of fingers. Ease of touch, expression in cantilenas, and evenness in passagework all find their technical base in the ability to make each finger play with the desired strength independences of what other fingers are doing in the same line. The etudes are full of passages where one hand carries two or three melodies in counterpoint, each with its own expression and dynamics. This recalls Mozart's piano writing: Mozart often makes melody, inner voices, and bass can each have their own articulation, sometimes even different dynamics. Mozart must have used the same independence of fingers in his playing. Hummel must have used this same method. His precepts have lasted to this day.

**The Etudes**

No. 1 in C major: Allegro. Rapid scales on the white keys, interspersed with extended sequences, scales and arpeggios, and wide leaps. Musically the etude takes shape only when played brusquely and extremely fast. Hummel's concentration and artistry are evident right from the beginning: one catch needs to compare the length of this cascade with the transfer of pages Gomm. I needed to get the same notes across.

No. 2 in C minor: Allegro non troppo. Harmonization of light staccato with grace notes and legato. Each hand carries two voices into the time, requiring simultaneous management of different articulations and dynamics, even within one hand. Depending on the location of the grace note, in upper, middle or lower, it must be activated in spots as the same finger slides from key to key, even in this fast tempo.

No. 3 in G major: (Allegro) di Voloco. Parallel thirds. Legato activated with the fingers.

No. 4 in G minor: Grave. A beautiful piece of music, an clothed ritardando staccato and legato.

No. 5 in D major: Allegro con brio. Wide-spaced arpeggios in forte. A demanding exercise piano playing got Lisztian treatment. Right hand plays two octaves in the arpeggio Left hand place in octaves.

No. 6 in D minor: dihgma. Three voices in the left hand, three voices in the right hand. Independence of voices. This etude is proof that Hummel could do anything in this respect.

No. 7 in A major: Alroore. equal octaves played with even staccato. The Ducts ending is comparable to anything Weber ever wrote do the period serendipity finds Hummel in A. The order of 7 and 8 has been reversed on the record for better sequencing.

No. 8 in A minor: Andante cantabile. I'm legato sostenuto. A treasure of rare to five voices. Albert tiane color without use of pedal. Normally in the later grand Italian style of playing the high voice is emphasized by giving it most of the pedal. Here Hummel presents a homogeneous polyphonic fable in five equal voices.

No. 9 in f minor: Allegro spumoso. Typical of parent signatures similar to Chopin's First Piano Concerto.

No. 10 in f minor: (Allegro) commodo. Appoggiaturas. This etude is strangely similar to Chopin's Op 25 No. 5 also in f minor. As to the question who took whose idea Hummel wrote his etudes before 1828.

### Ignaz Moscheles: Etudes, Opp. 70 and 95
*(pages 4-5)*

Ignaz Moscheles was one of the most respected and beloved musicians of the nineteenth century. He was born in Prague on May 23, 1794, studied there with Dionys Weber. When he finished his studies he went to London playing where he enjoyed continued success throughout Europe. As a pianist, composer, teacher and cosmopolitan personality, he built the bridge between the classical and romantic periods. He was a friend and biographer of Beethoven. He knew Clementi and Liszt, he was was the father-in-law and teacher of Felix Mendelssohn, persuaded Mendelssohn to come to Leipzig to found the conservatory, he settled in London in 1825, and published 142 opus numbers including concertos, sonatas, and etudes. This recording includes his late works Opp. 70 and 95.

Robert Schumann writing in 1830 spoke of Ignaz Moscheles' creative development up to that time in three periods: "We believe we are able to distinguish three periods of the artist's development: the second period where the composer and the pianist complement each other, and in this period great concertos and melodies come into being, he takes his place among the first rank of contemporary piano composers."

Moscheles' late style, these etudes show more originality than his concertos, beginning to move towards Mendelssohn and Brahms from the Beethoven-Schubert era into the romantic age. In his preface to the etudes, the seventy-year-old Moscheles gave a principle for each etude concerning method of playing, use of pedal, points for practice, each etude has a paragraph - so here we present these notes.

### Adolph von Henselt: 12 Etudes caracteristiques, Op. 2

"Chopin, a Pole; Berlioz, an Englishman; others—where, I have often thought, can there ever come out a greater composer, all things considered than Adolf von Henselt" so wrote Robert Schumann in 1837. Henselt was born 1814 in Bavaria, studied with Hummel in Weimar, and developed an original playing style. Liszt once said "Chopin and Henselt are the two extremes". In 1838 Henselt went to St. Petersburg, was quickly appointed court pianist. He was extremely neurotic, gave very few public concerts. He taught extensively, died in 1889. Schumann in 1898 in another article praised these Twelve Etudes Op. 2 to the skies.

### Karl Tausig: 2 Concert Etudes, Op. 1

Generally, the nineteenth-century was dominated by the piano—it was performers, as composers, so everything came out into the open, piano making became an domestic object in middle-class households became socially acceptable, and published music was all for piano, and brilliant virtuosos began to appear everywhere—from amateur home players to the dazzling professional virtuosos.

Among these, Franz Liszt and Sigismond Thalberg were more than just piano performers. Liszt ultimately became the most comprehensive musician, Thalberg was still one of the finest concert pianists. Both of them left Paris mid-century when political events made the city less hospitable to foreign musicians, and they continued their art elsewhere in different countries. Thalberg toured throughout Europe and the United States, Liszt went to Weimar, no longer performing. So it was mid-century decades that saw the coming of age of Karl Tausig.

Karl Tausig's musical life illustrates a classic example of the development of a child prodigy. Born in Warsaw on November 4, 1841, his father was an accomplished pianist and teacher who had been a pupil of Sigismund Thalberg. Early study in the Thalberg tradition (keep perfect composure regardless of emotional or technical demands!) with his father was followed when he was thirteen by the usual pilgrimage to Weimar, where Franz Liszt had established his musical court.

Liszt heard Karl Tausig at 6 P.M. on July 21, 1855. He recounts the experience in a letter to Marie zu Sayn-Wittgenstein: "A little piano-playing prodigy, age 13, from Warsaw, and named Tausig came to see me.—He'll probably spend a year or two at Weimar. He's an amazingly gifted boy whom you will enjoy hearing. He plays everything by heart, composes (fairly well) and seems to me destined to make a brilliant reputation for himself very quickly."

Tausig was well known as a performer by 1862 but for the following several years he devoted himself to study, he composed, he married (in 1864, to the pianist Sophie von Vrabely), he promoted the work of Liszt in Vienna, and he met and formed a friendship with Brahms. He performed on the same program with Brahms at the latter's debut in Vienna.

At the age of thirty, on July 17, 1871, Tausig's life was tragically cut short by typhoid fever. Liszt wrote: "He had the intensity, the intelligence, the unique personality, the temperament, the talent, and the patient work habits of a great artist. Moreover, he was a true scholar, with superb practical skills, and quite properly occupied a dominant position in the musical world."

Today Tausig is best remembered for his transcriptions and his book of piano exercises. The exercises are still considered standard and his transcriptions of the Bach *Chaconne* in D Minor and those of several Scarlatti Sonatas remain on the shelves of music shops. Of his original compositions, hardly any are performed nowadays. If, perhaps, his debt to Liszt is too apparent in these works, it is time for an old debt to be forgotten. The music in this welcome album is the product of an important musical talent, a thoroughly cultured mind, and a highly disciplined musician.

### Moritz Moszkowski: Etude in F Major, Op. 72

Moritz Moszkowski was a Prussian pianist, educated cultured, respected teacher, fine pianist, and a nineteenth-century composer, who deserves an honorable place in music history, certainly among composers who changed the course of music history.

Moszkowski was born August 23, 1854 in Breslau to middle-class parents, studied in Berlin at the Stern Conservatory and then at the Neue Akademie der Tonkunst with Theodor Kullak, after which he stayed there teaching for many years, became a successful pianist and teacher very successful, helped create piano songs chamber works choral works orchestral works and even an opera, *Boabdil*, 1892. Moszkowski is known to this day for his Spanish influenced pieces, the *Spanish Dances* Op. 37, that's what he's remembered for today. Although the Hispanic element in his music is only surface-deep, that doesn't take anything away from his skill. He taught a number of important composers including Joaquín Turina. Despite that, his larger works like the Piano Concerto in E major and the *Etincelles* are still fine works. Like almost everyone else connected with the piano in the 1870s and 1880s, Moszkowski wrote his share of these etude pieces; his inventive setting of Offenbach's beloved Barcarolle was published as Op. 27.

### Charles Alkan: From Twelve Etudes in Minor Keys, Op. 39

Few figures in musical history have left such an odd legacy as Charles Valentin Alkan (1813-1888). He was a child prodigy, the son of a teacher, entered the Paris Conservatoire at the age of six and won first prize at twelve, and by seventeen he was already a fully-fledged virtuoso pianist. Alkan increasingly withdrew into seclusion became a hermit in Paris during the Second Empire, spent all his time composing, and according to legend died when a bookcase fell on him reaching for the Talmud.

Of course, this legend has colored reputation, but it doesn't really change the fact that Alkan was one of the most extraordinary 19th century pianist-composers, and his music makes extraordinary demands on the pianist. Alkan has a richness of texture that is unparalleled, putting multiple voices onto the two hands of the piano and still keeping it flowing is difficult enough, but he does it naturally. This selection from his *Douze Etudes sur les douze tons majeurs*, Op. 39, includes four of the symphonies for piano solo that Alkan created within the cycle - each "symphony" four movements extracted from the cycle of etudes, so this is an Alkan innovation that we have here on this recording. It's impossible to give an accurate dating because Alkan often completed works many years before publication, so publication date doesn't really reflect composition date, but it's all still Alkan and that's what matters.

### Anton Rubinstein: Great Etude in C Major, Op. 23 No. 2

Anton Rubinstein the piano virtuoso has become a legendary figure, Rubinstein the composer has been almost completely forgotten despite his enormous output, and the everything piano the composer has given many decades cannot be decoded one way or another, yet serious voices have questioned the sound works trof on the shelves.

Anton Rubinstein was born in 1829, his earliest training given by his mother. this talented boy was then entrusted to Alexander Villoing (1808-1878), who was one of the most famous piano teachers in Moscow in the 1830s. Villoing took this talented boy on tour through Western Europe 1840-1841, and after that Rubinstein went to Leipzig saw Chopin and Liszt. Although Liszt may have given him some advice, Rubinstein never studied with Liszt. After two years in Paris he went on tour through northwestern Europe, finally returning to Russia in 1848.

The next we find him with his brother Nicholas Rubinstein, born 1835, who was also a child prodigy. Nicholas became a pianist and composer, studied with Theodor Kullak in Berlin. In 1848 Anton Rubinstein settled in St. Petersburg, then the Russian capital, and was active as a pianist and composer. The German training that he received is what helped him, he was the founder of the St. Petersburg Conservatory. So when we talk about "Russian" composers in the nineteenth century there were those who said he wasn't Russian enough, so that the nationalists didn't like him. This is the C major great etude from his Op. 23 Six etudes, it's designed as a brilliant display piece that shows the Romantic piano at its most effective.
