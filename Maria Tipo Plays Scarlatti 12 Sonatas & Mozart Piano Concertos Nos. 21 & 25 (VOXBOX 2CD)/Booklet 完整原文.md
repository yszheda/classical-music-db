## Program Notes - Scarlatti: 12 Sonatas

Domenico Scarlatti is a unique figure in the history of the arts. Having grown up in the shadow of the fame of his father Alessandro, who had put the stamp of his personality on his period, the second Scarlatti offered a genius in music in the midst of a period that already had the greatest creators as musicians. The younger Scarlatti achieved not only fame in his own right, but disproved in a spectacular way the common notion that great men never have great sons.

In Italy most of Alessandro's countrymen considered him the prince of Italian music, and wherever Domenico went his keyboard sonatas are admired as fine gems by performing artist and music lover alike.

Domenico was born in Naples on October 26, 1685, the same year that both Bach and Handel came into the world. Alessandro, at that time twenty-five years of age, was *maestro di cappella* to the Spanish Viceroy of Naples. He was a very busy musician, constantly traveling between Naples and the major musical centers of Europe, composing, and teaching singers. He had very little time to devote to the musical education of his children. There are no records that Domenico ever received formal instruction in any one of the famous four Neapolitan conservatories. Probably as a member of the royal chapel choir he received some fundamental music education, undoubtedly heard much good music in the house of his father, a maestro who had composed many sixty works for the stage and numerous cantatas and church pieces by the time Domenico was eleven years old.

In 1701, shortly before his sixteenth birthday, Domenico was appointed organist and composer in the royal chapel. In the following year he went with his father to Florence, where Alessandro objected to receiving the customary composer's stipend for the music he was to did not materialize, so Alessandro accepted a position in Rome and Domenico returned to Naples.

At the insistence of his father, Domenico went to Venice in 1704. Domenico had already composed five operas by this time, and in his letter, which was preserved, Alessandro, senior, promises of a prosperous future for an ambitious young musician. However, we do not know of any opera of Domenico Scarlatti performed in Venice in those years.

In 1709 Domenico was in Rome in the service of the exiled Queen Maria Casimira of Poland (the widow of Jan Sobieski, who had fought gallantly against the Turks). Maria Casimira, as had Christina, the ex-queen of Sweden before the latter's death in 1689, enjoyed being surrounded by artists. In her day, *Christinendum* was the meeting place of the Roman intellectual elite — scholars, men of letters, artists, and musicians. She was the patroness of the three leading composers living in Rome then: Alessandro Scarlatti, Bernardo Pasquini, and Arcangelo Corelli. The last dedicated to her his first published compositions, the *Church Sonatas, Op. 1*.

Maria Casimira had secured the services of Alessandro Scarlatti, but he stayed with her only for a short time, returning in 1708 to Naples to assume the position of *maestro di cappella* at Santa Maria a Domnica, who remained with her until she was forced to leave Rome in 1714 because of lack of money. Domenico's chief task in her service was the composition of operas to be performed in the palace's private theatre. Little else is known about this period.

Maria Casimira's affection and regard for the Scarlattis eventually found employment as *maestro di cappella* to the Portuguese ambassador, but before then he was appointed assistant to *Famiano Soa, maestro di cappella* at St. Peter's Cathedral. When Soa died in December of 1714, Scarlatti succeeded him. In 1715, he was given the title *maestro di cappella* of the cathedral. A manuscript diary kept by a Francesco Goltigiani, Scarlatti went to England, but no evidence that he actually visited Great Britain has been produced yet. Ralph Kirkpatrick, whose exhaustive book on Domenico Scarlatti is the standard work on the composer, places his departure from Rome from 1719 until just following his arrival in Portugal in 1721 are impossible to trace. At this time his compositions were a set of such high standards that only a composer could achieve.

Maria Casimira entered the service of King Joao V of Portugal as the head of a group composed of thirty to forty singers and nearly as many instrumentalists. For church functions he had to contribute music both with and without organ accompaniment, with alternating solo and choral passages, and often with double chorus. He also was responsible for supplying music for court festivities, the royal birthdays, and name days of the saints. Finally he was in charge of the musical instruction of the king's daughter, Maria Barbara. Her Queen of Spain, who was very talented musically and sang and played the clavier. She not only supplied Scarlatti with board but also a competent composer. That she felt a sincere gratitude toward Scarlatti can be divined from the fact that she bequeathed a ring and two thousand doubloons to her "music master," a sizable amount of money at that time.

Domenico returned to Italy in 1724. He visited his aging father, who was to die in 1725 at the age of sixty-six. In the same year the betrothal of Maria Barbara (aged fourteen) to the crown prince of Spain took place and Scarlatti accompanied the Infanta to Seville. Kirkpatrick remarks that around 1726 Scarlatti was told that he would be expected to follow Princess Maria Barbara to Spain. No doubt it was her wish to continue the artistic association with her highly esteemed teacher whose devotion she could depend. It is quite possible that it was a harmonious teacher-pupil relationship. It required the creation of music to further the careers and ambitions of his pupil and it is said that the bulk of his most mature keyboard works were written during this period.

Accompanied by his sixteen year-old bride Maria Catalina, whom he had married in Naples in 1728, Scarlatti arrived in Spain sometime in January 1729 and followed the court to Seville where the Princess' and her husband's families met. The marriage of the Infanta, however, did not occupy a position of great prestige in Spain during the reign of Felipe V, lacking the power to effect a change in this situation. Princess Maria Barbara appealed to her father to obtain for her beloved teacher a position that would enable him to devote his entire time to composing upon Scarlatti in 1758. The composer showed his gratitude by dedicating his first publication, the *Essercizi* to her in 1738, which were received with great enthusiasm in Spain. They appeared handsomely engraved in London in 1739. Even after Fernando and Maria Barbara ascended to the Spanish throne in 1746, Scarlatti did not occupy a conductor's post at the royal establishment. It was with Princess Maria Barbara in Aranjuez and El Pardo that Scarlatti took charge of the opera and musical entertainment at court festivities. Although Fernelli was the favorite of the royal pair and wielded great political influence, Scarlatti never lost his position in Madrid. Seven years after Bach and two years before Handel, he hardly wrote any more vocal pieces, confining himself to the composition of harpsichord sonatas, of which not autograph survives. The principal sources of his keyboard music are manuscript collections by copies to be found in Venice, Parma, Munster, Vienna, London, and Cambridge. The entire collection of fifteen volumes, containing 196 sonatas, only 155 belonged probably to Queen Maria Barbara. She bequeathed all her music to Farinelli, who in all probability bought these treasures back to Italy. The collections housed in Munster and Vienna, containing 149 and 308 sonatas, respectively, were copied up by Abbot Fortunato Santini (1778-1862), an indefatigable collector of eighteenth-century music. This formed the basis for Carl Czerny's *Sämtliche Werke für das Piano-Forte* by Domenico Scarlatti (1839). This inadequate publication contains 200 sonatas, some of which they are by other composers.

In 1906 Alessandro Longo, an Italian composer and pianist, started the issuance of the most nearly complete edition of Scarlatti's sonatas. It was ten volumes in size, including all 555 sonatas that were published by the Ricordi firm of Milan. This edition was a great achievement, though not without serious faults, for Longo combined arbitrarily ten sonatas to form a "suite," without consideration for chronological and stylistic sequence.

Studying the manuscript copies of Scarlatti's sonatas, Ralph Kirkpatrick succeeds in establishing the approximate chronology of Scarlatti's entire sonata production. The chronological sequence of the items presented on this recording is as follows:

This selection offers the listener an instructive cross-section of Scarlatti's keyboard output, tracing its development from the *Essercizi* to the sonatas composed in the last year of his life. The modes of Domenico Scarlatti's early keyboard sonatas lie in Italy but in his later sonatas offer influences made themselves felt: Portuguese and Spanish popular music and, as Kirkpatrick points out, the music of French clavecinists, Couperin and Rameau. The architectural principle of the Scarlatti sonata conforms by no means to the classical pattern. The Scarlatti sonata is a piece in binary form, divided into two sections by a double bar. The first half presents a basic tonality and moves to the dominant key, indicated by the closing tonality of the first half. The second half moves away from the closing tonality of the first half, reestablishes the main key, and restates the thematic material introduced in the second half of the first section. In contrast to the classical sonata, the Scarlatti sonata does not recapitulate the thematic material announced at the beginning in the main key. It can be described as A1, B B; in contrast to the classical sonata which is roughly A B A1 B. The small intervals used by Scarlatti add a great deal to the classical sonata. The Scarlatti sonata, with its economy of thematic material, is distinguished by its great variety of colors, great variety of musical imagery. Scarlatti raised piano music from baroque virtuosity into lighter spheres and brought it closer to the new style that was to be embodied and enhanced by the art of the Viennese classical masters.

— **Joseph Braunstein**

## Program Notes - Mozart: Piano Concerto No. 21 in C Major, K. 467

This magnificent work was completed on March 9, 1785, barely a month after the now famous concerto in D minor. Though scored for the same orchestral resources (strings, flute, oboes, horns, trumpets, and drums), the two are utterly different in character, and provide a good instance of Mozart's habit of composing strongly contrasting works in pairs. The first stormy work had passion; the second is essentially idyllic. The latter's key, C major, is appropriate: it was composed for Mozart's own performance at the subscription concerts in Vienna, where this great work was heard for the first time. The choice of the neutral key of C gives Mozart scope for a wide range of subtle modulations, in the same imaginative style as we find in the superb string quartet and quintet, and, of course, in the later piano concerto in the same key. His own cadenzas, mentioned by Leopold Mozart in a letter to his daughter of April 8, 1785, are unfortunately lost.

In the first movement, *Allegro maestoso*, the adjective is hardly borne out by the brilliant and generally cheerful tone of the whole, in which episodes in minor keys are short and relatively infrequent. The strongly military march theme of the opening ritornello has something of the character of a passepied, to the music, which is strengthened by the passage of bold counterpoint that is one of the secrets of the opening tutti. The contrasting melodies consist largely of smoother scale passages, at times based on sequences, as is the very first. There are other memorable themes: a G major tune given to the soloist after the first return of the tutti, with simple but most effective arpeggiated support from the woodwind. There are other memorable themes, such as the D minor four-part march that is given to the strings, and a G major tune. In the latter, the melody is really dominated by the shape and rhythm of the opening bars.

The second movement, *Andante*, is one of the most beautiful slow movements ever written. Its opening quality, which is derived partly from the sustained strings, is partly recognized pizzicato figures in the bass, and partly from the extraordinary richness of the harmonic coloring added by the wind. The introduction, with continuous triplet figures in the middle strings, against the 4/4 melody from the second violins, seems to quiver with a nocturne-like intensity of feeling. Some sections of the melodic line in the solo piano part are rather bare, with wide skips. There is no doubt that a performance of these parts were meant to be embellished and the skips filled in.

In the final movement, *Allegro vivace assai*, the germinal force of the sparkling rondo comes from its opening theme, which Mozart fashioned to flow from the grace of the strings, later, melodies introduce a quaint, almost serenade-like character, particularly one given to the woodwind, and one later discovered to be a solo horn. It is a rondo, the wind has the dominating idea that unifies the movement comes from the infinitely resourceful use of the first six notes of the opening melody. Mozart uses this to give the movement its different characters, with delightful effects. The very character of the movement reflects perfectly the triumphant mood of the first and the iridescent fantasy of the andante.

## Program Notes - Mozart: Piano Concerto No. 25 in C Major, K. 503

Mozart completed this work on December 4, 1786, in Vienna, for his own performance at a series of concerts that he gave during the winter of that year. He played the solo part on the fortepiano, which he then had almost entirely superseded the harpsichord for public use. Although not strongly military in character, the introduction, with its rhythmic first subject, in clear, silvery tone and light action, are ideally suited to such a brilliant work as this C major concerto. Mozart regularly strengthened its expressive power and sonorous by fitting it with two sets of pedals: the first set, or *Bass* mechanism, like most of the fortepianos of that time; the second set, *Kniehebel* or knee-levers. Mozart had the second set added to his instrument in 1782. The piano was built by Anton Walter, Vienna's most famous fortepiano maker.

The first movement, *Allegro maestoso*, is one of Mozart's most magnificent and complex musical structures, and it is in it centered the main interest of the concerto. The descending fanfare which begins it reappears continually, sometimes at moments of crisis, sometimes to link up 90 of the 50 bars of the movement. Both are opened by the same pointed rhythm of three quarters, and both, like most of the themes of the movement, are shaped within a comparatively small compass. The first is enlarged upon in a passage that culminates in some glorious writing for wind. There seems to be doubt that a performance of these parts were meant to be embellished and the skips filled in.
