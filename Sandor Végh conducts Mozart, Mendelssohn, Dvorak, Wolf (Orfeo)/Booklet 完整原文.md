### Preserving the Unrepeatable

The Salzburg Festival was founded in 1920. Ever since then artists and music lovers from around the world have been meeting annually at this crossroads of European culture. Much lived and often sworn, the Festival of the 1980s was exposed to many and varied changes - but some things survive, for the original idea as envisioned by its founders - a place where art could flourish under extraordinarily favourable conditions, where it could become a truly great event - has been confirmed time and again in wonderful ways.

Almost every summer there have been performances in Salzburg that the participants feel as if they had been unique and unrepeatable. Apart from people's memories, these impressions can be preserved only by means of acoustic documentation.

Austrian Radio broadcast its first Salzburg Festival performance in 1925, and since the end of the Second World War radio stations from the farthest corners of the earth have been connected to the Festival. Thus a unique body of acoustic documents has accumulated in Salzburg over the years. Only the preservation of the live event, rather than a protected studio recording or any striving towards perfection, can hope to keep the unrepeatable moment alive for posterity.

In 1992 the Salzburg Festival began to open its archives and to undertake the costly task of technical restoration, making the document available to music lovers around the world. It is our sincere wish that these Salzburger Festspiele documents will contribute to the great artistic achievements of the past, without which its present-day and future image would remain incomplete.

###证言之新的黄金时代 (Testimony to a new golden age)

When Sándor Végh took over as director of the Camerata Academica in 1978, both parties brought a rich and unusual past to their new relationship: not only did the pupils and teachers of the Mozarteum Orchestra form the ensemble benefit from the arrangement, so too did the Hungarian violinist, whose success as a teacher was matched only by his reputation as a conductor.

The Camerata Academica had been formed a quarter of a century earlier by Bernhard Paumgartner, with Salzburg and its festival specifically in mind. A native of Vienna, Paumgartner was noted for his versatility both as a conductor and as a musicologist. He made a name for himself as a scholar and teacher, and by 1951 he had been appointed director of the Salzburg Mozarteum. Three years later he helped to co-found the Salzburg Festival and from the outset did much to promote those of Mozart's works that had been more or less neglected until then. The Mozarteum concert season included three "Serenades" and the annual performance of the St Matthew Passion was due to his initiative, as were many of the other ambitious programmes ineluctably associated with Salzburg.

Formed in 1952, the Camerata Academica was made up of teachers and the best students from the Mozarteum, whose direction Paumgartner had undertaken. It was founded specifically to provide an ensemble for the Mozart performances at the Festival in 1928 and for which he devised a particular type of programme. His mixture of early and late works, including Mozart, the great instrumental concertos and the symphonies of Haydn were rarely performed at this time soon became a central pillar of the Festival. Not least as a result of the annual radio broadcasts of these concerts. A unique approach to the works in question ensured that the performances, too, soon became household names. During the course of the year, Paumgartner, who was a versatile musician and author, expanded the Camerata Academica's field of activity, performing not only his own works but also those of earlier Salzburg composers such as Eberlin and the two Borts. But his spirit of discovery also led him to the works of his contemporaries. Paul Hindemith's Mathis der Maler, for example, and works such as Schönberg's Verklärte Nacht and Bartók's Divertimento for strings.

Ensembles kümmern können, als Fest-präsident aber immer noch am anderen Orchester festgehalten. Nun war diese Situation für die Camerata Academica nicht mehr oder weniger privilegiert: dies betraf nicht nur einen neuen Leiter, sondern vor allem auch den Musiker. Sándor Véghs ungeschminkte Interessen innerhalb und außerhalb des inzwischen zur Musik-hochschule erhobenen Mozarteums verhandelten über Jahre eine gedrückt volle Enge, bis der ungarische Geiger die Leitung des Ensembles übernahm.

Sándor Végh, 1912 in Klausenburg geboren, in Budapest unter anderem von Jenő Hubay ausgebildet, als Solist und Kammermusiker weltweit erfolgreich, mit dem von ihm geführten Végh-Quartett eine Institution, übernahm eine Lehrtätigkeit am Mozarteum 1971 als Nachfolger von Bernhard Paumgartner, Südold aufgegeben hatte. Ich erinnere mich an die Gespräche, die ich damals mit dem Neuverpflichteten für die Salzburger Festspiele führte, in denen Végh von den Perspektiven sprach, die er der Alt-Österreicher und Musikerpersönlichkeit angesichts der "Fortführung Salzburg" sah. Er träumte für das Mozarteum von einer neuen Art "mitteleuropäischen Führung" und sprach mit seinem unverwechselbar ungarischen Akzent, was mir unvergesslich ist: „Ich bin kommen als der erste Schwalbe".

Es enden, die von Véghs Visionen profitierten, waren die jungen Geiger, die von ihm nicht nur das handwerkliche Rüstzeug erhielten, sondern vor allem eine Erweiterung ihres musikalischen und persönlichen Horizonts erfuhren. Das Miteinander-Musizieren in diesen kleinen und größeren Besetzungen und seine ansteckende Vitalität brachte bald über die eigene Gegenwart hinaus eine junge Streicher entwickelte sich am Mozarteum eine Szene, die in die Geschichte der Camerata in die Salzburger Festspiele einen neuen Leiter gewann. Dieser begann seine Arbeit 1983 unter großer Anspannung und groß besetzter Kammermusik: das bald erweiterte er das Repertoire. Mit der wachsenden Erfahrung des Dirigenten, die auch er in fast nahtlos an die großen Traditionen des Hauses anknüpfen konnte, erweiterte er das Repertoire. Die ersten Erfolge bestätigten den Weg in eine neue Zukunft. So kam es, dass die Camerata, die für das Ensemble gleichen Namens ein neues Leben begann, unter der Leitung von Sándor Végh die ersten Früchte tragen wurde.

1983 war die Camerata Academica unter Sándor Végh erstmals wieder zu den Festspielen eingeladen, nachdem sie auf diesem Programm standen auch ein Konzert und eine Serenade. In späteren Jahren kamen andere Aufgaben hinzu,

Sándor Végh was born in Kolozsvár in 1912 and studied in Budapest, where his teachers included Jenő Hubay. He achieved worldwide success as a soloist and chamber recitalist, he endowed institutional status to the Végh Quartet, accepting a teaching post at the Mozarteum in 1971 as the successor of Bernhard Paumgartner. I can remember the conversations I had with him in his new post at the Salzburg Mozart shortly after he took up his new post. A product of the old Austro-Hungarian Empire, a cosmopolitan musician, he spoke of the "challenge" that Salzburg represented for him and dreamt of a "new Europe" for the Mozarteum. Speaking in his unmistakable Hungarian accent he said - unforgettable, "I've come as the first swallow."

All those who profited from Végh's vision of the future were young violinists who not only learnt the orchestra from him as their teacher, but above all extended their musical and artistic horizons. Making music together - one of Végh's principal concerns, both in small and large ensembles - went beyond the confines of his own violin class. A new generation of young artists gradually formed around the Mozarteum and at the end of the 1970s, from which just the right number of talent-spotters Végh's new director - the Camerata Academica, that played large-scale chamber music, also began to form the ensemble's own repertoire. With his growing experience as a conductor came increasing assurance in dealing with larger ensembles. The Camerata Academica's first successes with string concertos and its first tours confirmed the way ahead into a new age that bore plentiful fruit for both the ensemble and Sándor Végh himself.

After a gap of several years, the Camerata Academica was invited back to the Salzburg Festival in 1983. From 1992 the ensemble established a tradition that it was to continue for the next ten years: continuing its involvement in the festival had started to increase. Following the first musicians to profit from Végh's vision of the future, the Camerata Academica was invited back to give its own concert cycles, including a Serenade, during the Salzburg Festival. Recordings made during these years are now available and include symphonies by Haydn and Mozart (ORF CD 3141 [C 303 921 B]) and a cycle of Bach's orchestral suites available in the series of Salzburg Festival documents (C 537 002 B).

The present live performances from the 1986 festival are examples of the extraordinarily fruitful collaboration between Sándor Végh and the Camerata Academica during the ensemble's heyday. Végh had chosen works scored exclusively for strings on this occasion, a serenade in the festive ambience of the Felsenreitschule on 19 August and a chamber matinee at the Mozarteum three days later. Both were intended to show what the Camerata was capable of in all their spontaneity, disciplined playing and, above all, the intoxicating musicality that the seventy-four-year-old conductor was invariably able to impart to his players.

Our programme begins with Mozart's magical Serenata notturna, a concertoicante string ensembles, one of two violins, viola and double bass, the other comprising the tutti and made up of two violins, violas and cellos, with timpani adding a note of colour. Mozart wrote this serenade in January 1776. The young Gebhard Paumgartner was no doubt right to surmise, in his programme notes, that it may have been intended as table music for one of the Archbishop's banquets, as each phrase of Mozart is unlikely ever to have been bettered by Sándor Végh and his players.

Writing in the Viennese daily Die Presse on 26th August 1986, Edith Jachimowitz concluded that "anyone who wants to form a concrete idea of the catchword of the 'Klangrede' recently applied to Végh and the Camerata Academica should on no account miss this evening's concert in the Felsenreitschule, where each phrase of which affords audible proof of a lifetime's experience on the part of a conductor who for nearly half a century had led the Végh Quartet. It was this that left such an indelible mark on the partnership between Sándor Végh and the Camerata Academica, producing something utterly incomparable: even in the great symphonies of Mozart and Haydn, the players listened to each other in the true spirit of chamber music, making music together and breathing as a single organism."
