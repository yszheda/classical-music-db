## 内页 2-3 - 专辑注释 (Notes by Alan Blyth)

**ROSA PONSELLE AND the NORMA recordings**

Rosa Ponselle was the Metropolitan Opera's pre-eminent interpreter of Verdi's *La Traviata* throughout the inter-war years. She made many other role debuts, but to some extent, she was not wholly satisfied. Like so many artists, Ponselle was aware that the ambience of live theatre to give of her best. That we are able to be so fully indoctrinated that this privately produced set of highlights from her greatest Verdi role, *La Traviata* has survived, I have every reason to believe that by Alan Blyth's comments on that recorded performance.

Ponselle was born Rosa Ponzillo in Connecticut, on 22nd January 1897 into an immigrant peasant family. Her name was Ponzillo. It was as 'Rosina Ponzillo' that she shared with her older sister Carmela made their professional debut, later to become known as the name. Legend has it that the two sisters, Valentin speaking soprano and Carmela, who had been sent out by the Metropolitan's manager Giulio Gatti-Casazza, to procure new talent for that opera house. What is certain is that Rosa made her debut there on 15th November, 1918, as Leonora in *La Forza Del Destino*, with Caruso.

The rich timbre of her voice, her powerful stage presence and the dramatic quality inherent in her interpretations soon found favour with the public and she became noted for her interpretations of the great Verdi heroines. It was not only in *Forza*, but in *Aida* and *Il Trovatore*. In 1935, when the issue of many highly successful recordings had established her reputation worldwide fame, she preferred to spend most of her time in New York... though Covent Garden was able to hear her periodically. Her last new role was that of Carmen, in the season of 1935-6, which was not welcomed in some circles. She retired from the stage shortly thereafter; for reasons which remain unknown, she died on her Maryland estate on 25th May 1981.

The *Norma* recordings were made between 1928-9 and must count as among her most successful, though they also serve to underline the unfortunate drudgeries involved in the loss of a great artist. In the days of the acoustic process, the role of Bellini's opera (1831) provided an ideal vehicle for Ponselle's voice. These recordings enable one to hear just why it was that she celebrated an interpreter of this part.

## 内页 4-5 - 专辑注释续 (Notes continued)

Ponselle launches on her exhilarating entrance ("Pace, o mio Dio"), her voice, vibrant, exciting, passionate, is at its most seductive. As the ecstatic sighs at the end of the act are delivered, her entry is heard, serene, entirely justified, as does the little duet with the second tenor before the curtain.

Alfredo is the American tenor Frederick Jagel. Jagel's voice is sensitive in style, always phrasing with intelligence, particularly noticeable at the beginning of Act 2. Still better is the baritone Lawrence Tibbett. His success in this role led to his becoming Violetta's partner at the Met in the title role of the next year, notably over-bearing pompous, but always intensely realized. Violetta's motive is wonderfully expressed as he is with Alfredo. And in Act 3 his love for her is so movingly depicted. When the curtain goes down, Ponselle becomes the loving father thinking only of his daughter's future. His "Un di' all'angelo" is phrased tenderly, beautifully. The individual tragedy of the pair's relationship and their characters are finely and sensitively drawn by both artists. Ponselle catches Ponselle's "Addio del passato" with a resignation that emphasizes her frailty. "Ah! che alla morte" she sings in her mezza-voce on a thread of tone. You may say that neither Violetta nor Alfredo achieves these things, but only Callas in my experience has managed such a tragic intensity as Ponselle, and she only with less assurance. Tibbett, for his part, catches the warning note of "Un di', quando le ceneri" by fine observance of what Verdi wrote, where he could have made it a moment of pure melodrama.

Alfredo's denunciation of Violetta in the second scene of Act 2 is delivered with the right incisiveness and fire. You may find Ponselle's cries here a little shrill, but this only serves to underline her despair as the crowd admire her in all its glory. In her response to Alfredo's arrival with Ponselle's Violetta, Tibbett's is the overwhelming intensity that is a prelude to Violetta's surrendering to him, even if it is somewhat thickened as the recording inevitably recorded distortion. No wonder the Met audience gave her an applauding point.

Alfredo's "Di Provenza il mar" is the most long-sustained line I know. Unlike most baritones, he does not attempt the second verse, changing – with total success – a real *pianissimo* that demonstrates the power of a man suffering extra eloquence, the passionate intensity of his speech objects lesson in legato singing. This leads to a moving conclusion for "ma è alfi in tuo cor anco" the more effective. He is not wretched in his cabaletta. It might be as well to point out here, it is were in parenthesis, that this is very far from the version of the score with every traditional cut made – a pity, but that is the way the work was given everywhere in those days. We must be grateful for what we *do* have.

## 内页 6 - 专辑注释终 (Notes by Alan Blyth - Conclusion)

Rosa Ponselle's Violetta is, justifiably, something of a legend. She sang the role for the first time at Covent Garden on 15 June 1929. Sir Thomas Beecham, who had admired her voice, had never heard anything to surpass her performance, even so, the New York press raved, the critic of the New Statesman, rather grudging in his praise of the Italian's Italian repertory, wrote: "It was difficult to believe that it was she singing the role for the first time. In truth, the scene of the third act she repeats the words 'E strano' four times, each time it was as though she became the mental image at her back of the last note was different. It has also mentioned her 'heavenly modulations of tone' and the flawless quality of her vocal technique. Walter Legge wrote in *The Opera* magazine at the time of Ponselle's eightieth birthday in 1977, that 'her legato was perfect, utterly seamless, and exquisitely modulated, and with a control of breath that leaves only the listener breathless'.

I cite these contemporary witnesses because one should not underrate the evidence of records – yet everything that has been written about Ponselle, particularly about her singing in these two records. One does have to listen to them – and preserve what they have. The original acetates, from which they come, were taken from a broadcast performance in 1935. The basic sound is murky; it sometimes seems as if there was participation in the form of coughs and applause, and the occasional stage noise, but that, I suppose, is obvious. Yet enough can be heard of what Ponselle sang for us to feel in 1935 I should say honest to its finest quality, as well as to the glory of its qualities.

In act 1 we are presented with no fading flower of a Violetta, but a woman who is excited by her party and by the love of the man she loves. Her joy at being immediately clear in the duet with Alfredo is evident, that it verges on the hysterical.

The musical highlights are Alfredo's brindisi – the technical demands of which are met with a spirit voice such as Ponselle's; her at once evident in her duet with Alfredo; the ease and grace and steadiness of tone above the stave, her "Ah, forse e lui" which is also applicable here (only two, of course, of the traditional cuts made); "Un di' amore"; with its note of passionate declaration and "Addio del Passato". Then in the aria "Ah, forse e lui"... and the scene that follows, confirmed, but there is much more to be said about the music. One feels the woman's coming with the man she loves, the love that she asked for by Verdi. Then the recitative "Alfio, la morte di Violetta" is vividly followed to its close. We may regret the length at its close before

Violetta's hopeless plight. "Addio del passato" is sung as delicately and marked and phrased though with an unerring sense of how to weight the final high A sung full out and the final phrase works a "fil di voce" here but few could equal.

The remaining moments of happiness when Alfredo returns are brief, for they lead inexorably to then she rises to the fear of death. "Ah, Gran Dio! morir si giovane" – kicking against the pricks – her heart, though imprisoned, almost crossing as she reaches that high note. Then serenity comes into the voice for "E strano!" – for Violetta is again poised, secure) as she passes into her state of remembrance to her future life. The sounds of the world and its images recovery, are sung with an exultant "E salva", a joyful elevation. A marvellous end to an unforgettable performance. Alan Blyth has described Ponselle's singing here as "incredibly lovely"... just so.

The booklet is praised by the conductor, Ettore Panizza. Trained in the Italian tradition, his direction has the tautness and excitement that Verdi required. He is abetted by his singers, but, unlike his great conductor, he let the chorus and singers when they required him to.
