## 艺术家传记 (HIPPO_20241218_0004.jpg)

**BELLA DAVIDOVICH**

Born July 16, 1928 in Baku, Russia.

Bella Davidovich began studying piano at the age of six. Her progress was so astonishing that by the age of nine, she had made her public debut performing a Beethoven concerto.

In 1947, she enrolled at the Moscow Conservatory, where she studied under Konstantin Igumnov and Yakov Flier. In 1949, she won first prize in the Chopin Competition in Warsaw, establishing an active career as a soloist in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe. In 1962, she became professor of piano at the Moscow Conservatory. Davidovich gave her first concert in Western Europe in 1967 (in the Netherlands) and in 1971, she was permitted to tour Italy.

In 1977, her son, noted violinist Dmitry Sitkovetsky, defected to the United States, leading the Soviet authorities to cancel all of her foreign concert engagements. She herself was able to leave the Soviet Union in 1978 and became an American citizen six years later. She made her American debut at Carnegie Hall in 1979, and she soon became a much sought-after soloist in the United States, Canada, and Europe, noted for her interpretations of the Romantic repertoire.

In 1983, she was appointed to a professorship at the Juilliard School. In 1988, she and her son returned to Russia as some of the first emigrés to be invited to perform there during Gorbachev's period of Perestroika. Davidovich has appeared as soloist with the world's leading orchestras, including the New York Philharmonic, the Philadelphia Orchestra, the Boston Symphony Orchestra, the Cleveland Orchestra, the Los Angeles Philharmonic, the National Symphony Orchestra, the San Francisco Symphony Orchestra, the Toronto Symphony, the Montreal Symphony Orchestra, the Detroit Symphony Orchestra, the Houston Symphony, and the Pittsburgh Symphony Orchestra. She has collaborated with the Borodin Quartet, Guarneri Quartet, and Tokyo String Quartet. She has also served on the juries of the Queen Elisabeth and Chopin International Piano Competitions.

## 叙事曲介绍 (HIPPO_20241218_0005.jpg)

# BALLADES

*"I indicate, it's up to the listener to complete the picture"*
- Chopin -

Of all the great 19th century piano cycles, few have generated more misguided speculation than the ballades. Yet understand their being, and you have the key to their soul. A quartet of temporally fluid individuals conceptually similar yet neither definably nor repetitively alike ... a folio of inspirations self-contained and self-sufficient ... hybrid fusions hewn out of sonata and rondo, variation and fantasy ... entities independent yet bound together by common ties of dynastic or metric allegiance (6/4, 6/8) ... freely-imagined edificies, dramatic in voltage - are what they are. Dreamlands of the phantasmagoric ... invocations of Mickiewicz poems (construed from a far from specific remark of Schumann's concerning the genesis of the Second), salon soufflés ... lullabies of the boudoir - are what they are not.

**No 1 G minor Op 23 (1835-06)** In one of his letters Schumann calls this 'the most pleasing but not the cleverest' of Chopin's works, on another occasion praising it for its wildness and innovation. The colourfully adjectival American James Huneker likened its 'dramatic expression' to a 'whirlwind'. The Odyssey of Chopin's soul. That cello-like largo with its noiseless suspension [of key] stays us for a moment in the courtyard of Chopin's House Beautiful. Then, told in his most dreamy tones, the legend begins. As in some fabulous tales of the Genii this Ballade discloses surprising and delicious things. There is the tall lily in the fountain that nods to the sun. It drips in cadenced monotone and its song is repeated on the lips of the slender-hipped girl with the eyes of midnight [...] With such composition any programme could be sworn to [...]

**No 2 F major Op 38 (1836-39)** Dedicated to Schumann. A fusion of nocturne and etude, the Second Ballade is a work famous for its dramatic contrasts of idea and tempo (a gondolier-like barcarolle/siciliano refrain offsetting turbulent sequences). A unique, enigmatic declaration about

neither a Mickiewicz lake nor wild flowers tossed by the wind (the 'rustic maiden/knight' scenario of Anton Rubinstein's vision), it shares with the Op 31 Scherzo and Op 49 Fantasy an innovative 'progressive tonality' plan (pastorale F major buttressed against angst A minor).

**No 3 A flat major Op 47 (1840/41)** An arch-like sonata/waltz creation, a mercurial kaleidoscope of thoughts now here now gone, a scene coloured by images delicate and inflamed, private and public, the A flat Ballade belongs among Chopin's happiest works. 'Most original', reviewed Schumann: 'The finely intellectual Pole, accustomed to move in the most courtly circles of the French capital, will be distinctly recognized. Of the perfumed occasion of the first public performance - at a society concert with the singer Pauline Viardot, 21 February 1842 - *La france musicale* reported: 'And so Chopin has given in Pleyel's rooms a charming soirée, a fête peopled with adorable smiles, delicate and rosy faces, small, shapely white hands; a splendid fête where simplicity was wedded to grace and elegance, and good taste served as a pedestal to wealth. Gilded ribbons, soft blue gauzes, strings of trembling pearls, the freshest roses and mignonettes, in a word, a thousand of the prettiest and gayest hues, mixed and crossed in endless ways on the perfumed heads and silver-white shoulders of the most charming women for whom the princely salons contend.'

**No 4 F minor Op 52 (1842)** In this 'Chopin reaches his full stature as the unapproachable genius of the pianoforte, a master of rich and subtle harmony and, above all, a poet - one of those whose vision transcends the confines of nation and epoch, and whose mission it is to share with the world some of the beauty that is revealed to them alone' (Arthur Hedley). A masterly deformation of sonata form', Abraham ventured. A 'counterpoint', says Samson, balanced between 'directional' sonata aesthetic and 'static' variation procedure, with added elements of slow waltz, barcarolle, siciliano and chorale. A Bach/Beethoven 'homage and emulation', Forty Eight/Appassionata derived (Nigel Nettheim). High among the splendours of Chopin's art.

## 即兴曲介绍 (HIPPO_20241218_0006.jpg)

# IMPROMPTUS

*'It was [...] an unforgettable picture to see [Chopin] sitting at the piano like a clairvoyant, lost in his dreams; to see how his vision communicated itself through his playing, and how at the end of a piece,he had the sad habit of running one finger over the length of the plaintive keyboard, as though to tear himself forcibly away from his dream'*
- Robert Schumann -

*'With all the freedom of an improvisation'* (Huneker), *'as though born under the fingers of the performer'* (Cortot), the cross-referenced impromptus are outwardly slight - fragments of ballade and nocturne, flights of 'open' variation, wraiths of prelude, contained within the ternary bounds (though not double-bars) of the Bohemian/Viennese Biedermeier school at its most direct.

**No 1 A flat major Op 29 (1837)** Chopin's 'productions are not alike,' upheld Schumann. 'The trunk is indeed the same, but its fruits vary wonderfully in growth and flavour.' [This] impromptu [...] is so refined in form, its cantilena is from beginning to end so enclosed in charming figuration; it is [...] so unique an impromptu that it cannot be placed beside any other of his compositions'. Spontaneous and graceful triplets (effectively 12/8), the left hand beats here detached there held, with a central trio section in F minor (2/2) inhabiting the climate of the same-key nocturne from Op 55.

**No 2 F sharp major Op 36 (1839)** A lyric andantino (transmoded from the middle episode of the First Impromptu), distinguished by a grandly climactic, dotted rhythm D major march-trio, a false return in F major, and a closing variation-reprise whose triplets and purling runs have always been the plus ultra of refined pianism. Frederick Niecks (1888) felt an extra-musical programme was needed to justify the middle section; likewise, he could have added, the curious F major side-railing with its bizarre flanking modulations. Chopin (inevitably) left no clues.

**No 3 G flat major Op 51 (1842)** 'Faded [...] ophidian curves in triplets [...] with interludes of double-notes, in colouring tropical and rich to morbidity [...] allied to delicacy of design [is] a strangeness of sentiment - that strangeness which Edgar Poe declared should be a constituent element of all great art' (Huneker). An 'occasional piece' (Chopin). Two versions exist - the earlier without the later's double-notes. Research in the 1980s wondered if the revision prior to publication (spring 1843) could have been influenced by Chopin's Transylvanian protégé Károly Filtsch - who around this time, aged twelve, showed him an impromptu he'd modelled in the same key with double-notes.

**Fantaisie-Impromptu C sharp minor Op 66 (1835)** Earliest and most famous of the four, this was not published until after the composer's death, the 'Fantaisie' prefix at the instigation possibly of his friend and editor, Julius Fontana. Why it was withheld by Chopin is unclear. One opinion (Hedley's) is that it was 'derived', plagiarised even, from Moscheles's E flat Impromptu Op 89, printed in January 1834 in the same Paris anthology as Chopin's second set of nocturnes, Op 15. Another that it drew too conceptually on Beethoven's Moonlight Sonata - principally, the agitato finale, secondarily the opening adagio. The link between the coda and the end of Beethoven's first movement is unmissible, in both examples the thematic core (in Chopin's case the plaintive D flat song of his slower central episode) being transferred bass-wards to strong emotional effect.

## 前奏曲介绍 (HIPPO_20241218_0007.jpg)

# PRELUDES

*'[Chopin] lived in the nightmare world of legends. Ghosts called to him and embraced him, and [...] he would thrust their emaciated faces away from his own, and struggle from under the grip of their icy hands'*
- George Sand -

Op 28 (1836-39). 'Sketches, the beginnings of studies [...] ruins, eagles' feathers, all wildly, variegatedly intermingled' (Schumann). 'Poetic preludes, analogous to those of a great contemporary poet [Lamartine], who cradles the soul in golden dreams, and elevates it to the region of the ideal' (Liszt). Impromptus, etudes, morceaux de la nuit. The 'pearls' of Chopin's art (Anton Rubinstein), 'While charming your ear,' mused Sand, witness to their genesis, they yet 'break your heart.' Twenty-four confessional epigrams contrasted in tempo, preludes to meditation (André Gide), 'preluding' the keys, the cycle travel a circle of ascending fifths from C to F sharp [G flat] to F, each major paired with its relative minor a third below.

**No 1 C major** The 'passionate breathing' of Schumann's perception, the 'lovely, quiet wave' of Gide's. The last to be composed (January 1839).
**No 2 A minor** Harmonically black, the home key long delayed - to be 'played in a whisper for oneself alone' (Gide).
**No 3 G major** A study in brillante left-hand work, floating by 'like thistle-down on a summer breeze' (Jonson).
**No 4 E minor** 'Our sweetest songs are those that tell of saddest thoughts' (Shelley).
**No 5 D major** 'Chopin at his happiest, spinning his finest, his most irridescent web' (Huneker).
**No 6 B minor** 'Precipitating the soul into frightful depression' (Sand), and, with No 4, played at Chopin's funeral in the Madeleine.
**No 7 A major** A wraith of mazurka, the shortest of the series. The first to be written, along with No 17 (1836).
**No 8 F sharp minor** Anguished tension and swirling right-hand decoration, 'a brilliant bird flying over the horrors of an abyss' (Baudelaire), cadencing in a bitter-sweet oscillation of major and minor.
**No 9 E major** Low-register dotted and double-dotted funeral rhythms against a triplet accompaniment gravely trodden.
**No 10 C sharp minor** Purling descents, 'the whirling entrance of a ballerina' (Thomas Higgins).
**No 11 B major** Lyrically lined Chopin, more vivacious in spirit than speed. **No 12 G sharp minor** A heavyweight, physically tricky in its repeated note patterns - like a summer storm on Lake Como (Jonson).
**No 13 F sharp major** Nocturnal sighs.
**No 14 E flat minor** Dynamically swelling and fading triplet unisons, prophetically Famenl March finale style (see Sonatas, No 2).
**No 15 D flat major** [Raindrop] A fantasy on one note (A flat/G sharp), in the central minor 'the shades of dead monks seeming to rise and pass before the listener in solemn and gloomy funeral pomp' (Sand).
**No 16 B flat minor** A fiery study of relentless right-hand semiquavers, creating an inferno of sound and dissonance.
**No 17 A flat major** An emotionally expansive gondoliera of noble climax, its luxuriantly nostalgic coda built around 'the sound of an old clock in the castle which strikes the eleventh hour' (the composer's Franco-Russian pupil Camille Dubois).
**No 18 F minor** Operatic declamation, boldy unisons, harmonically vibrant.
**No 19 E flat major** Wide stretches, plucked basses, the melody like stars picked from the heavens.
**No 20 C minor** Jane Stirling used to call this *The Prayer*, its 'celestial'-sounding chords 'an aspiration [...] extended into eternity'.
**No 21 B flat major** A dialogue between contrasted registers and keys.
**No 22 G minor** Germanically-veined temper, dominated by hammering left-hand octaves.
**No 23 F major** Delicate and rippling, 'like a sun-shot spider-web oscillating in the breeze of summer', the simpering E flat of the penultimate bar beaching the music on a teasing question-mark.
**No 24 D minor** A venomously impassioned set-piece. Beethoven Appassionata linked, its angst, rocketing runs, lead-shot descents, searing chromatic thirds and 'revolutionary' rhetoric abruptly silenced in 'the loud diapason of the cannonade' (bottom D), 'the clangour of overthrown reason,' with which it so terrifyingly ends.

**Ates Orga**
