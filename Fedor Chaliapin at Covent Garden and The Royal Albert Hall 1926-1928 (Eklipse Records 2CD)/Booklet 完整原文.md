## 内页 1：历史介绍 (Historical Introduction)

**FEDOR CHALIAPIN AT COVENT GARDEN AND THE ALBERT HALL 1926-1928**

**Live Recordings at Covent Garden 1926-1928**

With the invention of the microphone and electric recording in 1925 it occurred to Fred Gaisberg that it would be possible to record actual performances in the opera house. He began negotiations with Gatti-Casazza at the MET, Signor Scandiani at La Scala, Monsieur Roncé at the Opéra and Colonel Bliss at Covent Garden. However royalties, copyright and consent of the artists presented such complications that in the end only Covent Garden and Berlin participated. A pair of recording machines were allowed to be set up in a small room near the stage at Covent Garden and, as Fred Gaisberg wrote: "One would be revolving and receiving the sound while the other was being hooked up. The actual recording would be in charge of a chief operator. In close touch with him by telephone would be a 'monitor', usually a good musician, seated behind a switch-board, and spread out before him a score of the opera in which 'cuts' and probable time duration of records was marked off. There were further complications which resulted in the truncated pieces that were actually recorded. It was not possible to record an artist who had a contract other than with HMV. Thus Charles Hackett, who was under contract to Columbia, could not be heard singing with Melba. This had as a result that the Act 2 duet of 'Romeo et Juliette' which was performed on Melba's farewell was left unrecorded, even though the equipment was set up to do so. Similarly Lotte Lehmann, who sang Desdemona in Zezanella's Otello, could not be heard as she was under contract with Pathéphone. Thus the love duet in Act 1 was not recorded. Furthermore it seemed to the engineers at the time that applause or any other extraneous noise other than the singers could not sound on these records. Consequently great energy was then expended trying to catch the enthusiastic audience's response to the artists.

It was Fedor Chaliapin's honour to be the first opera singer to be electrically recorded during a live performance in an opera house. The series of five recordings included on this compact disc began almost as an experiment on May 26, 1926. Of the nine sides that were recorded of Boito's "Mefistofele" only six were published at the time. However the result was good enough to warrant further recordings and on June 4 a public performance of "La Boheme" with Margaret Sheridan as Mimi was recorded. All of the nine sides were made although they remain unpublished, except for two excerpts that have subsequently appeared on LP. The "Boheme" recordings were intended as a dress rehearsal for what the recording company really set out to record: Nellie Melba's farewell performances at Covent Garden on 8 June 1926. Out of the 11 sides recorded only 3 were published at the time. However of all the recordings made between 1926 and 1928 Chaliapin, in retrospect, is the artist who benefits most. In total excerpts from four operas were recorded featuring the artist and it is of him that we are able to form the clearest picture of what a "Chaliapin" performance was actually like. It is regrettable that the live recording project was not continued after 1928 but these discs offer, apart from the Mapleson cylinders for an earlier era, a sound picture of a period when artists like Melba, Zenatello and Chaliapin held their audience in thrall.
