## SCHUMANN AND THE LIED: A PERSONAL VIEW

Robert Schumann never did anything by halves. Perhaps conscious that as a composer he knew the piano but little else, in the years 1840-42 he embarked on extraordinary crash courses of self-education which, far from leading to a series of dry, didactic compositions, produced some of his greatest, most spontaneous works. The year 1841 was symphonic and 1842 was devoted to chamber music; but what concerns us here is 1840. Despite the continuing intrigues of his beloved Clara's ghastly father Friedrich Wieck, Robert managed to marry her and had to immerse himself in poetry and song, to the extent that he turned out the following superb set of two cycles of Lieder: Opp. 24, 27, 29, 30, 31, 33, 35, 36, 39, 40, 42, 45, 48, 49 and 51. As if the symphonic and chamber music years were equally productive, it is no wonder that by 1843 he was mentally exhausted and had to take a break from composition.

In his enormous enthusiasm, backed up by his considerable skill, Schumann more or less invented the Romantic Lied, just as Schubert had virtually defined the Classical Lied. The two cycles featured here, *Dichterliebe* (Poet's Love) for a male voice and *Frauenliebe und -leben* (A Woman's Love and Life) for a female voice, are perhaps the most personal products of 1840. That they are of differing statures artistically is not Schumann's fault, unless you want to blame him for choosing Adalbert von Chamisso's rather sentimental verses in the first place. Once you accept the words as a given, it is hard to fault Schumann's settings; and it is wonderful to have a song cycle which belongs unequivocally to the female voice and expresses a woman's point of view. But of course *Dichterliebe* is in a different class. In setting the bitter-sweet, ironic and, above all, concise utterances of Heinrich Heine, Schumann met his Romantic match. And Heine found his ideal composer.

It is salutary to note how Schumann repeats phrases of the poems—and sometimes whole verses—in the case of *Frauenliebe*—to make a dramatic point or to intensify emotion, especially when we contemplate the fact that Heinrich Heine, had he been born a century later, would certainly have been murdered by Hitler. Did Hüsch and Müller see the irony in their choice of repertoire? Having made those points, we may be thankful that the German pair were able to record the cycle at all—they had to come to London to do it. Gerhard Hüsch (1901-84) had the loveliest voice among German baritones of the last century, with the possible exception of Herbert Janssen, and like Schütz was able to convey the beauty of a song without making a meal of it. Schumann seems to have had that talent in mind for *Dichterliebe*, but his taste for music does mean that he might have been better served with a simpler tone than Panzera's. Hüsch manages to keep the phrases airborne—and the extra heft is helpful in the rather hard-won German speech-rhythm articulation the words in a supple and palpable advantage. His plaintive "later" to be killed in an Allied air raid—is not in Moore's claus but is alert and alive to the singer's moods.

Sincerity and intensity are vital assets for any interpreter of *Frauenliebe und -leben*, if the words are not to sound slightly laughable in places. Fortunately those two attributes are possessed in full measure by the three grand ladies who portray Schumann's and Chamisso's devoted heroine here. Somewhat surprisingly, perhaps because she had a long affair as a presentee of masterclasses, Lotte Lehmann (1888-1976) is discussed in the history of lieder singing only in relation to her career; she showed relatively little interest in the Lied. True, there were some recordings, but they almost invariably featured small orchestras or ensembles and were far from rich. Her first version of *Frauenliebe*, made in 1928, had Frieder Weissmann conducting an orchestra to what is arguably the most over-romantic score that can be heard in the United States. Lehmann took on a new lease of life, giving concerts and broadcasts of Lieder with high-class professional pianists on a regular basis. She appeared regularly in recital at New York's New Friends of Music, where the audience was largely composed of immigrants from central Europe, and she was surely the first to offer an entire programme of Beethoven Lieder in New York. In those last years she championed the cause of her few favored composers, among them the three Bs and Brahms, and the qualities she had brought to her operatic portraits, in fact. Intellectualization of the Fischer-Dieskau kind, of course, was not for her. The warmth, simplicity and honesty before she could bring herself to release it, will not be found in a Lehmann performance. Recorded live at a Town Hall recital for The New Friends in 1946, with Ulanowsky at the piano, she depicts the protagonist of *Frauenliebe* as a woman of intense feeling and vulnerability.

It is difficult to separate the art from the life in the cases of Schütz, Cortot, Hüsch and Müller, as it is impossible when we come to those wonderful contraltos Marian Anderson and Kathleen Ferrier. Both wholly creative tension to her higher notes. Her singing always had "face", perhaps partly a legacy of her early career as a telephone operator—how one would have liked to hear her speaking voice in that role. As with Lehmann and Anderson, it is the inner identification with the character portrayed in *Frauenliebe* that ultimately matters with Ferrier. She means every note, every syllable. She encompasses every mood from joy to the tragedy of the final song, where her weight of tone is a real asset, and behind the purely musical manifestations, we sense her utter sincerity. In all of this, the Haller had her committed accompanist.

My own shot of the Schumann recordings does not stop with these interpretations, of course. I would not be without the *Dichterliebe* by Peter Schreier and Norman Shetler, for instance, or the few songs we have from Richard Tauber. Fischer-Dieskau and Jörg Demus make a powerful team in the baritone class. For *Frauenliebe und -leben*, there are Elly Ameling with Dalton Baldwin, Irmgard Seefried with Erik Werba, and Edith Mathis with Christoph Eschenbach. Or we can turn to Janet Baker at her most intense. But the six interpretations in the present set of compact discs present a valid and fascinating picture of Schumann singing in the 1930s and 1940s and are of much more than mere "historic" interest.
