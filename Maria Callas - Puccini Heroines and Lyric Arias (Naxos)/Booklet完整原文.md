## Introduction

1954 was for Callas an exceptionally busy year. As well as appearing regularly at La Scala, Milan, and elsewhere in Italy she made her American debut at the Chicago Lyric in November. Her September 1954 Watford Town Hall sessions featured on this recording were released on two albums, one devoted to Puccini arias, not without blemishes, and the second featuring a selection entitled *Coloratura Lyric Arias*, which remains justly famous to this day.

In this recording, Callas revolutionises performance practice: she not only correctly sings all the written notes, but she understands implicitly what the dramatic situation calls for in her treatment of the cadenzas at the end of each verse. Throughout the remarkable length of her phrases, her choice of vocal colours, her command of rubato and her dynamic range reveal much more than one had thought the music contained.

Producer's Note: Some distortion in loud passages and a few extraneous noises are inherent in the original master tapes, and this is a function of the French LP sources used for the present transfer. Tracks 12 through 16 are the "lyric" portion of Callas' 1954 recital LP; the "coloratura" portions have already appeared on Naxos Historical 8.111028-29.

Maria Callas (1923-1977): This time she had slimmed down and her hair was dyed blonde. She was quickly becoming the most famous opera singer of her day, and her peculiar vocal skills were helping to revive an almost forgotten repertory. Two months before she recorded this recital, sessions began on 15th September and lasted until 21st September. One album was devoted to Puccini arias, the other to a medley of selections entitled *Coloratura Lyric Arias*. Unlike her other EMI recordings these were made not at La Scala, Milan, but in London, and not at Covent Garden, but at Watford Town Hall, a suburban venue some twenty miles to the north west, used by Walter Legge as a recording studio; he produced them. The accompaniment is provided by the Philharmonia Orchestra, Legge's house orchestra, conducted by Tullio Serafin.

Among those rejected, which she already knew or had sung, were 'Divinité infernale' from Gluck's *Alceste*, 'Bel raggio' from Rossini's *Semiramide*, 'Selva opaca' from Guglielmo Tell, 'Io son Titania' from Thomas's *Mignon* and Musetta's Waltz Song, which she never recorded but sang in concert later in her career? Why then should he have wasted time recording the arias when she might have included other excerpts. The Puccini arias include selections from *Gianni Schicchi* and *Suor Angelica*, as well as those from *Manon Lescaut*, *La Bohème*, *Madama Butterfly* and *Turandot*, but there is no *Vissi d'arte* from *Tosca*.

Other excerpts include two arias from Cilea's *Adriana Lecouvreur* 'Io son l'umile ancella' and 'Poveri fiori'. Callas does not begin the first of these, as many singers would, with the piece of declamation from Racine which Adriana has to speak before beginning 'Ecco: Respiro ancella'. Her interpretation benefits from her remarkably expansive breath span.

Many of the London critics when the Puccini album was issued in December were not slow to note the remarkably expansive breath span and her control of the line and moulding of the phrases reveal just how accurately she resolves the trills to the upper note as written), but also shaping them with her customary rhythmic freedom. Throughout the remarkable length of her phrases, her choice of vocal colours, her command of rubato and her dynamic range reveal much more than one had thought the music contained. It becomes not just a verismo aria, as sung in the fashion of, say, Muzio and Tebaldi: Callas makes us aware it is one of the last Mad Scenes.

Tullio Serafin (1878-1968), born at Rottanova di Cavarzere, near Venice, was one of the great conductors of Italian opera. After studying at the Milan Conservatory, at first he was a violinist in the orchestra part, then in 1900 at Ferrara began a career as conductor. Engagements followed in Turin and Rome, appeared at Covent Garden, London (1907, 1931, 1959-60), La Scala, Milan (1910-1914, 1917, 1918, 1940, 1946-7), Colón, Buenos Aires (1914, 1919, 1920, 1928, 1937, 1938, 1949, 1951), San Carlo, Naples (1922-3, 1940-1, 1949-58), Metropolitan, New York (1924-34), Rome Opera (1934-43, 1962), Lyric Opera, Chicago (1955, 1957-58), and numerous other opera houses in Italy and abroad. His repertory was vast. He conducted conventional and unconventional operas as well as introducing a variety of new works, and worked with numerous famous singers, including Battistini, Chaliapin, Ponselle, Gigli, Callas and Sutherland. His numerous famous recording career was exhaustive and embraced the HMV (1939) Verdi Requiem as well as both Angel/Columbia Normas (1954 and 1960) with Callas.

Here she revolutionises performance practice by not only correctly singing all the written notes, but she also understands implicitly what the dramatic situation calls for in her treatment of the cadenzas at the end of each verse. She makes something quite extraordinary out of these, not only singing them correctly but shaping them with her customary rhythmic freedom.
