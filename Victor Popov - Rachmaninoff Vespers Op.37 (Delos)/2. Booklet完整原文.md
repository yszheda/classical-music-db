### Page 2

Rachmaninoff wrote Vespers in Moscow in the winter of 1915. He had conceived the work in the early 1900s, but took only two weeks to complete the score. The composer recalled in his Memoirs: "Even as a child, I was fascinated by the magnificent chants from the Obikhod (Book of Canticles of the Russian Church). I have always felt that their particular character needed choral adaptation, and have cherished the hope of achieving this in Vespers."

For centuries, evolving in the bosom of the Orthodox Church, Russian melody accumulated an immense spiritual wealth, nourishing many generations. Throughout the 19th Century Russian composers sought to create a many-voiced style in line with the nature of Old-Russian church singing. Rachmaninoff, too, created his Vespers as an adaptation of authentic church melodies.

But Rachmaninoff's masterpiece is stunning for its novelty. By combining Old-Russian church chants, treasured for their spiritual wealth, and his inner artistic world, Rachmaninoff discovered a "philosopher's stone" of his own. He surrounded the ascetic with light, air, and chiming bells; with colorful frescoes of sublime and touchingly simple subjects. The two worlds merged in a wonderful unity.

Vespers was first performed on March 10, 1915, in Moscow by the Synodal School Choir. Later on, the concert destiny of Vespers was not a particularly happy one. Church restrictions on concerts of sacred music, the events of the World War, the Revolution and later, Soviet ideology put obstacles in the path of allowing the people access to this masterpiece. Prior to the 60-year hiatus, its last concert performance took place in 1926 at the Great Hall of the Moscow Conservatory.

Though banished from the concert hall, this great work went on to live in the Orthodox Church. Thus, in the '60s and '70s, there was a tradition to perform Vespers on Easter Eve in one of the churches across the Moscow River. In 1982, Vespers returned to the concert halls, and the traditions of concert performance of this work were developed anew by the best choirs.

The Academy of Choral Art, Moscow was founded in 1991 by **Victor Popov**, as a perpetuation and modernization of the Moscow Choral College, established in 1944 by Alexander Sveshnikov and led since 1970 by Professor Popov.

The Academy, like the Choral College before it, specializes in reviving early Russian religious music, continuing the 1000-year tradition of Russian choral singing, and presenting music and chant of both Orthodox and Western Christian traditions. However, the choir is equally at home performing contemporary choral works, by composers such as Shostakovich, Kikta and Dmitriev, as well as choral arrangements of Russian folk songs. Today the Academy includes some 400 singers, with a separate Boys' Chorus, Youth Chorus, Mixed Adult Chorus and Men's Chorus.

The Academy choir has appeared in numerous high-profile festivals and concerts in Russia, Austria, Germany and France, appears frequently on radio and TV, and has produced more than 40 CDs.

The Academy Choir has sung under the direction of guest conductors such as Igor Markevich, Hermann Abendroth, Evgeny Svetlanov, Vladimir Spivakov, Gennady Rozhdestvensky, Dmitri Kitayenko, Vladimir Fedoseyev, Helmuth Rilling, Rudolf Barshai, Thomas Sanderling, Alexander Rudin, Mikhail Pletnev, and Yury Temirkanov. The choir has also worked with featured soloists such as Montserrat Caballé, Maria Guleghina, Angela Gheorghiu and Roberto Alagna.
