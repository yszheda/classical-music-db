## 内页说明

Rachmaninoff wrote Vespers in Moscow in the winter of 1915. He had conceived the work in the early 1900s, but took only two weeks to complete the score. The composer recalled in his Memoirs: "Even as a child, I was fascinated by the magnificent chants from the Obikhod (Book of Canticles) of the Russian Church. I have always felt that their particular character needed choral adaptation, and have cherished the hope of achieving this in Vespers."

For centuries, evolving in the bosom of the Orthodox Church, Russian monody accumulated an immense spiritual wealth, nourishing many generations. Throughout the 19th Century Russian composers sought to create an always richer liturgical music by harmonizing traditional Russian church singing. Rachmaninoff, too, created his Vespers as an adaptation of authentic church melodies.

But Rachmaninoff's masterpiece is stunning for its novelty: By combining Old-Russian church chants, treasured for their spiritual wealth, and his inner artistic ideals, Rachmaninoff discovered a "philosopher's stone" of his own. He surrounded the ascetic with light and air, and clothed the holy melodies in robes of sublime and touchingly simple subjects. The two worlds merged in a wonderful unity.

Vespers was first performed on March 10, 1915, in Moscow by the Synodal School Choir. Later on, the concert destiny of Vespers was not a particularly happy one. Church restrictions on concerts of sacred music, the onset of the World War, the Revolution, and the consequent ideology put obstacles in the path of allowing the people access to this masterpiece. Prior to the 60-year hiatus, its last concert performance took place in 1926 at the Great Hall of the Moscow Conservatory.

Though banished from the concert hall, this great work went on to live in the Orthodox Church. Thus, in the '60s and '70s, there was a tradition to perform Vespers on Easter Eve in one of the churches across the Moscow River. In 1982, Vespers returned to the concert hall and the traditions of concert performance of this work were developed anew by the best choirs.

*"The Academy Choir directed by Victor Popov is a unique phenomenon. There is no other such choir in the world!"* — Rudolf Barshai, 2002

*"…Vocal plenitude and tenderness, rhythmic energy, perfect ensemble, precise and convincing diction by the choirmaster — this all witnessed not only a spiritual community of performers, but also their participation in the great national culture, one of whose elevating forms is Orthodox church singing."* — Ilya Musin, 1993

The Academy of Choral Art, Moscow was founded in 1991 by Victor Popov, as a perpetuation and modernization of the Moscow Choral College, established in 1944 by Alexander Svechnikov and led since 1970 by Professor Popov.

The Academy, like the Russian Choral College, specializes in reviving early Russian religious music, continuing the 1000-year tradition of Russian choral singing, and preserving and sharing the chant and body of Orthodox culture. At the same time, however, the choir is equally at home performing contemporary choral works, by composers such as Shostakovich, Kitka and Dmitriev, as well as choral arrangements of Russian folk songs.

Today the choir numbers 110 singers, some singing with a separate Boys' Chorus, Youth Chorus, Mixed Adult Chorus and Men's Chorus.

The Academy Choir has appeared in numerous high-profile festivals and concerts in Russia, Italy, Germany and France. It has also appeared on radio and TV, and has produced more than 40 CDs.

The Academy Choir has sung under the direction of guest conductors such as Igor Markevitch, Hermann Abendroth, Evgeny Svetlanov, Vladimir Spivakov, Gennady Rozhdestvensky, Dmitri Kitaenko, Vladimir Fedoseyev, Helmuth Rilling, Rudolf Barshai, Thomas Dausgaard, Alexander Ivashkin, Mikhail Pletnev and Yuri Temirkanov. The choir has also worked with featured soloists such as Montserrat Caballé, Maria Guleghina, Angela Gheorghiu and Roberto Alagna.
