## Mary Garden: Career and Roles

Mary Garden sang from 1900 to 1934, the year in which she made her farewell at the Opéra Comique as Katiuchka in Alfano's *Résurrection*. She appeared at the Opéra Comique from 1900 to 1906 and then more occasionally from 1930 to 1934, was invited to Covent Garden, became one of the "sensations" of the brilliant but ephemeral Manhattan Opera in New York from 1907 to 1910 and settled finally in Chicago from 1010 to 1930, a company which she directed even during the season of 1919-20.

Amongst other things she passionately defended the "contemporary" French repertoire which she had made her own at the beginning of her career, in addition to the more traditional repertoire of lyric soprano and later almost mezzo (Marguerite in *Faust*, *La Traviata*, Antonia in *Tales of Hoffmann*, *Carmen* etc).

Of Scottish origin and born in Aberdeen on 20 February 1874, she grew up in Chicago where she obtained a scholarship from a wealthy patron to study in Paris. She became the protégée of Sibyl Sanderson who got her engaged at the Opéra Comique by Albert Carré in 1900.

Very soon Carré took the risk of asking her to replace Marthe Rioton, the creator of the role of Louise who was indisposed during performance, and she established herself immediately as a singer of strong and attractive personality with a seductive and nuanced voice. her feeling for French diction was profound and her coloring of text particularly subtle and varied. So it was that she seduced not only Charpentier but soon Debussy and Massenet who entrusted their works to her without hesitation.

*Mélisande* (in 1902 with Jean Périer and Dufranne), *Chérubin* created at Monte Carlo then repeated in Paris with Marguerite Carré and Lina Cavalieri, revivals of *Thaïs*, *Sapho*, *Hérodiade*, *Grisélidis* or *Manon* and even the role of Jean in *Jongleur de Notre Dame* which she was able to adapt for her voice with Massenet's approval to recreate it in America, at the Manhattan Opera House in 1908.

But in Paris, she also engaged herself in promoting the works of Gabriel Pierné (*La Fille de Tabarin* with Fugère), of *Aphrodite* by Erlanger which had a resounding success in France partly thanks to her, of *La Reine Fiammette* by Xavier Leroux with Marguerite Carré, but also of *Monna Vanna* and *Gismonda* by Henry Février (respectively revived and created in the USA in Chicago in 1913 and in 1919), and finally *Salomé* by Richard Strauss which created a sensation in New York at the Manhatten Opera and even *The Love for Three Oranges* by Prokofiev which she courageously staged in Chicago in 1921.

## Mary Garden: The Voice and the Recordings

Mary Garden had an exceptional career between 1900 and 1934, but her recordings are relatively few in number. In these recordings her voice is extremely personal, recognizable and attractive, a lyric soprano voice with a very natural timbre and open and clear emission, warm in the lower range but not easy to record faithfully in the upper range. Despite everything her discs are unforgettable, they always show her extremely musical involvement with very subtle and nuanced diction both in English and in French.

Her French is not however always very pure and one often detects traces of accent which become even an additional source of charm and seduction, because once again, her sense of diction and phrasing are always perfectly in accord with the language and its poetry, translating grace with naturalness and simplicity.

This explains the very strong impression she made on most composers of the period, including Gustave Charpentier, Debussy, Henri Février, Xavier Leroux, Erlanger or Massenet whom she served in return with exemplary consistency, especially in New York and later in Chicago. It was however always in French that she sang Richard Strauss' *Salome*, Violetta in *La Traviata*, Prokofiev's *The Love of Three Oranges* and finally Katiouchka in Alfano's *Resurrection*, her farewell role together with *Carmen*.

Her recordings do not leave the listener indifferent. They are always very captivating and equally moving, because in revealing her sensibility they do not dissimulate her fragility: we sometimes hear her lightly clearing her throat before singing Debussy or the aria from *Louise*, or even attacking "L'ombre des arbres" too low and correcting herself very quickly with the help of Debussy who reassures her with discreet support afterwards.

In "Il pleure dans mon coeur" we can also hear that instead of the minor second supported by the harmony in the piano she sings a major second on "De ne savoir pourquoi" apparently happily accepted by Debussy who accompanies her without underlining, giving a more luminous and disorienting effect to the start of the phrase. All these elements seem to contradict accounts of Debussy which present him as precise and meticulous with regard to following his indications in the score. In fact they seem to show that he was far more demanding with regard to the correct expression of his music rather than observing the text exactly as written as long as the emotion was precisely that which he had desired. Without doubt the spirit was more important to him than the exact letter.

Let us remember precisely how Mary Garden, protégée of Sibyl Sanderson and creator of *Thaïs*, made her debut at the Opéra Comique during the 23rd performance of *Louise* with Marthe Rioton, who, although ill, had agreed to take the role that she had created so well, because her official cover Catherine Mastio was also ill, but by the second act which begins with the great aria "Depuis le jour" she had no more voice to continue the performance. So it was that Albert Carré, who had noticed the diligence and personality of Mary Garden during rehearsals in which she took a small role, dared ask her if she felt able to take up the performance mid-way and she accepted with fearless determination, starting with Louise's aria and making such a success that it launched her career immediately. It was thus that he thought of presenting her to Debussy with a view to interpreting Mélisande in preference to Georgette Leblanc, Maeterlinck's companion who wanted the role, but who had already sung successfully a very fiery and provocative *Carmen* which had shocked Bizet's widow.

Following this Mary Garden would go on to create the title role in Massenet's *Chérubin* and to gain authorization to take on, en travesti, the role of Jean in *Jongleur de Notre Dame* created in France by Adolphe Maréchal, for the New York premiere.

Nevertheless she was capable of incarnating roles of totally different character such as Aphrodite by Camille Erlanger, Salome by Richard Strauss and in a more ambiguous fashion Massenet's *Thaïs*, the role she chose for her debut in the United States. This debut was delayed because she was without voice for the premiere, but after which she jealously kept exclusivity for "her" role of *Thaïs* during all her New York seasons.

Like many of her contemporaries, Mary Garden had by means of her voice, technique and temperament the possibility to be both sensuous and bewitching and at the same time to give an impression of fragility, modesty and innocence, which made her right for interpreting and singing Marguerite in *Faust*, Juliette or *Louise* as well as *Carmen*, in the tradition of Galli-Marié. But unlike Emma Calvé, Nellie Melba or Georgette Bréjean-Silver, her technique and her attack are more imprecise, and her top notes were not always so focused and free. For this reason she lost them more quickly than those singers trained according to the old school (she transposed the aria from *Louise* in her 1928 recording and sang *Carmen* more often at this time when she abandoned higher roles).

But Mary Garden is above all recognizable, unique, attractive and touching, in a word irreplaceable - as this disc which embraces her whole career proves - thanks to her weaknesses, let us not speak of faults, which she compensates with extremely committed singing and by phrasing which is always expressive and captivating. This phrasing even enables her to give a musical sense to the few vocal shortcuts that she allows herself (shifting the voice towards the high note in *Traviata*, taking extra breath in *Chérubin* Louise). Thus none of her recordings are indifferent or ordinary. Nearly all are unforgettable and successful.

The two versions of *Comin' thro' the Rye* on Pathé and Columbia recorded magnificently, compare with the greatest singers, Patti, Melba, Calvé, Sembrich, Blauvelt, Farrar or Teyte, and each is particularly natural, lively and beautiful, but also indispensable and unique. Hers was always a profound art that never sought to be ostentatious, an art where it seems the artist manages to forget herself even to abandon herself to the work. One is persuaded even better after repeated hearings that are never wearisome, and after which the "faults" that one could have reproached her at first hearing eventually seem necessary and attractive, because sensitive and human. In our opinion, this is the finest compliment one can make this exceptional artist, she asks us to become accustomed to her particular voice but once you do you never cease to love it.

**Jean NIROUET**
