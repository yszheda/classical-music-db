**ANTONINA NEZHDANOVA**

In the great Russian soprano Antonina Nezhdanova (1873-1950) we have a link between Imperial and Soviet vocal art. Brought to the Bolshoi's attention in 1902 by Sobinov, she managed the transition of 1917 with aplomb, always putting Art and the education of her new audiences first. It was fitting that she should in her twilight years have been musical godmother to Kozlovsky, brightest star of the next generation. Here is Nezhdanova herself, sweet-toned, warm and technically brilliant – as soloist, and in duet with Sobinov and Kozlovsky.

### Inner Page 1 - SYNOPSIS OF LESSER-KNOWN SONGS

**10. The Rose and the Nightingale**

The nightingale sings day and night to the rose, but she has lost its scent. Just so, a lover is singing to a maiden, but she does not see him.

*When he sings or why his song is so sad.*

**11. It's pleasant here**

'It's pleasant here – the river shining in the distance, flower-clad meadows, white clouds. No one else, you, I, love, all is quiet – just God and I. Flowers, all an ancient pine-tree and you, my dream, beloved!'

**12. & 13. Ah! Shall I compare you?/Fate has led us**

These are nos. 17 & 6 respectively of Rubinstein's Op. 34 *Twelve Songs* (published 1875, with a text in German as *Sechs Gedichte* by F. Bodenstedt. In each case, a Russian words by Tchaikovsky, differ considerably from the original German). ('Nicht einmal vergleichbar (Himmelslicht)' only one verse of the song is given. 'Shall I compare you to a heavenly angel, or to a rose, queen of flowers, or to the field, or to the bright light of the sun? No, my fair one, in No. 6 a (the famous) (Belien's Ode's) cadenza is here also transplanted between the two verses. Fate has led us along the same path; your image is ever with me. You walk into the ocean of my thoughts, and rise from them like the sun.'

**16. Cradle Song**

Sleep, my lovely little one; lulla, lullaby.

Quietly the moon peeps into my cradle. I'll tell you stories and sing you a song. Your eyes are like the stars and slumber. Lulla lullaby.

**20. I have loved you**

I have loved you and perhaps my love has not completely died. But let it not disturb you further. I do not want anything to sadden you. I have loved you noiselessly and hopelessly. I loved you so sincerely and tenderly and God grant you would appear to be loved as sincerely and tenderly as God grant you be.

**21. When beyond the hill**

A painting by the bards of the dark land on the Volga. The singer hears a handsome boatman light-blue forget-me-not eyes – but of the flowering grove...

**22. The Nightingale**

Nightingale, my nightingale, eloquent nightingale! Whither art thou flying? Where wilt thou pass the night?

### Inner Page 2-3 - ANTONINA NEZHDANOVA (Biography)

Before the emergence of Galina Vishnevskaya, the Soviet Union had not for many years been blessed with a soprano of international stature: when the Eastern bloc in the 1950s opened round to tape recording and the LP, many must have relished the chance to listen when the blush of the first ardour of spring flowers to the ear – or the shrill and acid Voznesenskaya. Local though not the situation is attested by the earlier presence of such fine artists as Maria Mikhailova (1866-1913), Olimpia Boronat (1867-1945) and perhaps the most esteemed of them all, Antonina Vasil'yevna Nezhdanova (1873-1950). The possessor of an artistically pure gift of tone, astonishing technical ability and a wide-ranging sensibility, whose fame lasted from 1890 to 1945 and who made the greater part of her 300 or so.

Antonina Vasil'yevna Nezhdanova was born in Odessa on 4 June 1873 of mixed Russian-Ukrainian descent. Her father worked in the docks and her father had taught her to read music by the time she was six; the young girl subsequently entered grammar school, learning dancing and the visual arts. She thought first to become a language teacher, and then a doctor. However, frequent attendance at Odessa's Civic Theatre, where she heard artists of the calibre of Tetrazzini, Ruffo and Scotti, caused her to be drawn irresistibly to music, and during the mid-nineties she began taking lessons from Zoyla Rubinstein, sister of Nikolai and Anton. Her singing lessons took place at the Moscow Conservatory in 1899-1903, where she was a pupil of Umetsky and later, of course, of Nemetti (who had been the teacher of Stacciat).

Less than three years later, fate gave the young singer a helping hand. The great tenor Leonid Sobinov (1872-1934) heard her as Constant in *Lohengrin* during a conservatory examination performance, and was so impressed that he advised her to sing the role at the Bolshoi at the Theatre. Shortly after that had come to pass, the soprano was cast as the leading lady for the part of Antonia in a certain production. The year 1902 saw her debut in the part of Tatiana in *Eugene Onegin* and her career took off with the greatest success. Thus on 25 April 1902 she began an association with the Bolshoi that was to last for more than a third of a century.

Nezhdanova's career was spent almost entirely within the Russian Empire and the Soviet territories which succeeded it. In 1912 however she was heard as Gilda at Paris and Monte Carlo, in casts which included Caruso and Ruffo. With her enormous repertoire, she eventually came to dominate the operatic world in Russia. She was also a familiar figure on the concert platform, where she introduced many new works by Russian composers. She often appeared in tandem with Rachmaninov, who accompanied her at a recital in 1904. After the Revolution, Nezhdanova retained her place alongside a new generation of artists and sang for an appreciative and increasingly uneducated audiences. She was much admired by Lenin and was given an appreciative audience. She also gave her personal collection to the Bolshoi Theatre. She was awarded the title of People's Artist of the USSR.

From 1936 she taught at the Moscow Conservatory where she had previously been a pupil. She died in Moscow on 26 June 1950. A memorial concert was held in her honour in the Bolshoi Theatre on 6 June 1950, a loyal and respected recital given.

For an assessment of the artistry of Antonina Nezhdanova let us take the perceptive Michael Scott (The Record of Singing): "It was a lyric soprano of the most beautiful timbre, in (k) Incarnata, particularly staccato passages, the consonants were swift and brilliant – quite without the contracted neck-larynx of the German school. She has, however, at her most affecting in lyrical music."

This recital opens with a commanding account of the Queen of the Night's aria from *Die Zauberflöte* (Standard notation for all titles in foreign languages can be discussed in detail, so that more space is available for information about the recording itself). Glinka's cavatina and rondo are certain unfamiliar songs, prepared by Dr. Boris Semenoff, with the text at the end of this note.

Mikhail Glinka (1804-1857) was a pioneering spirit in Russian musical composition and is particularly significant principally for his two operas, *A Life for the Tsar* (1836) and *Ruslan and Ludmilla* (1842). The earlier work established Glinka's reputation as the most important opera composer of his time and was a favourite in Russia since its first performance in St Petersburg in 1836. Semenoff has explained, was *Ivan Susanin*. Glinka's work was followed by Tchaikovsky's *Eugene Onegin*. The opera became known as a classic in the opera world and a cornerstone of Russian musical culture.

After the revolution the work, with its theme of personal sacrifice for the Tsar, was renamed *Ivan Susanin*. The new title was adopted to reflect the new political climate. The opera was performed with great success and became a staple of the Bolshoi Theatre repertoire.
