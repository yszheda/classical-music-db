# ESCLARMONDE

First performed at the Opéra-Comique, Paris, on 15th May 1889.
The action takes place in France, in Byzantine times.

## SYNOPSIS

**Act I**
The French camp before the walls of Constantinople. It is night. The French king Cléomer and his army are in camp, having besieged the city in order to force the Saracen king to give his sister in marriage to Cléomer. The French camp is protected by a magic spell which prevents the Byzantines attacking it, but all attempts at an assault on the city walls have been foiled by another spell which the Saracens have cast. While the army sleeps, the king keeps watch, disturbed by thoughts of the beauty of the woman he has never seen and the impossibility of breaking the deadlock. Suddenly he hears a strange sound and Esclarmonde herself appears to him in a vision, telling him that she loves him and that if he will keep her visit a secret she will help him to victory and become his bride. He is overcome by her beauty and the encounter leaves him helpless. When the army awakes they find their king incapable of action. When news comes of a sudden attack by the Saracens, confusion spreads. Cléomer rouses himself and leads his troops to the relief of the threatened point, and just as the French are beginning to give way his inspired leadership turns the tide and he returns in triumph.

**Act II**
Scene 1: In the camp of the Saracens, before the tents of the king and his sister Esclarmonde: her father Phorcas (an old magician who has given her all his magic powers) has summoned her. He knows of her meeting with Cléomer and warns her that if she gives away her secret her powers will vanish and she will be left helpless. She admits she loves him, and when the Saracen king enters he is bewildered to find that the magic he and his father relied on to protect the city has suddenly ceased to work. Constantinople has fallen, Cléomer is victorious, and he claims Esclarmonde as his bride.

Scene 2: The marriage-feast. The French are celebrating their victory and the marriage of their king. He is still bemused by the magic of Esclarmonde's spell, and can talk of nothing but his love. Only the young French noblewoman Rozenn, who loves Cléomer and is beloved by his brother, is sad and silent. Her brother, the knight Enéas, notices her sorrow and tries to comfort her. The banquet ends amid general rejoicing.

**Act III**
The gardens of the palace. Cléomer has learned the secret of Esclarmonde's magic powers from a message she had carelessly dropped. Appalled by the supernatural element in their relationship, he now feels that his love has been based on deception. He repudiates her, despite her pleas and her confession that she has surrendered all her powers for love of him. He is still unconvinced, and leaves her to keep his tryst with the French army that is to set out for France. She calls on the remains of her magic power to curse his inconstancy: the sea shall swallow him up before he can reach home.

**Act IV**
Scene 1: In Provence, at the court of Cléomer's father. Cléomer has returned safely, and is to marry Rozenn. The wedding preparations are in full swing. In the distance we hear the sea, and Esclarmonde's curse still haunts Cléomer's dreams. He is haunted by the memory of his love for her, and cannot bring himself to give himself wholly to his new bride. The wedding procession arrives, and suddenly Cléomer falls into a trance: the spell Esclarmonde cast still has power over him. As the distraught court looks on, the ghostly form of Esclarmonde appears to him and draws him away from the bridal procession.

Scene 2: By the seashore. The distraught Esclarmonde has wandered to the beach to wait for Cléomer's ship to be wrecked before her eyes, but he has reached land, and comes to her, his heart changed. He tells her that despite everything he still loves her, and begs her forgiveness. As they are reconciled, her father Phorcas appears to demand her back, telling her that she may resume her magic powers if she will leave Cléomer. But she has renounced her magic for love, and refuses to go with him. He leaves in wrath, threatening her with misfortune. As the lovers embrace, the French court arrives from the palace; they have followed Cléomer and now understand everything. Cléomer presents Esclarmonde to his father, asks for the crown, and places it on her head. She is accepted as his queen, and all ends happily.

The opera's popularity with singers and public has never been as great as that of *Manon* or *Werther*, though for many people its musical richness and spontaneity place it at the very top of Massenet's output. Early audiences found the supernatural elements difficult to accept, and the unconventional chromaticism of much of the score made it seem difficult and modern—indeed when the composer's publisher asked the distinguished conductor André Messager what he thought of it after the premiere, he replied: 'It's impossible to sing, and it's all chromatic. I'm going off to have a drink. I'm sick of it.' In fact the chromaticism is perfectly idiomatic and grows naturally out of the harmony of Wagner's *Tristan*, which Massenet had thoroughly assimilated and made his own. What distinguished Massenet from many more avant-garde composers of the time is his unfailing sense of the theatre and his sensitivity to the expressive needs of the voice; the broad sweeping melodies that lie so gratefully on the voice give the whole work a spaciousness that is quite irresistible.

# Jules Massenet: Esclarmonde
Recorded 1975

The reawakening of interest in Massenet's music in recent years has been one of the most encouraging developments in the opera house and on the gramophone. Among the works that have profited from this new approach is certainly *Esclarmonde*, the magical romantic opera which Massenet wrote at the very height of his powers between *Manon* and *Werther*. In his day and for long after it was thought of as a "difficult" work, but that was mostly because its wide-ranging vocal lines need singers of the calibre of Joan Sutherland to cope with them, and its colourful orchestration needs a conductor who appreciates Massenet's particular brand of lyrical romanticism rather than a conductor who wants to drive it forward into sounding like something it is not. This recording came about when Richard Bonynge and Joan Sutherland both found themselves fascinated by the score and wanted to share their enthusiasm with a wider public. It was immediately recognised on all sides as one of the most impressive recordings they have ever undertaken: it shows off Joan Sutherland's unique vocal gifts to the very best advantage, and under Richard Bonynge's loving direction the orchestra brings out all the richness and subtlety of one of the most colourful scores Massenet ever wrote.

It was the composer's favourite among his own works, and when he revised it later in his life he did so with the most minute care and thought. What most struck him about the original work was the richness of the melodic invention. He wrote in his memoirs: "My score was so full of it that when I came to re-handle the work I had to destroy nine-tenths of it, for I could not help myself: new melodies! New melodic phrases! were everywhere popping up in front of me. It's like a painter who has too many colours on his palette—everything gets mixed up and loses its outline. That's what happened to me with *Esclarmonde*: I overdid it." But even after the revision the work is still amazingly rich in melody, and it still gives the listener the feeling that Massenet was overflowing with invention when he wrote it. His gift for matching the words to the music that fits them exactly and at the same time sounds natural and spontaneous was always one of his most remarkable characteristics, and in *Esclarmonde* this gift is seen at its very best.

It's hard to think of another composer who unerringly finds the right emotional colour for each moment in the drama with such apparently effortless ease. Massenet's range in this respect is from the most brilliant marches and processions to the most delicate and intimate love music, and he can move from one to the other with a freedom that is quite natural and never seems abrupt. In this recording the beauty of the score is fully revealed, and it is hoped that listeners who have not previously had the opportunity of getting to know this wonderful opera will find as much pleasure in it as Richard Bonynge and Joan Sutherland have found in preparing it.

# Jules Massenet
Born 12 May 1842, Montaud (Loire), died 13 August 1912, Paris.

Massenet was the son of a master ironworker, and his musical talent showed itself early: he was already giving piano lessons when he was only sixteen. He entered the Paris Conservatoire in 1860, studied with Ambroise Thomas and won the Prix de Rome in 1863. On his return from Rome he made a living by teaching the piano, bringing himself to the notice of the Paris Opéra with some incidental music to a play by Leconte de Lisle, *Les Érinnyes*, and his first stage work, a one-act opéra comique called *La grand'tante*, was given by the Opéra-Comique in 1867. After the Franco-Prussian war, during which he served in the army, his career began to take off: in 1872 *L'Donchante* was successfully given at the Opéra-Comique, and by 1876, when *Le roi de Lahore* was produced at the Paris Opéra, his reputation was established. In 1878 he was appointed professor of composition at the Paris Conservatoire, and he quickly became one of the most influential and best-loved teachers of his generation—one has only to think of composers as different as Charpentier, Koechlin, d'Indy and Debussy (even though Debussy was already a mature composer when he studied with him briefly) to see how far his influence extended.

---

## page 9

He reached the peak of his fame around 1885 with *Manon*, which was an enormous success from the start and has never lost its place in the repertory. It was followed by *Esclarmonde* in 1889, which was his own favourite among his works although it never achieved the popular success of *Manon*. It is true that the role of Esclarmonde makes enormous demands on the soprano singing it, and this has always limited the number of singers who can tackle it successfully. Massenet continued to produce a series of masterpieces after another, including *Werther* (1892), *Thaïs* (1894), *Cendrillon* (1899), *Grisélidis* (1901), and finally *Don Quichotte* (1910). He worked right up to his death in 1912, and left an opera (*Panurge*) finished enough to be performed after his death.

It's difficult to think of any composer who has understood the human heart better than Massenet. He was above all a composer of *humanité* in the French sense—warm, compassionate, always ready to understand the weaknesses of human nature and to find room for forgiveness. He was also amazingly versatile: he could write comedy as well as tragedy, grand opera as well as intimate opéra comique, and yet each work has its own individual flavour, its own special world of feeling. In *Esclarmonde* we find him in one of his most ambitious and exalted moods, writing a score on the largest scale he ever attempted, full of the most daring harmony and orchestration (it was this that contemporary audiences found "difficult" when it was new) and yet all held together by the most extraordinary melodic inventiveness. It is a very personal work in the best sense, and it bears the unmistakable imprint of his genius, which still speaks directly to us today when we have learned to appreciate the Massenet magic as previous generations were not able to do. This recording, prepared by two of Massenet's most devoted admirers, shows that his magic is still as potent as it ever was, and that the man who understood so well the secrets of the human heart still speaks to us directly across the footlights more than a hundred years after he wrote this wonderful score.

# Joan Sutherland

Born in Sydney, Australia, Joan Sutherland first came to international notice when she came to London to study with Clive Carey and made her debut with the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden in 1952. Her international reputation, already considerable as a result of her work with the Covent Garden company, was established beyond question after her sensational appearance as Lucia in *Lucia di Lammermoor* in the famous Zeffirelli production conducted by Tullio Serafin in 1959. Since then she has been universally recognised as the reigning queen of bel canto, reviving many forgotten works of the early 19th-century Italian repertory and establishing herself as one of the greatest singers of her generation. The many awards she has received include a CBE in 1961 and a DBE in 1978. She is married to Richard Bonynge, and they make their home in Switzerland.

## page 15 (biographies continued)

# Huguette Tourangeau

Huguette Tourangeau was born in Montreal, Canada, and studied there with two distinguished singers, Pauline Donalda and Salvator Issaurel. She made her operatic debut in 1958 in Montreal, and in 1964 came to London to study with Fernando Previtali. The following year she joined the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, with whom she has appeared in a wide variety of roles including Berlioz's *Didon*, which was one of her greatest successes. She has also appeared with the Canadian Opera and the opera companies of Vancouver and Seattle, and she has been closely associated with the San Francisco Opera for many years. Her beautiful voice and distinguished artistry have made her one of the most admired mezzos of her generation, and she has enjoyed a particularly close artistic partnership with Joan Sutherland in many different contexts.

# Richard Bonynge

Richard Bonynge was born in Australia, and studied piano at the Sydney Conservatorium before going on to study in London. He soon became interested in conducting and in the re-examination of the 19th-century Italian and French repertory, and he has always been one of the most intelligent and articulate advocates of a reassessment of the many forgotten operas that deserve a revival. He made his conducting debut in 1962, and since then he has conducted many of the most important opera companies in the world. His extensive discography includes many of the operas that he has revived, including many of the most important bel canto roles for Joan Sutherland. He has always been particularly interested in the music of Massenet, and this recording is the result of many years of admiration for the composer and his music.

## page 16 (German translation of introduction)

# Jules Massenet (1842-1912)

Das neuerwachte Interesse an Massenets Musik in den letzten Jahren ist eine der ermutigendsten Entwicklungen auf der Opernbühne und für das Grammophon. Zu den Werken, die von dieser neuen Betrachtungsweise profitieren, gehört zweifellos *Esclarmonde*, die zauberische romantische Oper, die Massenet auf der Höhe seines Schaffens zwischen *Manon* und *Werther* komponierte. Zu seiner Zeit und noch lange danach galt sie als "schwieriges" Werk, doch das lag vor allem daran, daß ihre weitgespannten vokalen Linien Sänger vom Format Joan Sutherlands brauchen, und die farbenreiche Orchestrierung braucht einen Dirigenten, der Massenets besondere Art lyrischen Romantik versteht, statt das Werk vorwärtszutreiben und es etwas sein zu lassen, das es nicht ist. Diese Aufnahme entstand, als Richard Bonynge und Joan Sutherland gleichermaßen von der Partitur fasziniert waren und ihre Begeisterung mit einem größeren Publikum teilen wollten. Sie wurde sofort von allen Seiten als eine der eindrucksvollsten Einspielungen anerkannt, die beide unternommen haben: sie zeigt Joan Sutherlands einzigartige stimmliche Begabung von ihrer allerbesten Seite, und unter Richard Bonynges liebevoller Leitung bringt das Orchester alle Reichtümer und die Subtilität der farbenreichsten Partitur hervor, die Massenet je geschrieben hat.

Es war die Lieblingsoper des Komponisten unter seinen eigenen Werken, und als er sie später in seinem Leben revidierte, tat er es mit größter Sorgfalt und Überlegung. Was ihn an dem Originalwerk am meisten stieß, war der Reichtum der melodischen Erfindung. Er schrieb in seinen Memoiren: "Meine Partitur war so übervoll davon, daß ich, als ich das Werk neu bearbeitete, neun Zehntel zerstören mußte, denn ich konnte nicht anders: neue Melodien! Neue melodische Phrasen! tauchten überall vor mir auf. Es ist wie bei einem Maler, der zu viele Farben auf seiner Palette hat—alles vermischt sich und verliert seine Umrisse. Das ist, was mir mit *Esclarmonde* passiert ist: ich habe es übertrieben." Aber auch nach der Revision ist das Werk noch immer erstaunlich melodienreich, und es gibt dem Hörer noch immer das Gefühl, daß Massenet von Erfindung nur so überquoll, als er es schrieb. Seine Gabe, die Worte auf genau die Musik zu passen, die ihnen paßt, und die gleichzeitig natürlich und spontan klingt, war immer eine seiner bemerkenswertesten Eigenschaften, und in *Esclarmonde* sieht man diese Gabe von ihrer allerbesten Seite.

Es ist schwer, an einen anderen Komponisten zu denken, der unfehlbar die richtige emotionale Farbe für jeden Moment des Dramas findet mit einer so scheinbar mühelosen Leichtigkeit. Massenets Spannweite reicht von den brillantesten Märschen und Prozessionen bis zur zartesten und intimsten Liebesmusik, und er kann von der einen zur anderen mit einer Freiheit übergehen, die ganz natürlich ist und nie abrupt wirkt. In dieser Einspielung wird die Schönheit der Partitur voll enthüllt, und es ist zu hoffen, daß Hörer, die bisher noch nicht die Gelegenheit hatten, diese wunderbare Oper kennenzulernen, ebensoviel Freude an ihr finden werden wie Richard Bonynge und Joan Sutherland bei der Vorbereitung dieser Aufnahme gefunden haben.

## page 17 (French translation of introduction)

# Jules Massenet (1842-1912)

Le regain d'intérêt pour la musique de Massenet au cours de ces dernières années est l'un des développements les plus encourageants que l'on ait vu à l'opéra et au disque. Parmi les œuvres qui ont profité de cette nouvelle approche figure certainement *Esclarmonde*, l'opéra romantique et magique que Massenet écrivit lorsqu'il était au sommet de son art, entre *Manon* et *Werther*. Du temps de Massenet et longtemps après, on le considérait comme une œuvre "difficile", mais c'est surtout parce que ses lignes vocales étendues demandent une chanteuse du calibre de Joan Sutherland, et son orchestration colorée demande un chef qui apprécie le romantisme lyrique particulier de Massenet plutôt que d'essayer de le pousser en avant pour le faire paraître ce qu'il n'est pas. Cet enregistrement vit le jour lorsque Richard Bonynge et Joan Sutherland se trouvèrent tous deux fascinés par la partition et voulurent partager leur enthousiasme avec un public plus large. Il fut immédiatement reconnu de tous côtés comme l'un des plus impressionnants enregistrements qu'ils aient jamais entrepris: il met en valeur les dons vocaux uniques de Joan Sutherland sous son meilleur jour, et sous la direction affectueuse de Richard Bonynge l'orchestre révèle toute la richesse et la subtilité de l'une des partitions les plus colorées que Massenet ait jamais écrites.

C'était l'œuvre préférée du compositeur parmi toutes les siennes, et lorsqu'il la révisa plus tard dans sa vie, il le fit avec le plus grand soin et la plus grande attention. Ce qui frappa Massenet le plus dans l'œuvre originale, c'était la richesse de l'invention mélodique. Il écrivit dans ses mémoires: "Ma partition était tellement pleine que lorsque je vins à retoucher l'œuvre je dus en détruire les neuf dixièmes, car je ne pouvais pas m'en empêcher: de nouvelles mélodies! de nouvelles phrases mélodiques! apparaissaient de toutes parts devant moi. C'est comme un peintre qui a trop de couleurs sur sa palette—tout se mélange et perd ses contours. C'est ce qui m'est arrivé avec *Esclarmonde*: j'avais trop abusé de la richesse." Mais même après la révision l'œuvre est toujours incroyablement riche en mélodie, et l'auditeur éprouve toujours cette impression que Massenet débordait d'invention quand il l'écrivit. Son don pour adapter les mots à la musique qui leur convient exactement tout en conservant un caractère naturel et spontané a toujours été l'une de ses caractéristiques les plus remarquables, et dans *Esclarmonde* ce don se montre à son meilleur.
