The East German state, the German Democratic Republic (DDR), was formed in 1949 during the blockade of Berlin, and one of its leading cities was Leipzig, home of Wagner, Mendelssohn and Schumann and of one of the most famous of all musical publishers, Peters Edition. It was therefore a city with a rich musical heritage, but perhaps its most famous musical connection was Johann Sebastian Bach, who spent much of his working life playing at the Thomanerkirche.

In July 1950 Leipzig commemorated the 200th anniversary of Bach's death by holding a prestigious piano competition and invited Dmitri Shostakovich to be one of the judges. One of the contestants, Tatyana Nikolayeva, although required to play only one of Bach's Preludes and Fugues from the Well Tempered Clavier, had prepared all 48 pieces and so impressed Shostakovich that he recommended first prize. Winning this trophy led to other engagements and later, in Berlin, Shostakovich deputised for an indisposed Maria Yudina and thereby played alongside Nikolayeva himself.

Although he was working on a song cycle on his return to the USSR, the Bachian atmosphere of Leipzig had clearly set Shostakovich's pulses racing and in October 1950 he telephoned Nikolayeva asking her to drop by and hear some new pieces. These turned out to be the Preludes and Fugues in C major and A minor. At this stage he had no intention to compose a complete cycle a la Bach, however once bitten by the bug he composed one prelude and fugue every three days or so, always testing them out on Nikolayeva. According to her, Shostakovich was not only composing the 'public' song cycle Ten Poems on Texts by Revolutionary Poets, but had also begun work on his Tenth Symphony and she recalled hearing passages from this in preparation at the same time as sampling the latest Prelude and Fugue. Despite his earlier avowed dislike of composing something as formulaic as a fugue, very few corrections were deemed necessary and only one (B minor Prelude) required a second draft. The entire cycle was completed on 25 February 1951, Shostakovich having followed a compositional pattern of ascending fifths major and minor alternating, as opposed to Bach who had composed in an ascending semi tonal sequence.

Shostakovich submitted the choral cycle Ten Poems for publication first and predictably received an officially warm reception (since the work is in the ‘easy' style of his well crafted, attractive but untaxing Song of the Forests). However the Preludes and Fugues were another matter. Shostakovich played a selection to the Union of Composers on 31 March 1951. Shostakovich had slipped back to his gloomy, complex and introspective self and had 'not wholly overcome all his previous errors'. However certain pianists spoke out on his behalf and Gilels was the first person of note to include extracts in his concert programmes. The first complete cycle was given in the summer of 1952 by Nikolayeva (the work's dedicatee) at the Committee for Artistic Affairs and it was this performance which finally prompted publication. Part of the blame for the earlier adverse reaction must presumably be laid at the composer's door, since many of his earlier critics were now enthusiastic in their praise: by all accounts Shostakovich's playing on 31 March 1951 left a good deal to be desired and although he was a fine pianist, Shostakovich's own recordings of some of these Preludes and Fugues have been dismissed as 'nervous, inhibited .. and in places close to perfunctory' (Stephen Johnson).

Nikolayeva duly gave the public premiere of the 24 Preludes and Fugues spread over two evenings in Leningrad on 23 and 28 December 1952. Thereafter she sought to play the cycle complete whenever possible, insisting this was the composer's wish (other colleagues of Shostakovich did not necessarily agree, and the composer made no effort himself to perform the complete cycle). Often however Nikolayeva agreed to play individual Preludes and Fugues at times to support a premiere of a new chamber piece, such as at the posthumous premiere of Shostakovich's final work, the Viola Sonata.

The opening Prelude in C major provides an immediate link with the age of J S Bach, being a sarabande with a brief note of anxiety. The following Fugue maintains the mood of serenity. In contrast the Prelude in A minor features a helter skelter of semiquavers and its corresponding Fugue is a spiky polka. The Prelude in G major opens majestically in octaves with a second more insistent theme providing a contrast. The jaunty Fugue in 6/8 features a memorable motif consisting of an upward scale followed by a downward leap of a major sixth then an ascending fifth. The Fourth Prelude in E minor makes use of the previous prelude's second theme but is here transformed into a reposeful figure. The double Fugue that follows has a tender feel and the piece closes with an optimistic tierce de picardie (unexpected major third). Prelude and Fugue in D major are probably the lightest of the set: in the prelude the rippling chords in the treble are offset by a lyrical cello like melody whilst the Fugue begins with a curious stammer like passage.
The dotted rhythms of the Sixth Prelude in B minor call to mind the opening of the Fifth Symphony One can almost imagine the corresponding Fugue orchestrated, starting in the lower strings and woodwind gradually rising to the higher registered instruments before dying away in the distance The Prelude and Fugue in A major which follow are, like the D major set, of lighter texture; the Fugue is especially attractive and, fanfare like, is based on the notes of the A major chord. The Prelude No. 8 in F sharp minor features an impish figure in the treble set over an ostinato bass. The subject of the lengthy Fugue is a horn like figure, the prevailing mood is desolate but not totally without feeling. In the E major Prelude Shostakovich again treats the keyboard as an orchestral palette with low octaves being answered somewhat eerily by high treble octaves. The bustling theme of the Fugue seems irresistibly drawn to its key note. The Prelude and Fugue in C sharp minor finds Shostakovich recalling his baroque predecessor. Perhaps he had these works in mind when he finally decided to compose a complete set a la Bach and stated that he wanted to 'perfect mastery in the polyphonic genre'. The Prelude in B major recalls the manner of the Ninth Symphony whilst the staccato fourths anticipate the ‘Careers' movement of the Thirteenth Symphony. Staccato fourths also feature in the brisk Fugue. In total contrast the Prelude in G sharp minor is a profound and contemplative passacaglia, rudely interrupted by a Fugue in 5/4. At one point, just as the texture threatens to become over muddied, Shostakovich strips it down to the bare minimum before ending with another tierce de picardie.

Yet again there is a total contrast in the Prelude in F sharp minor as Shostakovich introduces a Debussian melismatic line over a rocking accompaniment. The corresponding Fugue, in five parts, is very deep: a piece for the top drawer. A sombre tremolo ushers in the doom laden Prelude in E flat minor. Full of angst the composer seems undecided as to which direction to proceed so that the dance like Fugue comes as a shock. Shostakovich waltzes often contain pleasantly unexpected harmonies and the Prelude in D flat major is no exception. Its Fugue, based upon an eleven note row, is possibly the most complicated of the set, with frequent changes of direction and with no consistent time signature. The florid and romantic sounding Prelude in B flat minor is a set of four variations. The highly ornamented Fugue recalls eighteenth century works for harpsichord.


---

Very much back to the twentieth century with the occasionally motor rhythmic is the Prelude in A fiat major, featuring a tripartite melody in which the outer parts carry the tune. Like that in C sharp minor, this Fugue demonstrates Shostakovich's technical expertise. The same would apply to the Fugue in F minor whilst the Prelude preceding it demonstrates the melancholy streak which is so typical of Shostakovich.

The Prelude in E flat major opens with massive chords; the second subject in contrast sounds distinctly jumpy over a pedal note which slowly dies away. The constant tugging towards F flat rather than E flat in the Fugue sounds abrasive and only in its final moments does the piece achieve any feel of ‘security'. The main feature of the Prelude in C minor (low octaves answered by a lonely treble figure) reminds us that Shostakovich began work on his Tenth Symphony around this time: it resembles the slow passage before the devil may-care finale. It also contains his private password DSCH (D - E flat - C - B). The Fugue begins with the same four notes as the Prelude. Over a marching bass, the theme of the Prelude in B flat major is carried by a semiquaver motif which seems to encompass the entire keyboard. This is followed by a dancing Fugue. Only eleven days separated the Fugue in C minor (No. 20) from the completion of the cycle, yet some commentators feel that it was in these final pieces that Shostakovich was most inspired. The flowing and lyrical Prelude in G minor has a persistent cello like theme and the lyricism is maintained in the Fugue, although one gains the impression once again this is a very private piece. The penultimate Prelude and Fugue in F major shows the composer raising his hat once again to J S Bach, although the harmonies are very much his own in both pieces. No longer does the Fugue seem like a technical exercise; here Shostakovich is enjoying himself. The final Prelude in D minor opens sturdily but does not remain so for long and out of the depths comes a new theme which receives the fugal treatment within the Prelude. This same theme is treated afresh in the Fugue proper and gains gradually in anxiety (although some say in defiance) until a massive climax is achieved: a fitting end to the cycle. In 1937 (aged 13!) Tatiana Nikolayeva (1924-93) had enrolled at the Moscow Central School of Music where she studied piano with the renowned Alexander Goldenweiser. Subsequently she studied composition alongside Alfred Schnittke.
In her later years achieved a near mythic status in the West. So lauded have been her recordings that it is a wonder, and a considerable cause of regret, that she only came to be appreciated outside Russia and eastern Europe so late in her career. In her own country she acquired considerable respect as a teacher at the Moscow Conservatory, where she became Professor of Piano from 1965 and it was as a jury member for the Leeds Piano Competition that the UK first saw her in 1984 She gave her first London concert in 1986 and appeared at the Salzburg Festival the following year. Although her repertoire was by no means confined to Bach and Shostakovich, it is for her interpretations of their music that she is best remembered.

In her final years she visited Britain regularly playing for example at the Last Night of the Proms in 1991. It was while on a USA tour, in San Franciso, on 13 November 1993, that she suffered a fatal cerebral aneurism. She was playing one of the Shostakovich Fugues at the time but managed to complete it before leaving the platform. She died 9 days later.
