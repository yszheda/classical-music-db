## Inner Page 2 (Liner Notes by Akira Asada)

# 解冻之梦

Akira Asada
Dean, Graduate School, Kyoto City University of Arts

Before Stalinism and socialist realism froze the various possibilities born of the Russian Revolution, there was a heated era when the social revolution pursued by the political avant-garde and the cultural revolution pursued by the artistic avant-garde seemed to proceed simultaneously. Even before the collapse of the Soviet Union, we knew something about that era's Russian avant-garde, but information was always limited, especially in music compared to other fields like art, architecture, literature, theater, and film. To put it simply, among Russian avant-garde we knew Scriabin before, and after Stalinist system was established we knew Prokofiev and Shostakovich, but the period in between was left blank. Isn't it true that Japanese music fans first learned the names of avant-garde composers from that era like Lourie and Roslavets from Yuhji Takahashi's 1972 book on Scriabin?

Of course, since then research on Russian avant-garde music has progressed, and we've had more opportunities to encounter the actual music through recordings. But opportunities to hear it live remain extremely limited. In that sense, having twice been able to hear Irina Mejoueva play piano music of the Russian avant-garde has been a precious experience for me. The first opportunity was when an exhibition of Russian avant-garde posters was held at the Shiga Prefectural Museum of Modern Art in 2010, and Ikuo Kameyama and Mejoueva gave a lecture concert at Biwako Hall as a related event. Furthermore in 2012, Mejoueva again held a special collection of Russian music at Waka Hall, and I joined for a talk and concert. This album contains a different recording, but much of the content overlaps.

I must confess here: I've long known that besides Chopin Mejoueva also plays and records Medtner, and that Mejoueva gives a beautifully polished performance to Medtner, who is called "the Russian Brahms," but I never expected that she would play Russian avant-garde music with such sharp dynamics.

Indeed, starting from Scriabin, the first of the Russian avant-garde, who expanded endlessly through mysticism to the very brink of disintegration—the famous Tenth Sonata—Mechoueva's performance, with overwhelming dynamism nevertheless maintains the overall unity of the music, and it far exceeded my expectations. When Vladimir Horowitz played this for the composer himself, his 1966 live recording was like leaping into the abyss together with the composer. In our era when such emotional identification is no longer possible, Mejoueva simply calmly reads the score, and lets the music that is written there—just the music—stand there as a pure crystal.

That Scriabin-esque broad arpeggios riding across wide intervals sailing out onto the boundless sea—that's Roslavets. Furthermore, experimenting with sound like a mobile floating in air—that's Lourie (the title "Formen in der Luft" can also be translated as "floating in air"). Prokofiev too, from that same era, wrote music full of wild dynamics, and Mejoueva's sharp cutting edge brings out the work's expressiveness wonderfully. (Incidentally, at La Folle Journée she also played a selection from Sweidinsky's Petrushka, it was equally sharp, I really await the recording.)

On the other hand, this album also includes Medtner, a work Mejoueva has long made her specialty, and as you'd expect you can hear a full, perfect performance without deficiency. Of course, it goes without saying that even within the unfolding of avant-garde experiment in Russia, there was also music like Brahms, and this album presents a balanced view of the whole picture.

Nearly a century has passed since the dream of the Russian avant-garde was briefly sealed. Now unfrozen that dream, to have it reborn—that is a difficult task. Mejoueva avoids direct emotional identification, and reads the avant-garde music of that time from a distance, even music that was considered conservative back then, and resurrects it in the present as perfectly proportioned music. There is no revolutionary fever here. Only the fragments of a dream that have survived closed in water for almost a century, everywhere emitting transparent radiance.

(August 22012)

## Inner Page 3 (Liner Notes by Ikuo Kameyama)

# Atemporality, Geometry, Irony, and the Innovation of Lyricism

Ikuo Kameyama
Russian literary scholar, President of Tokyo University of Foreign Studies

One hundred years since the birth of the Russian avant-garde, looking back we must now reexamine the importance of the early 20th century birth of avant-garde art... Over these past years, Irina Mejoueva has continuously given performances, and now she releases this new double CD. Listening to it, I have had new thoughts. The music by four composers collected here portrays four distinct contrasting faces of early century art. Let me organize a little: one, atemporality; two, geometry; three, irony; four, lyrical innovation. These four elements are also four trials that test Mejoueva's personality.

Against existing Russian music, with the resolve to even negate the very essence of music as temporal art, Scriabin pushed ahead building "atemporality." Seized by the idea of the "end" of the world, he believed that after this "end" human spirit must fundamentally change, it cannot go on unchanged, and he expressed this vision in music. If you listen to the first two movements of Piano Sonata No. 10, it's clear how—within the nineteenth-century musical tradition—he wrote something completely different. In an age where the world is dissolving toward its "end," and the boundaries of self are about to be lost, Mejoueva's performance of Scriabin crystalizes clearly this process, just like setting it in crystal glass—an incredible fusion of the inorganic and the mystical.

Lourie's performance is also superb. Going a step further beyond Scriabin, this music has no mystical shadow. Rather, the world and nature are drawn sometimes softly, sometimes with sharp angles, and Mejoueva's quality matches perfectly with Lourie, turning the geometric forms born from Malevich and Kandinsky wonderfully into "paintings in sound."

Scriabin, Lourie, and Roslavets all tried to make music part of the organic world, thoroughly rejecting traditional "form" as the symbol of human arrogance. In contrast, young Prokofiev chose the path of conflict, not organic unity with the world. The Russian word "sarcasm" translated as "irony" actually means closer to "disgust," it expresses physiological disgust toward existing tradition, and you can see that Mejoueva's musical cutting edge in this piece is completely different from before—she grinds away the ambiguity of the nerves, leaving only sharpness, yet even so there is no violence here, you can still hear the infinite possibility of the world and lyricism.

And so, the second disc contains Medtner. Among the many works Mejoueva has played, this can be called one of her undisputed masterpieces. While contemporary composers all pursued sharper methods, Medtner resolutely stepped onto "the last line of music." But even his piano playing still bears the imprint of the era. The greatest masterpiece "Night Wind" overflows with thick structural sense, and behind Romantic conceptions we can see the bold strategy of this 20th-century composer.

Among excellent pianists, there are moments when a breathing rhythm is born that seems like god has descended, isn't that exactly what happens in this work? With wonderful tempo and magnificent, leisurely sonority, it maintains throughout strong empathy and great rhythmic pulse. You can certainly experience for yourself through this famous performance what kind of resistance this "lyrical innovation" was for the Russian avant-garde.

(August 2012)

## ■ Disc-1

### Alexander Scriabin (1872-1915)

Alexander Nikolayevich Scriabin (1872-1915) studied piano and composition at the Moscow Conservatory. Against the nineteenth-century style that his contemporary Rachmaninov clung to, Scriabin's early works already show heavy Chopin influence, but he quickly established his own unique atonal style. In 1905 he encountered Madame Blavatsky's theosophy, fell into mysticism, and systematically began using the "mystic chord," establishing this mystical aesthetic which bore fruit in music.

**5 Preludes op.74** is the last numbered work of Scriabin, composed in 1914.

The first piece, only 16 bars *"Douloureux, déchirant"* (3/4), its unique harmony swells with melancholy mood. The second piece *"Très lent, contemplatif"* (4/8) uses chromaticism, followed by the more violent third piece *"Allegro drammatico"* (9/8). The fourth piece *"Lent, vague, indécis"* (3/4) is a 24-bar piece that pushed beyond the limits of music composition at the time, using dissonance in an extremely novel way. And the final fifth *"Fier, belliqueux"* (3/2) truly lives up to its bellicose name, harmony continues to progress and becomes very complex.

Scriabin's last piano sonata **No. 10 op.70** was composed in a short time in summer 1913. It is in one movement, 9/16 time, mosaic texture, proceeds introduction → exposition → development → recapitulation → coda.

**2 Poèmes op.69** was composed between 1912-1913.

The first *"Allegretto"* (3/4) is in ABA'B' binary form. The second *"Allegretto"* (6/8) has an rising figure that repeats stubbornly. Neither have key signatures, they are atonal.

---

### Arthur Lourie (1892-1966)

Arthur Vincent Lourie (Arthur Vincent Lurie, 1892-1966) was born into a wealthy French Jewish family in St. Petersburg, and became one of the leading cultural avant-garde figures in St. Petersburg at that time. Indeed, right after the 1917 Russian Revolution, when the education commissariat culture department was organized, Lourie together with Alexander Shering and Vsevolod Meyerhold became head of the music department, taking a leading position in music under the new system. He later recalled this: "...friends, that is, artists and poets, just like the young avant-garde we were, I too sincerely believed in the October Revolution, I unconditionally joined the revolution. ...because Lenin supported us." (Incidentally, in the 1920s he exiled via Berlin to Paris, later moved to America.)

**Formen in der Luft** was composed in 1915, it is Lourie's representative work and at the same time the most representative work of modern music in Russia from this period.

This work is dedicated to Pablo Picasso, and consists of three short pieces. The most characteristic feature of this work is in the notation of the score: the staves are cut into fragments, empty parts of the staff are cut away, and as the note rises (or falls) staves are added above or below as needed. This so-called "graphic score" was absolutely extraordinarily novel and avant-garde back in 1915.

Furthermore, although clefs (treble and bass) are present, there are no bar lines, and of course no time signature marking, and moreover no key signature—all accidentals are written as temporary. In fact, this piece is atonal, so there's no need to mark a tonality. Regarding performance, although dynamics are marked everywhere, only the metronome mark is at the beginning, no tempo terms (allegro etc.) are marked.

The form of the score symbolizes the spatiality of this work. The absence of tempo marking can also be seen as an attempt to exclude the temporality of music. And this spatiality is precisely the characteristic of Cubism.

---

### Sergei Prokofiev (1891-1953)

Sergei Sergeyevich Prokofiev (1891-1953) entered the St. Petersburg Conservatory in 1904, graduated from the composition department in 1909, then studied piano and conducting. He completed the second major in 1914. When he graduated, his performance of his own First Piano Concerto didn't get particularly high marks, but he had already composed many works by that time.

The set of five pieces **Sarcasms** op.17 was written while he was still a piano-conducting student, and it's full of youthful energy, along with the contemporaneous Toccata op.11.

No. 1 "Tempestoso" (2/4), syncopation changes moment by moment, music forms a great vortex, presenting excellent dynamics. After No. 2 "Allegro rubato" (3/2-4/4), No. 3 "Allegro precipitato" (4/4), No. 4 "Smanioso" (4/4) to No. 5 "Precipitossimo" (2/4) it builds to a climax. Composition dates: No.1 1912, Nos.2-3 1913, Nos.4-5 1914. Early works often show polytonality (compound keys creating tension), combined with mechanical dissonant rhythm, showing an amazingly sharp character.

---

### Nikolai Roslavets (1881-1944)

Nikolai Andreyevich Roslavets (1881-1944) studied violin and composition at the Moscow Conservatory, graduated with a silver medal in 1912. Stylistically he inherits from Scriabin, developing his own harmonic technique called "synthetic chords." From the mid-1910s he began to attract attention, was a leading composer among Russian avant-garde composers in the 1920s played a leading role in development of Soviet modern music, but after the 1930s with drastic political changes he gradually retreated from the front line, and eventually his name was erased from history.

**5 Preludes** was composed between 1919-1922. No. 2 "Allegretto con moto" was composed in November 1919 in Moscow, combines 5/8 + 3/8 time, phrase repeated three times, the main part of three-part form continues in (5/8 time). Dedicated to Soviet musicologist/composer L. Sabaneyev, the fourth "Lento" (3/4) and fifth "Lento" (9/8) were both composed in 1922 in Kharkov. In these two pieces, below the bass staff they marked the position of chord changes, these marks function as "functional chords."

After perestroika in the 1980s and the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, Roslavets was rehabilitated, and now in his native Russia he is recognized as one of the important composers of the 20th century.

## ■ Disc-2

### Nikolai Medtner (1880-1951)

In the shadow of the mystical Scriabin and the technical Rachmaninov, Nikolai Medtner was a forgotten composer of the first half of the 20th century in Russia. His works are all difficult, but difficulty doesn't mean they have no expressive effect—on the contrary, the more you listen the more flavor they have, so he has been rediscovered in recent years, and opportunities for performance and recording have increased.

Nikolai Karlovich Medtner was born January 5, 1880 in Moscow to a German industrialist family. He began studying piano with his mother at age six, entered Moscow Conservatory at twelve, graduated in 1900 taking first prize gold medal in piano, and debuted as a pianist that same year. After that he was active as a leading contemporary Russian pianist, and also taught at his alma mater, never giving up his dream of becoming a composer, seriously began composing almost self-taught.

After the October Revolution in 1917, he escaped domestic chaos abroad in 1921, afterward traveled throughout Europe and America giving concerts, both performing and composing earned recognition. In 1925 he settled near Paris, but at that time Paris was gathered with avant-garde artists like Prokofiev, Stravinsky, Medtner—who was conservative—never fit in, and his music didn't get full evaluation. Britain had already given him positive recognition early, so in 1935 he moved to London.

Life in Britain became increasingly full, but in 1944 he had a heart attack, performing became difficult, and life became poor. Two years later a Maharajah of South India provided patronage—he loved Medtner's music feared it would be underestimated, and funded establishment of the Medtner Society, which from the 1910s to the 1950s recorded all major piano and song works performed by the composer himself, so that Medtner's skill as a pianist and correct interpretation of his works could be recorded for posterity.

In November 13, 1951 he died of a heart attack at age 71. His wife Anna survived, the Soviet Ministry of Culture accepted her wish to publish the complete Medtner works, and the next year she returned to the Soviet Union with the autograph manuscripts, and from 1959 to 1963 the complete Medtner works were published in Moscow in 12 volumes.

Most of Medtner's surviving works are piano pieces, so it's not wrong to call him "the Russian Chopin." Three piano concertos, fourteen sonatas and many other compositions—his most characteristic works are the character pieces he called "Skazki" (fairy tales). The Russian word "Skazka" means fairy tale, so it's based on Russian folk tales, but actually it reflects more the inner world of the author, so it's "telling a story," in German it's "Märchen"—that's the origin of the word.

Medtner's music has a certain conservatism. There's an anecdote: when he heard Prokofiev's work he said "if that counts as music, I'm not a composer," so he insisted on respecting classical form, he is "the Russian Brahms."

To sum up the greatest characteristic of his music in one Russian word, "stikhiya" fits perfectly—it means "primordial energy of all things," this implies sudden thick Russian emotional impact—this is not entirely correct. Medtner never simply puts impact on individual emotional expression, he insists on maintaining self and maintaining complete form, so even today after the twentieth century Medtner's name remains an不可忽略 presence in the world of Russian musical imagination.

**Piano Sonata in E minor op.25 No.2 "Night Wind"** is paired with the "Fairy Tale Sonata" (op.25 No.1) to form a large sonata, composed in 1911, published the following year, dedicated to Kumanin-Kratynsky. In terms of length, richness of material, strictness of development—it is an outstanding work, and there are those who call it one of the greatest piano sonatas of the twentieth century.

At the beginning of the score it quotes a poem by Fyodor Tyutchev (1803-1873), the leading Russian Romantic poet who influenced Russian symbolism—"Night Wind"—divided into two sections. The first section (Andante, 5/4) begins with a recurrence of three powerful G-E-E chords as introduction—this motif runs through the entire piece. It's said that in the composer's autograph next to these three chords he wrote in Russian "Slushayte!" meaning "Listen!" Heavy motivic repetition through the introduction brings forth the magnificent first theme, complex sonata form truly begins, then the second theme appears, music changes to 4/4—that is the second section (4/4), marked "poco a poco Allegro molto sfrenatamente" meaning "little by little accelerating, extremely wildly"—based on the introduction material new sonata form starts, various motivic-thematic changes constantly occur give birth to new texture that continues to develop. Therefore the second section is a kind of fantasia, and finally it ends casually with two arpeggios as the wind blows past.

## Back Page (Biography of Irina Mejoueva)

### Irina Mejoueva

Born in Gorky (now Nizhny Novgorod), Russia. Began piano at five, studied with Vladimir Trop at the Gnessin Special Music School and Gnessin Institute of Music (now Russian Academy of Music). After winning the Queen Elisabeth Competition in Rotterdam in 1992, began giving concerts in Netherlands, Germany, France and other countries. Based in Japan since 1997.

Her repertoire ranges widely from Baroque, classical to modern contemporary, and in recent years she has been particularly committed to introducing reevaluated Russian composer Nikolai Medtner—In 2001, for the 50th anniversary of Medtner's death she held a four-night "Forgotten Melodies" series recital bringing major works to attention. In 2002 she held a series recital at Hamarikyu Asahi Hall, and that same year did a national tour sponsored by Steinway Japan. In 2003 she collaborated with the Saint Petersburg Radio Symphony Orchestra in four Japanese cities. In 2004 and 2006 she collaborated with Karl Litzsche Italian, and in 2005-06 held four series recitals at Shofukan Hall in Osaka, continuing actively performing.

Received the Aoyama Music Prize in 2006. In 2010 her release "Chopin: Nocturnes" (Wakaba Kobo / WAKA-4143-44) received the Record Academy prize (instrumental category) that same year.

Professor at Kyoto City University of Arts since April 2012.
