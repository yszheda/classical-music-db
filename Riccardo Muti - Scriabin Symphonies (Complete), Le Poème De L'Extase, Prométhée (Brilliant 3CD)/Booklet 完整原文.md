## Liner Notes by Priska Frank (2005)

### Scriabin
### Symphonies 1, 2, 3; Le Poème de l'extase; Prométhée, le poème du feu

Alexander Scriabin had revolutionary ideas about art, philosophy, religion and humanity. Ideas that often seemed far removed from daily life and that the composer himself could not quite "pin down." He needed self-confidence to be able to work out his ideas and give them form. His always fascinating music therefore often raises more questions than it answers.

Compared to his contemporary Stravinsky, Scriabin falls short in terms of style, despite his obviously Russian characteristics. Unlike Stravinsky, who constantly changed style, Scriabin held strictly to the ultra-idealization of a single style. Like Debussy, he stands at the crossroads of many facets of modern music. Some of them have a clear frankness, not only musical but also general cultural. However, Scriabin possessed Stravinsky's gift for being able to distance himself from things—with irony, wit, not always with life, and his music is the embodiment of enthusiasm and growing.

What is particularly interesting is the rhythmic structure of this First Symphony. After a loose opening, the entire work up to the finale is written in triple meter. This creates a flowing unity, and the feeling of a flowing movement is reinforced by the transition to the last movement—with the choir in four-four time is overwhelming.

Scriabin wrote his Second Symphony in 1901; the premiere took place in 1902 in St. Petersburg. In this piece the aesthetic reorientations of his later music are hardly recognizable. There is no program and the structure is classically viable, as is the opening of the entire work—Andante, conceived as a slow introduction. Also in the use of instrumental [relationships] Scriabin is not far from tradition. In other respects he shows himself quite original. For example in the application and repetition of accents. And in the masterful use of the first theme, that of the second movement of the clarinet, as a melodic and rhythmically binding element in the entire work, for example as the basis for the Maestoso finale.

The Second Symphony also shows Scriabin in a completely individual expression and instrumental invention. Thus the trumpet with the copro of a completely different way and it floats in piccolo on a completely different way and the opening movement. Here we find a delightful foreshadowing of Messiaens music-religious renewal and a bird chorus for woodwinds.

Scriabin's three symphonies gradually move further away from classical ideas toward the most individual religious mysticism of the composer. The Third Symphony, *Le divin Poème*, was written by the composer at the beginning of the year [between] 1904 and 1908. As with the well-known *Poème de l'extase*, the *divin Poème* was given an external musical occasion. The following text was distributed at the premiere:

**Luttes (Struggles):** The conflict of human as slave of a personal God and the God in himself. This last

The implicit program would be as follows:
I. Being in the orgy of love
II. The realization of a fantastic dream
III. The delights of his own lust

Unlike Scriabin's earlier orchestral works, this "Fourth Symphony" does not return to the traditional division into loose movements. Instead, four deep-rooted but never more than two species create an overwhelming cross-pollination between a wealth of small thematic, often chromatic broken forms.

Scriabin completed his last orchestral work, *Prométhée, le poème du feu*, in 1910 in Brussels. In intended positions it is a piano concerto; at the same time it forms the climax of his symphonic work and metaphysical cognitions. With this work the composer comes closest to the removal of the boundary between art and religion. Apart from the usual orchestral apparatus, with expanded woodwind section, Scriabin calls for a color keyboard, which was indeed omitted at the premiere in 1911. In the climax of the work, a mixed choir joins with a wordless, vowel-only choir. The color element is not mandatory.

*Prométhée* is, like *Le Poème de l'extase*, final. This was probably inevitable for the composer. His harmonic style tended to dissolve traditional tonal elements and thereby the structures that support them. In *Prométhée* he achieved his own signature high point through a chord consisting of stacked fourths, systematically arranged.

Perhaps Scriabin's ultimate involvement with the Prometheus myth was by the hand. He saw himself as an inspired bringer of enlightenment to humanity. This god, who brought fire to humanity, demanded a natural identification point and called music—perhaps the best Scriabin wrote—on which a public in fire and flame can be set, with or without color keyboard.

— Priska Frank, 2005
