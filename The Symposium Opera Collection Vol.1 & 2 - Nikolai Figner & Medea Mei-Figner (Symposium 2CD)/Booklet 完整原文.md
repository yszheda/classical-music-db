**Renée Efimovna Radina-Figner (1872-1944)**

The Figners, one Italian, one Russian, studied and began their careers in Italy. After gaining experience in Europe and South America they settled in Russia where they achieved their greatest successes, in opera and in concert, as soloists and in duet. Their records, made in Russia a century ago, and amongst the very first to be accorded Celebrity-Record status, were not issued outside Russia. They are now extremely scarce, indeed, many of the transfers for this album are almost certainly from unique surviving copies. On these two very full compact discs it is possible, for the first time in a century, to form a comprehensive assessment of their singing. The records portray them as soloists and, for what they were even more adored, in duet. Sadly, they went eventually their separate ways. Nikolai married again. His new wife, Renée Efimovna Radina-Figner (1872-1944) was also an opera singer. She too went to the recording studio with him, so we have records of her, though they did not record together.

### The Symposium Opera Collection Volumes 1 & 2

For many people there is an added warmth when romantic attachment on stage extends beyond it into private life. The story of Nikolai Nikolayevich Figner and Medea Mei has such extra romance in great measure. For twenty years in both their private and public lives they were inseparable, as indeed they remain in their posthumous reputations. But there is also great poignancy, as for each of them, their domestic partnership, which coincided with the finest years of their careers, both followed and was followed by other relationships. Mei has been recognized as an important mezzo-soprano in Italy, Spain, Rumania and South America in the early 1880s, but the major part of her career was in Russia, where she achieved even greater fame as Mei-Figner.

This aura of romance has in some respects been intensified by paucity of detail concerning their background and their early careers. All known sources agree that Nikolai was born in St. Petersburg in 1857, of a reasonably well to do Russian family, and intended to follow a naval career. It seems that he developed a belief in his ability to become an operatic artist, and that a romance led to a marriage with an Italian governess named Luisa, at about the same time. The Russian establishment disapproved and he was dismissed. Nikolai and Luisa went off to Italy and had two children. He took singing lessons, from Lamperti amongst others, and started a new career. Dates are vague, but all this in, effect the official history, seems to have happened in the years 1880-1883.

However, there was also a sister, Vera Nikolayevna, born in 1852 at Kristoforovka, Kazan Province. After medicinal studies and marriage, she became interested in education of peasants in rural areas, including political enlightenment, which was incompatible with belief in the paternalistic, all-protecting Tsar. In 1879, in consequence of a major split within the Party of Land and Freedom, of which she was a member, she went with the terrorist faction, which formed itself into the Party of the People's Will. As a member of the executive of that party, she helped plan the assassination of leading politicians and of Tsar Alexander II, which took place in 1881. After contriving to avoid arrest she continued her revolutionary activities in the south. However, in 1883 she was arrested, and the following year she was sentenced to penal servitude to capital punishment. This was commuted to life imprisonment. She served 20 years in solitary confinement, followed by a further two years exile in Archangel. In 1906 she was released and allowed to live abroad. In 1915 she was a member of the Party of Russian Socialist Revolution. In 1915 she was able to return home, where, until her death in Moscow in 1942, she occupied herself with social work and writing. It is believed that the legend of her punishment and her eventual release, were not unconnected with her career, certainly they occurred when he was at the height of his popularity.

He may have been dismissed from the navy on account of his marriage; he may have been told that his sister's activities in his homeland, at the height of her notoriety, would make it secure a friendly senior officer, he realised he would be better off abroad for a while, or his departure for Italy may have been pure co-incidence. These, and other similar explanations, are entirely speculative; quite possibly several factors decided him.

It is unclear precisely when Nikolai first appeared on the operatic stage. It seems likely that his first significant engagement was as Faust, with a company at the Teatro Tosi-Borghi in Ferrara. He was praised for both his vocal and acting abilities. The following September he appeared in Donizetti's Lucrezia Borgia in a minor theatre in Siena, with Medea Mei as Leonora; this, presumably, was their first appearance together.

The beginnings of Medea's career are seemingly well-charted in an autobiography written in 1932. Whilst she identifies a considerable number of early performances, very few dates are given. Her family was relatively poor, they gave 1859 as the year of her birth; her mother died young; her father disapproved of a singing career. It seems that Medea began her studies at the Florence Conservatory in the horn and piano departments, and was subsequently taught by her first teacher, Professor Bianchi, propelled her towards a lower repertoire. She made her début in Sinigalia, not far from Florence, as Azucena in Il Trovatore. According to Florentine records, Medea's operatic début was in January 1874, before her fifteenth birthday. This seems highly improbable. Medea herself claimed to have been sixteen when she appeared at Sinigalia and there is no doubt that she performed with great success at that age.

It is not unusual for singers to modify their dates of birth to seem younger, but as Medea survived to an apparent age of 93, this seems less understandable. Interestingly, when she joined the French representative of the Gramophone Company in Paris in 1933 she declared herself as being "born in 1860"—although she was then seemingly 74. In the autobiography Medea suggests that as a result of her success at Sinigalia she was engaged by the Teatro della Pergola in Florence, immediately after her début, as the Queen in Thomas' Hamlet at the Teatro Pergola in the same city, with the great Victor Maurel in the title rôle. Given that this performance, in fact, took place on 17 December 1880, there seems to be four or five missing years.

The performance of Hamlet brought recognition. Whilst most attention was focused on Maurel, the young Medea was warmly applauded. She felt she owed a great deal to the help, the encouragement and the instruction of a month later when she again sang the Queen at the Teatro Regio in Turin music critics were already predicting a great future for her. During this Turin season she was also appearing with Maurel in no less than 11 other performances of Granmann's Melusine. ### Recording Notes

All tracks are sung in Russian except as indicated.
All but tracks 22-29 on the second disc, were originally recorded by the Gramophone and Typewriter Co. and its successor, the Gramophone Company. The piano in these sessions is further back than was usual, and when heard closely, often gives the impression of a grand rather than of an upright.

All of the recordings by the Figuer夫妇 which exist, almost certainly, in only one or two copies, and few, if any, of them appeared outside Russia. Thus the listener will be the more grateful to those who have made available, and understanding when listening to examples which have not survived in pristine state. A considerable number of titles were remade very quickly, perhaps to exploit advances in recording techniques, perhaps to replace worn or damaged metal parts. Whatever the reason, the effect is to add yet further to individual items. Medea's records disappeared first from the catalogue and are, consequently, of even greater rarity than those of Nikolai.

In his career wind-up of his records were released available were red-label black-label status, and his later records were issued in the even cheaper "Zonophone" series, where he was joined by Renée.

### Notes on the Artists

Much more research is needed into the lives and careers of both Medea Mei and Nikolai Figner. Extremely little is known of Medea's family. Both Medea and Nikolai are reported to have had previous marriages, but virtually nothing is known about their spouses. The circumstances of Nikolai's departure from Russia are similarly enveloped in obscurity. One writer refers to many alleged early performances by both artists, but places and dates are vague. Figner is said to have had lessons at the St. Petersburg Conservatoire from Pyschinskaya and Everardi before leaving Russia, and on arriving in Italy, from de Roxas in Naples; Medea Mei, sometimes Mey, may at one time have been Amedea Mei Zoviade.

Investment in factual details and relationships is not to uncover what should be afforded decent obscurity, but for more affectionate development. Only in recent years, for example, has it become clear how much of Enrico Caruso's career was built on foundations laid by the Giachetti sisters.

Professor Henig and Symposium Records will be glad to hear from any readers with access to original sources, so that new information can be included as an appendix to a future booklet in the series.
