## Synopsis by Raymond Leppard (剧情简介)

The opera is set in the city of Fez in North Africa.

### ACT I

Ormindo, Prince of Tunis, and his friend Amida, Prince of Tremisene, have both fallen in love with the young Erisbe, consort of the elderly King of Morocco, Ariadeno – despite the fact that Amida has already sworn eternal love to Sicle, Princess of Susio. Sicle, disguised as an Egyptian, arrives with her nurse Erice and her lady-in-waiting Melide in search of Amida, who fled on learning of his marriage to Sicle.

Erisbe is flattered by her suitors' compliments and tells her confidante Mirinda how she regrets being married to Ariadeno. She cannot choose between Ormindo and Amida and tells them that they must share her favours. Even King Ariadeno looks kindly on the two princes, who have both helped to defend his kingdom. Sicle, still disguised as an Egyptian fortune-teller, reads Amida's hand and reveals that he is a faithless deceiver; on hearing this, Erisbe rejects him and bestows her love exclusively on Ormindo, who persuades her to elope with him.

### ACT II

Erice, pretending to be a magician, summons up the 'spirit' of Sicle, telling Amida that this is someone whose love's betrayal caused her to commit suicide. Overcome by guilt, Amida once more declares his love for Sicle, who is then 'miraculously' restored to life.

A storm prevents Ormindo's and Erisbe's flight and they are captured. King Ariadeno orders his captain, Osmano, to poison the adulterous couple. Osmano, encouraged by Mirinda, decides to substitute a strong sleeping draught for the poison. Meanwhile, Mirinda regrets his order to murder the young couple. When Osmano reveals that he did not carry out the order after all, Ariadeno magnanimously renounces his young wife and abdicates in favour of Ormindo and Erisbe.

## Liner Notes by Anne Schneider (说明文字)

Francesco Cavalli, who, after Monteverdi's death, became the acknowledged first musician of Italy, was born in Crema in 1602. His baptismal name was Pietro Francesco Caletti-Bruno and his father was an impoverished organist at the Cathedral of Crema. Federico Cavalli, whose son, at the age of fourteen, attracted the attention of Federico Cavalli, Podesta (magistrate) of Crema, took the boy to Venice in 1616, at the end of his term of office. Federico Cavalli's family had been banished from Venice, but his father's death left him as heir to the estate, and he was able to return. Cavalli entered the choir of St. Mark's as a soprano, where he received his musical education, and studied with Monteverdi. He rose to the position of second organist and first organist; finally in his old age, he was Music Director at St. Mark's. In 1639 he composed his first opera, *Le nozze di Teti e di Peleo*, for the Teatro SS. Giovanni e Paolo. In 1639, the year that Monteverdi revived *L'Arianna* for the opening of the new Teatro San Moisè. Cavalli wrote first for the San Cassiano theatre, the oldest of the opera houses, which stood near the present-day Accademia. He continued to produce operas there each year until 1646, when he wrote *La Didone*. In the years following that great work he produced fewer operas for the stage, fulfilling occasional commissions in Milan and Florence; he remained in Venice until 1656 when an event took place which was at once the greatest compliment to his genius that he ever received and the greatest blow. Cardinal Mazarin, who virtually ruled France during Louis XIV's minority, was anxious to celebrate the end of the Fronde and the young King's wedding. Nothing less than a new opera by the great Cavalli would satisfy him. He tried to persuade Cavalli through the person of the French ambassador in Venice, but Cavalli was going to undertake the commission. Cavalli, already in health, was at first very reluctant to accept, but pressure was put on him both by the French and the Venetians, and at last he agreed to make the long journey to Paris and there compose (in this present instance performance) an opera entitled *Ercole amante* in honor of the Royal love and his bride. Cavalli went to France in 1659 and died and Cavalli lost a valuable protector in 1660. In spite of this, Cavalli composed an opera, *La Didone*, which was a great success.

In January 1676.

The actual amount of music he composed during his life is astounding. Between the years 1639 and 1666 he produced over 40 operas, beginning at the rate of one a year but increasing, as his fame spread, to a record of four operas in 1651. The speed at which he worked is evident in the manuscripts of these works, which bear signs of his haste and last-minute changes. As a libretto to music, he composed it straight through without stopping for more than a few days. In a character, generally a serious man, on the contrary, Cavalli was very different. In his private life he showed an utter lack of scruple in dealing with anyone who stood in his way. As a result, Lully's ballets were acclaimed and Cavalli's work was greeted with respect—but no enthusiasm.

Cavalli returned to Venice tired and disillusioned, vowing never again to write for the stage. He accepted the final direction of the stage made Maestro di cappella at St. Mark's, and although he was persuaded to compose three more operas, he devoted most of his last years to religious music, even composing a Requiem for his own funeral. He died in January 1676.

The middle years of Cavalli's working life were not made easier by a sad decline in public taste, which made his colleagues' more notable passions and emulating the distinction of classical drama was soon swallowed up in the general desire for more and more visual spectacle, and already, the cult of the virtuoso singer had begun: to make them more important than the characters they portrayed. It was particularly unfortunate that the castrati should have come so soon into vogue. They were a freak of nature, produced by some unfortunate accident or deliberate mutilation, and their high, unbroken voices made or female and either emasculating the other, was bound to result in the moral and physical degeneration for the weaker librettos to resist.

The plots of many of these operas are such a ridiculous complexity that it is doubtful if anybody could have followed them. It is not clear what was happening on the stage once the disguises had got under way: it is not only improbable, for instance, to have two pairs of brothers and sisters, all separated since birth, who meet, recognize each other and then separate again, but it is even more incredible that the operas all dressed on sixpence for no very good reason, and falling in love from time to time in the most unsuitable habits possible. Cavalli was forced to set a number of these poor libretti which no doubt that these works, although no longer dramatically viable and will remain so until public taste suffers a similar debasement.

But at the beginning and at the end of his career, to comment generally, he was most fortunate in his collaboration with men like Francesco Busenello, who also wrote the libretto of *L'incoronazione di Poppea*, Nicolò Minato, who wrote the libretto of three of his late operas, and Giovanni Faustini, who wrote the libretti of several of the earlier operas, including *L'Ormindo*. It is, perhaps, particularly surprising in view of his teacher and his own early training that he responded to the best libretti with his best music.

He was above all a great melodist with a power that none of his contemporaries could rival. His melodic invention is dramatic situation with his tunes. And it was in cultivating this fine lyrical gift that he most clearly deviated away from the style of his great predecessor. Technically, the need for recitative was paramount if new horizons in opera were to be explored. The search for an intense expressiveness in the new Italian system was past: that work had been done. What was required now was a sense of form and for expansion within the system that the later generations had thrust on them. Yet he, of all his contemporaries, never lost sight of the early ideals of recitative as a form of musicalized speech. Cavalli's recitative, better than the aria, formed the basis for operatic expression. He made use of the arioso in a different way from Monteverdi, his arias and ariosus grow out of and merge into the recitative. He does not allow the chorus to separate from it. It was a style which was going away before the public demand for more and more arias and ariettas: recitative was, while Cavalli lived, a rapidly dying art, and with it died the ideas which were at the root of opera's beginnings.

The public desire for amusement could encourage a three-hundredth aspect of opera: the solo comic scene. In a character, generally a serious man, on the contrary, Cavalli was very different. In his private life he showed an utter lack of scruple in dealing with anyone who stood in his way. As a result, Lully's ballets were acclaimed and Cavalli's work was greeted with respect—but no enthusiasm.

Cavalli returned to Venice tired and disillusioned, vowing never again to write for the stage. He accepted the final direction of the stage made Maestro di cappella at St. Mark's, and although he was persuaded to compose three more operas, he devoted most of his last years to religious music, even composing a Requiem for his own funeral. He died in January 1676.

In the principal roles as well as in those of the servants, Cavalli's perception of character and his ability to draw it down sharply is evident in *L'Ormindo*: it is fascination to see how the music for the young Queen Erisbe changes, as she herself changes from the happy, half-innocent, mischievous girl, into the lover who is prepared to die for her beloved Ormindo. He, too, develops in a similar way, seeming to mature before our eyes. Very different is the dark colouring which Cavalli gives to the part of the assassin Amida: he is a more dispassionate Princes from Egypt, and the tense, introspective Amida when she is pursuing her vengeance. Very different again are Ariadeno, the aging King, whose final gift has been to win our affection and respect. Above all, Erisbe, is made at first a subject for ridicule (comment by her irrepressible maid, Mirinda), but whose perspicacity and gift of eloquence with Ormindo's real enough, as is her capacity to be driven to attempt murder by poison. Finally, this duality of personality seems to be resolved in the remorse and self-pity of the King, whose 'Un talamo ed un letto' shows what he has done.

The opera as a whole is a fascinating and highly entertaining work, composed of as a light-hearted comedy in which the two pairs of lovers seem to be working out their destiny with all the confidence of youth. Only why the colours darken, and the atmosphere is made to change is not clear but the cause of these changes is seen in Sicle's anguished outbursts which her act as a fortune-teller fails to conceal, and also in the long scene in which Amida has been unfaithful to someone else and so hurt him, she decides to abandon him.
