## 内页

# TITO SCHIPA – The Romance of Spain

"It is the best voice, isn't it?" observed one opera-lover with a knowing nod. He had been enveloped in a warm flood of romantic, lyric tenor tone and had succumbed to the special appeal of that type of tenor voice. What is its special appeal? Is it the inherent romanticism which pervades its very tone, or the association of youth and vitality in the characters he portrays? His is a world of the hopeless (and sometimes hopeless) romantic. Not for him are the dark, brooding and frequently brutish roles of the robusto. The lyric is the lover, and frequently the paramour. He speaks directly to the heart; one instinctively responds, identifying with the character and empathising with his situation.

The lyric tenor does have a special appeal, and composers have consequently written their most romantic music for him. He is Rodolfo, Romeo, Des Grieux, Alfredo, Edgardo and Nemorino. The voice is invariably not large, though at its best it will carry to the farthest rows in the house. He will be a master of morbidezza, that quality of tenderness which will suffuse his sound in duet with his lover or the one he desires. Frequently, too, he can call on a certain vocal metal, saved for moments of great passion or the expression of unrequited love, or unleashed rapier-like at a rival. That 'steel' may allow him to portray roles more suited to a heavier voice, such as Werther. Such will serve only to enhance his versatility. Yes, perhaps it is the best voice, and its most famous exponents have been justly celebrated. Few would argue that one of the greatest of them, this century, has been Tito Schipa. For many, he is the quintessential lyric tenor.

During a career which began in 1909 and did not end until 1963, after a long series of concerts, he covered himself with glory.

Tito Schipa was born Raffaele Attilio Amedeo Schipa in Lecce, in the very 'heel' of Italy on 2 January 1889. The 'Tito' was a derivation of a term of affection or nickname which stuck with him. Lecce at that time had a thriving musical community and a promising voice was discovered by his musical mentor, the composer Giovanni Albani, while Tito was still at school. At the age of about 14 he began musical studies with the celebrated maestro Alceste Gerunda (himself a pupil of Mercadante). Schipa thrived under Gerunda's tutelage, not only developing a sound grounding in piano and composition but forging a sound technical base for a voice which was already placed naturally. He then studied with Emilio Piccoli in Milan, who polished and refined his voice ready for an operatic debut.

This occurred on 4 February 1909 (when the tenor was just 20 years of age) at the Teatro Facchinetti, in Vercelli, in *La Traviata*, with the soprano Lina Simeoli. The years 1910 until late 1912 were formative for the younger tenor in which he sang in such operas as *Adriana Lecouvreur, Mignon, Mefistofele, Rigoletto, Faust, Il Barbiere di Siviglia, Cavalleria Rusticana, Zazà, Fedora, La Favorita* and *Tosca*. These were in secondary theatres throughout Italy, such as the Teatro Quirino rather than the Reale in Rome, the Teatro Biondo and not the Massimo in Palermo. However, the first years of any young singer's career are perhaps the most valuable. He soon realised that operas such as *Tosca, Madama Butterfly, Faust, Adriana Lecouvreur, Fedora, Mefistofele, Cavalleria Rusticana* and *Zazà* were unsuitable for his voice. These would gradually be dropped from the Schipa repertoire. He carved for himself a niche of ten roles in which he was supreme: Almaviva, Ernesto, Nemorino, Edgardo, Des Grieux, Duke of Mantua, Elvino, Werther, Alfredo and Don Ottavio.

Highlights from the earliest years include his first encounter with the fiery, but at that time equally green, Claudia Muzio in *La Traviata* at Messina on 9 July 1910. Muzio would sing with him many times throughout his career, usually in that opera. Possibly the first house of importance at which he sang was the Dal Verme in Milan, that city's second theatre, again as Alfredo, on 28 December 1912. In 1913 he made the first of many journeys to South America, where he made his debut at the Teatro Colón, Buenos Aires, on 16 June as Elvino in *La Sonnambula* with Maria Barrientos (a great favourite there) and Alfredo Brooli. She was also his partner in *Mignon* and *La Traviata* that season.

Following his debut at the San Carlo as Fenton in *Falstaff* on 26 December 1913 there was no looking back. One by one he encountered the major houses of the world. First the Costanzi in Rome on 16 April, 1914 in *Don Pasquale*, with a collector's dream of a cast: Storchio, De Luca and Kaschmann, then the Municipal in Rio on 20 July, 1914 as Alfredo, do with Storchio and Sammarco, followed by the Municipal in São Paulo on Des Grieux in Storchio's Manon on 9 August of that year. Returning to Italy he made his debut at the Politeama Genovese in Genoa as Elvino on 31 October and, at the end of the year, he entered the hallowed halls of La Scala, where his debut on 26 December was in an unusual role: Vladimir in *Prince Igor* with Gilda Dalla Rizza, Giuseppe Danise and Fanny Anitua. It was a role he would sing again on only two occasions. He was still only 26.

He had triumphed in all the important houses in Italy and South America. There were still triumphs to come in the major European houses and in America.

One highlight in the Schipa career worthy of mention at this point is his participation in the world première of *La Rondine* on 27 March 1917 at Monte Carlo. He created the role of Ruggero, and his colleagues were Gilda dalla Rizza, Ines Maria Ferraris, Francesco Dominici and Gustave Huberdeau. Gino Marinuzzi conducted. The cast had the great pleasure of working with Puccini, whose presence helped ensure its success. However, the role was not to remain in the Schipa repertoire; he sang it only twice more there in 1919.

He still had one continent to conquer: North America. The Met was slow to secure his services. Quick off the mark was Chicago, and he became a great favourite there following his debut as the Duke in *Rigoletto* with Galli-Curci and Carlo Galeffi on 4 December, 1919. He appeared there practically every season until 1931. Lucky were the audiences who regularly enjoyed performances by Schipa, Bonelli, Edith Mason, Claudia Muzio, Giancomo Rimini, Galli-Curci, Virgilio Lazzari, Kipnis and so many other 'greats'. It was not until that company folded that he made his debut at the Met in December 1932 as Don Ottavio with Ponselle, Pinza, Pasero, Müller and conducted by Serafin. Golden years indeed!

To its eternal shame, Schipa never sang at Covent Garden. But then neither did his great colleague and frequent duet-partner Amelita Galli-Curci.

It would be pointless to try to give the reader more than a flavour of the career of Tito Schipa. His star shone brightly for two more decades, the operatic appearances gradually giving way to endless concert tours of the great cities of the world. Schipa's concert career was as important as his operatic one, and from 1920 onwards, a significant proportion of many years was given over to that facet of his art. Like most great singers he was equally at home on the concert platform, where he excelled in a wide range of music from arie antiche to a simple Neapolitan or Spanish song.

Like many artists active in the '20s and '30s, Schipa made films: shorts for Vitaphone in the late '20s and ten full-length commercial films, the first *Tre Contini in Frac* in 1932 and the last *I Misteri di Venezia* in 1950. Nor should one forget his skill as a composer: he wrote a Mass, in which he participated in the Duomo in Lecce in 1920, and an operetta, *La Principessa Liana*, which he conducted at Milan's Teatro Lirico, in Lecce and for Italian radio, all in 1935. In addition, he wrote several Italian and Spanish songs, four of which are on this CD.

Schipa's recording career was one of the longest of any singer. His first records were made in 1913; the last an 8-inch extended-play microgroove disc during a concert tour of Russia.

Tito Schipa died on 16 December 1965 in New York. Little more than two years earlier he had been still active, singing the occasional concert.

Perhaps the last word should come from Beniamino Gigli, who wrote:

---

### Producer's note

Though there were many fine tenors singing in the 1920s, 1930s and 1940s who were endowed with greater vocal potential than Schipa, when he sang we all had to bow down to his greatness.

**JOHN WILLIAMS**

Schipa's Spanish records fall into two categories: the very common such as *A Granada* and *Ay!-Ay!-Ay! (1 and 25)* and the really quite rare such as *La Granadera* and *Rosalinda* (21 and 5). The latter were never ordinarily available in Britain being for the Spanish market, especially South America. Sadly, when they do appear they are usually so badly worn as to be unusable. Some of the rarest of the discs on this CD were fine copies from the Norbeck and Peters collection (New York), others from the collection of Larry Lustig (England). Without their help this CD of some of Schipa's most delightful recordings would not have been possible.

Amongst the 25 tracks given here are 4 where the music was written by Schipa. They prove that he was not just a superb and unique singer, but a talented songwriter skilled in the Spanish idiom. His writing like his singing tended to be understated, and the more powerful for that; he let the music speak for itself.

A number of the recordings are from the beginning of the electrical recording era (Spring 1925) e.g. *A Granad* and *Madrigal Espagnol* with greatly increased fidelity of voice compared with the older mechanical (acoustic) process. The engineers however still had to work on the orchestral sound which has that characteristic thinness of very early electrical recording. Somehow though it only adds to the charm of the material.
