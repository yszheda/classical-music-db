**FRANZ LISZT**
**WORKS FOR PIANO AND ORCHESTRA**

The most noteworthy aspect of this disc is, of course, the appearance of Franz Liszt's Piano Concerto No. 3 in E-flat Major, Op. posth., which languished for 150 years after it was composed, but which does merit a place beside its two better-known cousins.

In 1988, the American musicologist Jay Rosenblatt, working on a Ph.D. dissertation on Liszt's music for piano and orchestra, unearthed the previously unknown concerto during routine archival research. The discovery was widely reported and caused considerable excitement, leading to performances of the new work. The New York premiere on 3 January 1991 featured pianist Jerome Lowenthal, with the New York Philharmonic conducted by Zubin Mehta.

Rosenblatt reported his findings in a short article entitled "A Recently Recovered Liszt Concerto," in the journal of the American Liszt Society 26 (1989): 64-66, and more recently he edited the orchestral and two-piano scores published by Editio Musica Budapest.

As Rosenblatt put it in his article, the posthumous concerto "was apparently misplaced or lost during [Liszt's] lifetime, and then persistently miscatalogued by archivists after his death." A complete manuscript of the concerto, with some portions of the solo part missing, was located in the Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv in Weimar, where it had been catalogued as rejected material for what we know as the Piano Concerto No. 1, which is in the same key, E-flat Major. Not until 1983 did a scholar – Michael Saffle – surmise that the manuscript might be 'a draft of an otherwise unknown piano concerto.' (See Saffle's 1983 article 'Unpublished Liszt Works at Weimar,' in the JALS 1:3.)

When Rosenblatt's research took him to Budapest, he was shown a photograph by M.E. Satkova-Shcherbacheva from the M.E. Satkova-Shcherbacheva Public Library in Leningrad, of a Liszt manuscript that proved to contain the bulk of the autograph for the posthumous concerto. Again, this had been thought to be drafts for the Concerto No. 1; indeed, the pages of the autographs of the two concertos were mixed together – or shuffled like a deck of cards," as Rosenblatt later told the New York Times. Whoever had catalogued the mixed-up manuscript had assumed that all the pages were for the Concerto No. 1 – they were all in E-flat, after all – and had numbered them consecutively, even though there was no musical connection from page to page. Three pages of the posthumous concerto were missing from the Leningrad autograph, but Rosenblatt later recovered them: one is in the German National Museum in Nuremburg, but the other two in the Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv. (How the autograph became so dispersed is a mystery, though it is known that a Liszt pupil took manuscripts from his house as mementos after his death.) Collating the autograph sources in Leningrad, Nuremburg, and Weimar, Rosenblatt still found about thirty measures missing, but these could be readily recovered from the copyist's manuscript.

The Leningrad autograph allowed Rosenblatt to explain the gaps in the Weimar copy. Liszt had crossed out some solo passages in the autograph and asked his copyist to fill them in, but his assistant never made the intended revisions, but the original passages are still visible beneath his deletions in the autograph. The opening motive of the concerto, for example, turns up near the end of the finale, now transformed into a march: the chromatic ture (Alla breve/Piu mosso). The beautiful slow rising theme that opens the Adagio quasi is transformed into a swaggering, common-time march at the beginning of the finale (Allegro marziale animato), while a second lyrical theme in the slow movement, introduced in C Major by the flute and marked dolce espressivo, is reworked in a scherzando texture in the finale. Thematic material from the scherzo section also reappears near the end of the work. The finale in fact contains no new material of its own: it is a summary and transfiguration of previous themes, with the opening motive returning triumphant at the very end in a closing gesture appropriate to this cohesive structure. This finale is the greatest achievement of the concerto. With its ingenious thematic transformations, satisfying formal plan, brilliant orchestral writing, and assured orchestration (note the witty addition – if once notorious – use of the triangle), the Concerto No. 1 testifies to the maturity that, by middle age, Liszt had attained as a composer.

The concerto seems to have been completed in the fall of 1839, when the twenty-seven-year-old Liszt was living in Italy with the Countess Marie d'Agoult. Rosenblatt believes that the first drafts of all three concertos were finished around this time, though up till now the Concerto No. 1 has usually been assigned a date of no earlier than 1849. (All three concertos are written on an unusual type of paper that Liszt seems only to have used in the latter part of 1839, and the orchestral score for the Concerto No. 2 in A Major does bear the date "Pesth, September [18]39".) In fact, Liszt reported at this time, in letters and diaries, that he was planning to embark on a concert career in the near future. "He may have used this extended time in Italy away from the concert stage to compose music that he could use on his tours," Rosenblatt suggested, "and all three concertos may have been prepared with immediate performance in mind."

There is no evidence, however, that any of the concertos was used in the way Liszt in 1839 form; he tended rather to maintain his solo transcriptions and operatic paraphrases, and if a concerto was required he favored Weber's

Konzertstück in F Minor. But it may also be that he was dissatisfied with the concertos as they stood in the original drafts.

Liszt may actually have begun the posthumous concerto as early as 1836. In March of that year, he wrote from Geneva asking his mother to send him copies of some of his early published works, among them the Variations sur un thème original dédiées à Sébastian Érard – his Opus 1, composed in 1824 when he was just thirteen – and the Deux morceaux dédiés au comte Amadé. And as Rosenblatt notes, "the Érard variations and motives from the two works dedicated to Amadé were ultimately incorporated into the [posthumous] concerto."

So we have three, not two, piano concertos by Franz Liszt. Yet there are major musical differences that set the newly discovered concerto apart from the published pair: differences both of genesis and structure. Most important, the concertos we know as Nos. 1 and 2 were conceived intensively in mid-1839. By this time Liszt had retired as a touring virtuoso pianist and had settled in Weimar, devoting himself to composition and to his role as advocate for colleagues in the

**Totentanz** (Dance of Death), a set of variations on the Dies Irae (the traditional Gregorian chant for the dead), occupied Liszt from 1838 to 1859. It was first performed at The Hague in 1865, with Hans von Bülow at the piano, and was published in the same year, with von Bülow as dedicatee.

Conflicting accounts of the work's conception have been offered. It has long been believed that Liszt was inspired to write it after viewing the renowned *Il Trionfo della Morte* (The Triumph of Death) in Pisa, once thought to be the work of the fourteenth-century painter and sculptor Traino Orcagna but now attributed to Francesco Traini. In the twenty-seven-year-old Liszt saw the fresco while traveling through Italy with Marie d'Agoult, and it seems to have made an impact on him. (In a letter to Berlioz, dated 2 October 1839, he compared Orcagna's vision to that of Michelangelo and Dante.) As Roger Collet wrote in a chapter on the piano and orchestra version in *Franz Liszt: The Man And His Music*, edited by Alan Walker (New York: Taplinger Publishing Company, 1970), p. 273:

> The fresco portrays a series of ghastly scenes. Death is flying towards her victims swinging a scythe. A heap of corpses lie in her feet. Souls are rising to heaven while others are being dragged down to the flames of hell. There are open graves containing decaying bodies. The Triumph of Death, in fact, can be described as a set of variations on the theme of death.

This is a compelling story, but more recent evidence suggests that Liszt may have been inspired rather by Holbein's woodcut series *Der Totentanz* (See Sharon Winklhofer: "Liszt, Holbein, and the 'Dante' Sonata," *19th Century Music* 1 [1977]: 15-32.) In any event, the work went through at least three versions: revised versions appeared in 1853 and 1859. The latter is the standard version heard today, and the one used in this recording.)

*Totentanz* is a powerful virtuoso vehicle to be sure, but it is also a finely wrought and imaginative piece of music. "Malédiction" is not nearly as well known as the other piano-orchestra works recorded here. It seems to be played only rarely. (Even though much in the music is interesting and beautifully crafted. Like the posthumous concerto from the same period, "Malédiction" is strong on imagination, weak on technical polish and control of the piano-orchestra works of the 1850s period. Collet refers to "the mingling of Byronic defiance, great tenderness and a touch of religious sentiment," and to a resulting "mosaic-like" structure. Three distinct acts or sections of distinct characters are presented, then developed and juxtaposed throughout, creating a kaleidoscopic effect – a texture exploring a wide range of keys, moods, textures, and sonorities. Like the other works on this program, nearly all of Liszt's piano-orchestra works – "Malédiction" is an experiment in single-movement form: these works demonstrate that with Liszt balancing variety and unity was a basic priority, and hence his tendency to combine elements of variation and sonata form. Experiments in orchestration are less a priority here, of course, the accompaniment being for strings alone. In fact, the piano is even more dominating a force in "Malédiction" than in the other three works.

It is performed here twice: once in the standard published version with string orchestra, once in a new version with string sextet. This latter version is also the fruit of Rosenblatt's research. His supposition is that "Malédiction" is better thought of as a work for piano and six solo strings, and he offers the following reasons to support it.

First there is no need to assume a string orchestra based on the surviving manuscripts: these sources give every appearance of chamber music, and it is a mystery why the editor of the first edition chose to designate it a concerto. Second, there are no other known concertos from this time that use only string accompaniment: this then survives as a small experimental intermedium. Rosenblatt summarizes his view succinctly: "Malédiction" is a Piano Sextet, and it is a shame he would argue otherwise that he have the burden of proof."

The sextet version performed here, moreover, restores an important musical detail not given in the published version: a short interpolation that quotes Schubert's song "Du bist die Ruh". (Further evidence of programmatic intent?...) Liszt must have had second thoughts about the musical appropriateness of this quotation, for he later struck it out, but it remains biographically telling, offering us a little more evidence of his admiration for Schubert. And not only for Schubert's songs: surely Liszt is courting association with thematic transformation within large-scale one-movement forms owes much to Schubert's beloved brooding "Wanderer" Fantasy of 1822, a solo piano work whose four sections (like those of Liszt's Concerto No. 1) mimic a symphony and whose themes are the constant metamorphosis of its opening motive. It is perhaps fitting, then, that Liszt should have borrowed the "Wanderer" Fantasy for piano and orchestra. ## VANCOUVER SYMPHONY

Founded in 1919, the Vancouver Symphony is the third largest major symphony orchestras in Canada and the seventh largest performing arts organization in the country. The Vancouver Symphony Orchestra presents almost ninety concerts annually in the historic Orpheum Theatre including a variety of series (4 classical, 2 light classical, 1 pops, 1 silent film, 1 children's series) as well as a host of special events and school concerts.

Since resuming operations in 1988, the Orchestra has regained and exceeded its previous levels of service to British Columbians as well as established a sound national and international reputation. The VSO continues to spread themselves to the whole of British Columbia through its annual provincial tour, runout series and concerts throughout the Lower Mainland, annual outdoor summer concerts, and annual participation in the Dragon Boat Festival. Furthermore, the VSO has revitalized its educational program, and now reaches more than 80,000 school children annually through in-school performances and special school performances and the Orpheum. In 1991 the VSO opened the Great Canada Festival in Tokyo where performances received rave reviews and have resulted in invitations from international labels for recording engagements.

## SERGIU COMISSIONA, CONDUCTOR

Maestro Sergiu Comissiona first appeared with the VSO in 1979 and is now in his third season as Music Director. As well as his duties in Vancouver, Maestro Comissiona holds posts as Music Director and Chief Conductor of the Helsinki Philharmonic, Chief Conductor of the Orquesta Sinfonica de RTVE (Madrid) and Conductor Laureate of the Baltimore Symphony.

Comissiona began his musical training in his native Romania and in 1960 emigrated to Israel to become Music Director of the Haifa Symphony. Since then, Comissiona has led every major world-class orchestra, and has appeared at the most prestigious festivals, including those at Tanglewood, Aspen and Saratoga. Also an eminent opera conductor, Comissiona served formerly as Guest Conductor and Music Director of the New York City Opera. A strong proponent of music education, he is the Founder of the Baltimore Symphony's national competition for young American conductors.
