## Liner Notes by Jonathan Bassendale

### Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750)
### The Complete 'French' Suites

Whilst the chamber music of Bach's **Köthen** days was written mainly for groups of Prince Leopold's fellow musicians, his keyboard music was composed largely for other purposes. Although he frequently played his organ and harpsichord to entertain his immediate family or as support material for his activities as a teacher of keyboard technique, at the same time not only did Bach's wife and children count amongst his pupils, he also had private students **who** often lived as members of his household. In 1719, Bach compiled an *Auffrichtiger Clavier-Unterricht* or *True and Candid Instruction in the art of good keyboard playing* for his nine-year-old son Wilhelm Friedemann. Commonly known as the *Clavier-Büchlein vor Wilhelm Friedemann Bach*, it contains not only a sound grounding of keyboard technique, but also explanations of clefs, fingering and, importantly, ornaments, which is still relevant amongst musicians and scholars to this day.

Similarly, Bach compiled the *Clavier-Büchlein vor Anna Magdalena Bach* (**for** Anna Magdalena's instruction, these **manuscripts were** first described by Bach's biographer Johann Nikolaus Forkel in 1802 **as the "French Suites"**). **Forkel** stated that they were written in the French style. His rationale is unconvincing: by the time Bach was resident at **Köthen**, he was more than conversant with the French style and, had he decided to write suites in the same genre, he would probably have adopted a more consistently **French** technical and musicianship.

Intended for Anna Magdalena's instruction, these suites were first described by Bach's biographer Johann Nikolaus Forkel in 1802. Forkel wrote that 'the first five of six suites that have been subsequently become known as the French Suites (BWV 812-817). Although the title is probably not Bach's', since it is not used in connection with either the autograph or any of the copies made during his lifetime.

Unlike The Well-tempered Clavier (WTC), there seems to be no fair copy of the collection as a whole, and although the collection was revised once during the composer's lifetime, there are no definitive versions. With the exception of the first and fourth suites, which seem to be fair copies of works of an earlier provenance, the remainder appear to have been conceived at the time of their entry into the book.

Suites 1-4 were entered gradually throughout the course of 1722 and 1723, only a fragment of the Allemande dates from this period; possibly the remainder **was completed after** Bach's move to Leipzig in April 1723, its completion did not occur for another year and a half.

Since at least one third of the **JSB** is now lost, it is important to determine if the missing portion contained the sixth suite, since direct technical and musical evidence it seems to be somewhat disparate from the **preceding five**. There seems little reason to doubt the authenticity of the collection, and evidence suggests its inclusion was more the result of **mis**association than design. Initially, it is worth noting that the collection, as it stands in **JSB**, is somewhat unbalanced, especially when considering that Bach, following well-established conventions and norms, prepared collected editions of six (e.g. the organ trio sonatas, the Partitas, Inventions, Concertos etc.). Accordingly, it would have been unusual had he not finished this collection with a final work. Moreover, **JSB** has the appearance of a collection that has grown organically over time. Bach takes the guise of incomplete collection with the addition of later material from other composers. Given these two fragments, the c minor organ Fantasia and Fugue, BWV 906, that immediately follow the suites in the manuscript, there can be little doubt that the manuscript book had served its initial purpose. The reason for this might be the Fantasia's unexpectedly demanding, highly musical, **nature. With** Anna Magdalena's formal lessons in harpsichord playing being over, it has been shown that they move to Leipzig interrupted the composition of the suites. In addition, Bach's mounting responsibilities, coupled with the ensuing demands of family life—between 1723 and 1728, five children were born), must have resulted in Anna Magdalena's role changing considerably.

The evidence provided by the sources needs a little further discussion. Gerber's version of the sixth suite includes two extra movements: the **Allemande and** Polonaise. The similarities **of the Allemande** to the Gigue of the suite, particularly the characteristic rhythm, is so marked, which suggests a link between the two. Further to this, it is the same as one found in the other two, the Allemande, prelude in Wilhelm Friedemann's book and the E major English Suite, BWV 806. **Vogler includes BWV 819 in last place in a collection that includes a similar second and third in his manuscript.** Since Vogler had proved himself to be an inaccurate copyist, there is the possibility that these two movements were included by mistake, which Vogler omitted to make the suite more compatible with the remainder of the collection. However, in light of the source provided by Altnickol, this becomes unlikely. Instead, we must consider that the movements' inclusion was more the result of an aesthetic decision by Gerber than an error on the part of Vogler, and that the inclusion of the **Allemande** and Gigue are coincidental. However, it is important to note that both movements include other suites in their manuscripts: BWV 838, in a minor, and BWV 839, in E major. Gerber's manuscripts of suites 4 and 5: Vogler includes BWV 819 in last place in a collection that includes a similar second and third in his manuscript. Given that the content of the two suites is not extra movements, it can be inferred that disparities arise between their respective movements. As a result, it must be concluded that their claims came from a number of sources and suggests that their inclusion was neither the same intention nor function, as Bach's initial compilation.

Anna Magdalena's copy is also revealing. Her manuscript demonstrates that she possessed the technical ability and **quite possibly produced** a keepsake copy which includes the ephemeral material found in **the** JSB. However, her copy shows considerable deviation from Bach's text, and the relationships between this and Vogler suggest her source was also an intermediary one, perhaps containing only references to the original. What is certain, however, is that she had no intention of copying the sixth **suite**.

Altinckol's copy is the only one that contains all six suites in the order that they have come down to us, and evidence provided by the earlier manuscripts suggests that the inclusion of the sixth was his decision. If he had access to **the** JSB, which seems likely, the only possible reasons for choosing the E major suite would be the simplicity of BWV 818, which is predominantly two-part throughout, and the key of BWV 819, which had already been used earlier in the collection.

None of this provides a conclusive argument for the inclusion of the sixth suite into the collection, despite its concise architecture and inherent beauty, it remains somewhat detached from the original intention of the collection. It is important to consider that, with the exception of the first and fourth suites that appear to be fair copies, the remainder of the suites appear to have been composed at the time of their entry into JSB. Thus, in all probability, Bach intended to do the same thing, and although the individual suites, as they appear, are complete, the collection as found in JSB seems as much a fragmented collection of original plan as the unfinished organ fantasia that follows.

The genre of the suite, that is, a combination of instrumentally-conceived dance movements, has its origins in the 16th century. At this time, groups were played for communal dancing. During the sixteenth century, the suite increased in popularity, and the alternation of one dance with another became more common. With the gradual separation of folk and art music, dances became more idiomatic, ultimately achieving a purely instrumentalized purpose. Their collation, usually with the same movement order, became common before the turn of the eighteenth century, especially in France, where they gained acceptance from both composers and the court. Depending on the order of pieces, suites were given various generic names of dances vary. Generally, however, the following pairs are nearly always included: allemande and courante, sarabande and gigue. ## Dance Form Descriptions

**Allemande:** As the word implies, it is a German dance with a binary (AABB) structure. Usually, this is in a moderate movement of the suite. In the case of the *French Suites*, its most unusual treatment comes in the fourth and sixth, where the opening figuration is reminiscent of the style *brisé*, an exuberant lute flourish, sonority through overholding, which finds its parallels with the *Prelude and Fugue in E minor*, BWV 940.

**Courante:** Its name derives from *corrente*, meaning the current of a river. Originally, the dancers imitated the movement of swimming fish to a duple metre; this gradually altered during the seventeenth century to a triple time that alternated duple and triple metres, or were composed entirely in triple time. In the *French Suites*, the rhythmic effect is noticeable, particularly in the first suite, where the tempo is quick, and in the third, where 6/4 is employed.

**Sarabande:** A Spanish dance with origins dating back to the sixteenth century. Originally thought to be lascivious and lewd, it developed a more noble and restrained character in the musical culture of the high Baroque, with a stately nostalgia. The structure is built on a two-beat structure, in which the second and third beats of the first bar often take the form of a dotted rhythm; the second beat is always stressed, as is a minim on which the accent would be played. In the *French Suites*, this maxim is not always strictly observed, however. It is not clear why the underlying historical rhythm is quite different in the two suites are close in style and harmonic richness to the suites in the first grouping.

**Gigue:** An English dance from the reign of Elizabeth I, which has no precise format. The dance requires that the upper part of the gigue should be highly decorated and the bass **against** the ground in time to the music. This form was popular from the time of Louis XIV. It is noticeable that, with the exception of the second Partita, all of Bach's keyboard suites end with a gigue. Generally, he treats **them** in imitation, if not fugal, manners (for example, that of Suite No. 4).

After the four dances that, in terms of the suite genre, are often obligatory, the pieces that follow are more or less included at the discretion of the composer. Usually, they have no historical connexion with dance, and best serve to demonstrate that in Bach's time, the link between the movements of the suite and its historical precedents is distant.

**Menuet:** A courtly dance dating from the time of Louis XIV, it performed by couples who bow to each other, come together and separate. In the following century, its character changed to one of a much lighter and joyful nature, with a faster tempo. When present in the baroque suite, it is usually paired with the sarabande. Normally, when two minuets are to be found in a suite, a trio interlude is included, as in the case of the third suite.

**Air:** Used only in the second suite, the air takes a binary melody-accompaniment format, much in the same manner as that used in the third orchestral suite. Its historical precedent is found in the *Air avec doubles* from Lully's *Le Triomphe de l'Amour*.

**Anglaise:** A dance in 2/2 time, the Anglaise is similar to the gavotte, except that it begins without an anacrusis. Used only in the third suite, Bach evokes the feeling and festive spirit of the seventeenth-century peasant dance, much in the same manner as that achieved by Louis Couperin in his *Branle de Roupie*.

**Gavotte:** A rapid dance in duple time with a half-beat anacrusis. It originates in France, where it was the favoured dance at the court of Versailles.

**Bourrée:** This is similar to the gavotte, except that it contains only two beats to the bar and is usually played at a faster pace. The name of the dance originates in the French verb *bourrer*, the beating or flapping of wings. Like the *Anglaise*, it became part of the court repertoire towards the end of the seventeenth century, possibly because the gigue is slow against the rigid formality of the minuet.

**Loure:** Of rural origin, the Loure found its way into the repertoire of the French court towards the end of the seventeenth century. French harpsichordists often used it as a technical term to describe the rhythmic cadence of the sarabande. In the high Baroque, it is usually played slowly with a heavy emphasis on the first beat of the bar, and in the fifth suite, Bach uses it as to echo the Sarabande, thus reintroducing a slow movement before the Gigue and enabling the balance of the suite's construction to be maintained.

**Polonaise:** As its title suggests, it is of Polish origin. Originally a warriors' march, it became a procession for couples, and was later assimilated into the German instrumental suite mainly through the influence of Telemann. As used by Bach, it is reminiscent of the *mazurek* or *Morris* dance.

## Bernard Roberts

Bernard Roberts has long been acknowledged as one of Britain's finest pianists. A particularly profound interpreter of Beethoven, his recordings of the complete Beethoven Piano Sonatas for Nimbus has been given the highest praise in Europe and America as well as in Britain. He made history by recording the first 'direct-to-disc' Beethoven cycle for Nimbus in the late 1970s and then went on to record the first CD cycle (available on NI 1774).

He has performed at all the major British festivals and has been associated with the Dartington International Summer School for many years. His international career takes him throughout Europe, America and the Far East and he has given masterclasses at many leading festivals including the Van Cliburn Piano Institute in Fort Worth, Texas. Teaching and performing commitments take him on regular visits to Denmark and Germany. Masterclass weekends in his home setting of the mountains of Snowdonia are also proving very popular.

In addition to his recital work Bernard Roberts often appears as concerto soloist with the leading orchestras at home and abroad. He is well known to Radio 3 listeners and he has been the subject of a 45-minute television documentary made for the BBC by the director Mischa Scorer. Time is set aside each year for chamber music and he particularly enjoys performing the piano trio repertoire with his sons Andrew and Nicholas.
