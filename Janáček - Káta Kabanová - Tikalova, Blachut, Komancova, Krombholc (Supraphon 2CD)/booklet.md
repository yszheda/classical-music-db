One of the key elements of JANACEK'S musical inspiration was his deep affection for Russia, a state of mind which accompanied Janacek throughout his life, which can be traced down to his beginnings as a composer, and whose artistic expression culminated in the chamber, symphonic and operatic output of his mature years. While Janacek's russophilia was obviously rooted in his admiration -almost romantic, as it were - for the powerful Slavic nation, he showed a genuine interest in the Russian people and their culture, and closely followed developments on the Russian musical scene. He had intended to star learning Russian while still a student in Skuhersky's class in Prague, eventually managing to get around to it only after his first visit of Russia, in 1896, the year when the future composer of Katya Kabanova had his first sight of the Volga at Nizhni Novgorod, and admired the towers of the Kremlin in Moscow, the architectural sights of St. Petersburg, and the sea. He did not meet prominents of Russia's cultural life then, yet he did absorb the local atmosphere with the utmost sensitivity. Soon after his return from Russia, in 1898, he founded a Russian Circle in Brno, a society whose initial goal was to propagate the study of Russian language and to deepen the knowledge of classic Russian literature. Before long, Janacek was able to read Russian authors in the original. Thus did Russian literature come to offer him an enormous and most intimate source of inspiration to which he would gladly turn on so many subsequent occasions. While he never ceased to rever the heroic past of the Russian nation, he was now able to rid himself of his one time romantic view of Russia, and to see Russian society with all its social evils and iniquities. JANACEK was twenty two when he wrote his first composition on a Russian theme: a melodrama with orchestral accompaniment to the words of Lermontov's poem, Death. He remained faithful to Russian literature as
