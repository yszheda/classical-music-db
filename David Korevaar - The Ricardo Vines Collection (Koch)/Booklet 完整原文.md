## French Piano Music from The Ricardo Viñes Collection

The music on this disc is drawn from the University of Colorado's Ricardo Viñes Piano Music Collection, an important trove of music from the library of the great Catalan pianist who inspired some of the most important piano music by Debussy and Ravel in the first decade of the 20th century. Composers from across the stylistic spectrum sent him their music, assuming that a Viñes premiere performance would be a validation for their work.

Viñes was born in Lleida, Spain, in 1875 and, at the recommendation of Isaac Albéniz, moved to Paris with his family in 1887 to study at the Paris Conservatory. He quickly became friends with the young Maurice Ravel, and, together with Ravel, explored music, art, and literature. In the late 1890s, Ravel and Viñes formed the core of a circle of musicians, artists, and writers who called themselves *Les Apaches*. At various times, the group included Stravinsky, de Falla, Maurice Delage, Émile Vuillermoz, and other composers as members. In the period when Viñes was giving premiere performances of works like Ravel's *Menuet antique*, *Pavane*, and *Jeux d'eau*, the pianist's reputation was more firmly established than the composer's. In fact, even at the 1904 premiere of Viñes by the senior and very well-known Debussy (*Estampes*, composed with Viñes in mind), one critic wrote, "One certes has the right to believe that the three little piano pieces presented by M. Viñes – with such mastery and ability – are of very secondary interest." Other works that Viñes premiered include Ravel's *Miroirs* and *Gaspard de la nuit*, and Debussy's *L'isle joyeuse*, *Masques*, *Reflets dans l'eau*, and *Images II*. Championing the piano music of Erik Satie, Viñes was described in 1913 as "the ideal pianist for the cult of modernism." In the years 1898-1930, Viñes gave Paris performances (many of them premieres) of compositions including Albéniz, Bakaleinikov, Balakirew, Castelnuovo-Tedesco, Chausson, Dukas, de Falla, Fauré, Glazounov, Granados, Lalo, Liapounov, Moussorgsky, Poulenc (who studied piano with Viñes), Rimsky-Korsakov, Rodrigo, Tailleferre, and others. He gave the Paris premieres of Mussorgsky's *Pictures at an Exhibition* and brought back the works of Balakirew and Liapounov after working with them in Russia.

In the 1920s, Viñes toured South America twice, working with many composers of the emerging national schools of Argentina, Uruguay, and Chile. He had a gift of performing new South American music in Europe in this period, apparently with scant public success. Viñes recorded little in his life. His recorded repertoire includes short works by Scarlatti and Gluck along with works by Albeniz, Turina, Borodin, Debussy, Manuel Blancafort, Giovanni Tronci, Lopez-Buchardo, and Pedro Humberto Allende. Viñes's most important legacy is in the composers whom he inspired, from Debussy and Ravel to de Falla, Turina, and Poulenc. He spent the end of his life in Barcelona, prevented from returning to Paris by the Spanish Civil War. He died there in poverty in 1943.

In the 1950s, Viñes's family sold his library through a Paris dealer. The University of Colorado at Boulder paid $575 for more than 800 of Viñes's scores in 1954. The collection, which had been scattered through the intervening circulation events, was reconstructed in 2004 through the work of music librarian Laurie Sampsel and myself. Many of the scores bear Viñes's fingerings, tempo indications, and composer's input. The works chosen for this CD all include notations in Viñes's hand.

French composer, arranger, and conductor Louis Aubert was born two years after Maurice Ravel, and Ravel's influence is evident in *Sillages* (the title derived from the old word for "funerals"; refers to wakes formed behind boats). The association with Ravel is important to pianists: if Aubert's name is known at all, it is because he was a member of the first performance of Ravel's *Valses nobles et sentimentales*, a work written at the same time as, or *Sillages* ten years later, a work that Aubert clearly adored. *Sillages* is a set of tone poems with evocative titles – a response, perhaps, to Ravel's *Gaspard de la nuit*, but without the morbidity. The first movement, "On the shore," evokes breaking waves quite literally in its accompaniments. The second, "Soaring," was inspired by a small church (*Notre Dame de Secours*) in the village of Urrugne, outside of St. Jean de Luz (coincidentally Ravel's birthplace) in the Basque region of France. The proximity to the Spanish border is evident in the music, flavored with habanera rhythms. The last piece, "In the night," brings together motifs from the first two movements in a virtuosic display.

While these pieces sound like French music of the period, Aubert's aesthetic was to eschew the modernist formal and rhetorical experiments of his more famous contemporaries Ravel and Debussy in favor of a straightforward and not unattractive Liszt-inflected Romanticism.

Jean Roger-Ducasse (1873-1954), a member of Fauré's class, and collaborator and friend of Ravel, committed the only string quartet to their master, was very well known in his time — in fact, Gabriel D'Annunzio had considered him to set *Le martyre de Saint Sébastien* before approaching Debussy in 1910. He is best known for his large orchestral works, like *Chopin*, each of his 1908 *Préludes* is obsessed with a single musical idea. The six works together make a kind of cycle, with commonalities in the musical material, particularly evident in the first and last. The style synthesizes elements as diverse as Fauré, Satie, Chabrier, Chausson, and Wagner.

Henry Woollett (1864-1936) was a naturalized Frenchman born of an English family. Although he was mostly self-taught, he did study for a time with the pianist Raoul Pugno and the composer Jules Massenet. He spent most of his career in Le Havre, ultimately establishing a branch of the Paris-based Schola Cantorum (the upstart competitor institution to the long-established Conservatoire) there in 1924. He is remembered as the author of an important music history text, as well as his history of orchestration that he wrote in collaboration with Gabriel Pierné.

Woollett dedicated his monumental and virtuosic *Prélude, Fugue et Final* composed in 1899-1900, to his student André Caplet. As is clear from its title, the work is an homage to César Franck's two largest piano works, composed in 1884 and 1887, especially the earlier *Prélude, Choral et Fugue* — in the same key of B minor. Woollett's piece owes much to Franck in its chromatic language, its obsessive cyclicism, its episodic and often sequential structures, and even its musical material. It represents a conservative strain of composition for its time, 1899-1900. The work was given its Paris premiere by Ricardo Viñes in 1903. The University of Colorado's copy is inscribed to Viñes by the composer.

Vincent d'Indy (1851-1931) was a central figure in French music throughout his long and productive life. He was a prolific composer, an important writer (with biographies of his teacher Franck, Beethoven and his cousin, and the founding of the Schola Cantorum, an institution that was in direct competition with the Paris Conservatory. The *Schumanniana*, op. 30 (1888) is a set of three miniatures collecting aspects of Schumann's style. Viñes's fingering in the first piece are of particular interest, showing numerous organ-like finger substitutions to produce a true legato performance across all the voices of the texture.

Henry Février (1875-1957) was part of the group around Fauré that also boasted Ravel and Roger-Ducasse. He is best known (if he is known at all) for his Maeterlinck-based opera *Monna Vanna* (1909). His son, Jacques Février, was to be one of Ravel's favored interpreters in the 1930s. This Nocturne, composed and dedicated to Viñes in 1906, presents a gorgeous synthesis of Fauréan clarity and Wagnerian richness.

## Pianist David Korevaar

Pianist David Korevaar brings his consummate artistry to repertoire from Bach to the present. His critically acclaimed performances and recordings are only one facet of a career that has encompassed composing, teaching, music as well as scholarship at the University of Colorado.

David Korevaar has been heard at major venues in New York including Weill, Alice Tully Hall, Town Hall, and Merkin Concert Hall. He has performed across the United States from Boston, New York and Washington, DC to Chicago, Cincinnati, Houston, Dallas and San Diego, and he plays frequently in his home state of Colorado with orchestras, in chamber ensembles and in solo recitals. International performances have included appearances at London's Wigmore Hall, and in Australia, Japan, Korea, Abu Dhabi and Europe. Korevaar has performed as soloist with orchestras throughout the United States.

Currently a member of the Boulder Philharmonic chamber ensemble-in-residence, the Boulder Piano Quartet and University of Texas at Dallas's resident Clavier trio, Korevaar has performed to great acclaim with the Takács, Manhattan and Colorado String Quartets.

A founding member of the Young Concert Artists Award-winning piano and wind ensemble Hexagon, on tour he toured for many years.

David Korevaar's most recent CDs include three Beethoven Sonatas, and Ravel's 8 complete *Gaspard de la Nuit* and *Miroirs*. His most musical interests and extensive experience are reflected in his recordings, ranging from the two books of Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* to the piano music of Lowell Liebermann, Volume 1 and Volume 2. He has recorded Brahms Variations, piano music of Hungarian composer Ernst von Dohnanyi, and transcriptions his own and Liszt's of orchestral music of J.S. Bach, including the early harpsichord 2nd Menuetto Waltz.

David Korevaar's interest in new music is reflected in his programming. In addition to his continuing association with the music of Lowell Liebermann, Korevaar has performed and recorded music of other composers including Olivier Messiaen, Donald Martino, Jay Greenberg, Aaron Rochberg, Aaron Copland, Ned Rorem, Stephen Jaffe, Scott Joplin and Libby Larsen. He gave the New York premiere of three of Harrison Birtwistle's *Harrison's Clock* as part of the Juilliard School's Piano Century series in 2000. He is a frequent participant in the University of Colorado's Pendulum new music series.

The University of Colorado recently recognized Korevaar's work on reconstructing the Ricardo Viñes Piano Music Collection with a Faculty Achievement award. Other honors include two prizes from the University of Maryland William Kapell International Piano Competition (1985) and the Pestalozzi-Mason National Guild of Piano Teachers (1985), as well as a special award for performing the music from the Robert Casadesus Competition (1989). In May 2000, he received the Richard French Award from the Denver Press Club, honoring his doctoral research on the works of Claude Debussy.

David Korevaar began his piano studies at age six in San Diego with Sherman Short, and at age 13 he became a student of the great American virtuoso Earl Wild. By age 20 he had earned his Bachelor's and Master's degrees from the Juilliard School, where he continued his studies with Earl Wild and studied composition with David Diamond. He completed his Doctor of Musical Arts from the Juilliard School with Abbey Simon. Another important mentor and teacher was the French pianist Robert Casadesus. Additional mentors and teachers who have studied the music of Fauré and Debussy with Roger-Ducasse (a pupil of Fauré), and the music of Ravel with the composer.

Prior to joining the faculty of the University of Colorado in 2000, Korevaar taught for many years at the Westport School of Music in Connecticut, where he was Artist-Teacher. He now lives in Boulder, CO with his family. Mr. Korevaar is a Kawai artist.
