# Biography by Patrick Lambert (English)

## Page 4-5

[Photo of young Kubelík conducting]

The recordings on this CD can be regarded as definitive. They feature the art of one of the great conductors of the 20th century shortly before and after he had made the most painful and momentous decision in his career: departure from his Czech homeland, with the prospect of permanent exile – an action he believed to be essential to preserve his integrity as an artist. Moreover, the music itself represents that brave and beleaguered little nation at crucial phases of its evidence: national strivings (Smetana and Dvořák), halcyon days of independence (Janáček) and the tragedy of totalitarian domination (Martinu).

The very date of Kubelík's birth – 29th June 1914, a day after the fateful assassination in Sarajevo which sparked off World War I – seemed an omen of the many political upheavals that he was to witness throughout his life. His birthright was a musical family, his father being the celebrated violinist Jan Kubelík. Exceptionally gifted, and blessed with a remarkable memory, Rafael became an all-round musician, graduating from the Prague Conservatoire in composition and violin playing as well as in conducting. He was also a fine pianist, who accompanied his famous father on his world tours. But it was in the field of conducting that Rafael's talent shone the brightest, nurtured by his early association with Talich's magnificent Czech Philharmonic. He made his debut with the orchestra on 24th January 1934 conducting a programme that included his graduation piece Fantasy Op.2, with his father as soloist, and Tchaikovsky's 4th Symphony. "Everything I learned as a conductor, I owe to them. They carried me in their arms".

Kubelík's breakthrough as a conductor on the international stage came in October 1937 when he brought the Czech Philharmonic to England, departing for Edinburgh after the gruelling schedule – 20 concerts plus a recording session for HMV in 27 days – both audiences and critics were completely won over by the spontaneity of their playing.

The following year, a month after the Munich crisis, Kubelík and 'The National Orchestra of Czechoslovakia', as it had been renamed after the Germans took over the country, gave the first Prague Spring Festival, and this symbolic setting the seal of Czech musical independence after centuries of Habsburg rule.

By the mid-1980s, unreliable health, above all unbearably painful arthritic shoulders had forced Kubelík into virtual retirement. The eventual return of democracy and freedom to his homeland in the wake of the Velvet Revolution so rejuvenated Kubelík that he was persuaded to take up his baton again to conduct 'his' orchestra, the Czech Philharmonic, in the opening concert of the 1990 Prague Spring Festival – thus symbolically setting the seal on his country's liberation.

When Kubelík died on 11th August 1996, having been too ill to attend the centenary celebrations of the Czech Philharmonic, president Václav Havel paid him this tribute: "I admired Rafael Kubelík tremendously, not only for everything he did for the glory of Czech music, but also as an extraordinary personality and patriot".

All the music on this CD formed an essential part of Kubelík's Czech repertoire, especially the Sinfonietta by Janáček. He conducted it for the first time during the German occupation in January 1942 in his very first subscription concert as the Czech Philharmonic's newly-appointed Chief – a daring and provocative choice, given the work's intensely patriotic significance. After the

final concert of the 1946 Prague Spring Festival and a few months later he made the present recording.

Kubelík was also a great champions of Martinů's music. Martinů faisait partie de ces compositeurs tchèques vivant en exil dont la musique avait été interdite durant le 'protectocrat' and Kubelik lost no time in making amends by giving the Prague premieres of the Second Symphony, Field Mass and Memorial to Lidice in his first post-war subscription series.

The world premiere of the Fifth Symphony, dedicated to the Czech Philharmonic, provided one of the highlights of the 1947 Prague Spring Festival. That same year Kubelík had invited the Swiss conductor Paul Sacher to conduct the Double Concerto, written for him at the time of the Munich crisis. "The exhibition of international politics that took place in Munich had been a terrible shock and tragedy to me, but I think I succeeded in putting my emotion into a truly classic form", recalled Martinů.

For some reason Kubelík never performed Dvořák's Scherzo Capriccioso with his Czech orchestra, but it was an appropriate piece for him to record with the Philharmonia, since Dvořák himself had performed it as his "visiting card" when first invited to England in 1884. Kubelík would surely have been satisfied by the playing of the Philharmonia's principal horn – Dennis Brain. As for the Legend, the last of the series provided the fill-up side for the original 78s, Kubelík was fond of performing these neglected pieces as a complete cycle. In February 1949, a year before he became chief, he conducted the 10 Legend in a BBC broadcast with the Philharmonia, your before making the present recording.

Finally, the 'suite' from Smetana's Bartered Bride, the opera with which Kubelik introduced himself not only at Covent Garden in 1955, but also earlier in Brno in 1939. After the first night in London, the Times critic wrote: "The phrasing of the overture gave a foxtrot of the vitality that was to stream from the orchestra all evening."

The final comment on these thrilling performances should be left to Kubelík himself: "I try to make records so they have a concert kind of spontaneity".
