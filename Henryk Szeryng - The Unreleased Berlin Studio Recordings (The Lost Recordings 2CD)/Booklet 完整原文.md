## LINER NOTES (English)

### HENRYK SZERYNG

Born in Warsaw in 1918 into a wealthy Polish Jewish family, Szeryng displayed his musical talents at an early age, when his mother began teaching him the piano and harmony. But when still a child, he opted for the violin and studied the instrument under Maurice Frenkel, a disciple of Leopold Auer. At the age of 9, he played for the great Polish violinist Bronislaw Huberman, then based in Vienna. The encounter was decisive: Huberman declared that the young boy had "exceptional talent and the hands of Jacobus Heifetz", and advised him to continue studying in Berlin and Paris. Later on, Szeryng was to say, "I only became a violinist thanks to Bronislaw Huberman." From 1929 to 1932, he studied in Berlin with Willy Hess and then under Carl Flesch. He moved to Paris, where, between 1935 and 1937, he improved his command of the repertoire with Jacques Thibaud and Gabriel Bouillon; he also studied composition under Nadia Boulanger.

It was during this period that his career as a concert violinist took off. At his first concert in Warsaw in January 1933, he performed Brahms's Violin Concerto under the baton of George Georgescu. Szeryng then debuted in Paris, Vienna and Lausanne, where he played the Swiss premiere of Szymanowski's Concerto for Violin and Orchestra.

For Szeryng, the second world war, to some extent, put a temporary halt to his stage career. This pause was nevertheless very enriching. As a volunteer in the Polish army abroad, he gave many benefit concerts over the entire duration of the war. His gift for languages – he spoke seven – were invaluable for the Polish government in exile, and he carried out various diplomatic missions on their behalf. In 1942, during one of these missions with General Sikorski, Szeryng, who by then had taken refuge in Brazil, discovered another country, one whose nationality he took after the war: Mexico. He settled in Mexico City and took up a professorship at the Universidad Autónoma de México. There, from 1945 to 1954, he made profound reforms to the Mexican violin school. In 1956, the Mexican government appointed Szeryng to the Roving Ambassador for Culture and Goodwill. In 1970, he was named Honorary Advisor of the Mexican Permanent Delegation at UNESCO. Szeryng was the first artist ever to travel on a diplomatic passport.

After the war, his career took off with even greater verve. Arthur Rubinstein, whom he met in Mexico City in 1950, was moved to tears on hearing the violinist play Bach, and played a decisive role in his return to the stage. In parallel to his return to live performances, he made a significant number of recordings.

Teaching was important to him and he was zealous about precision in the editing of music scores. In 1980, he brought out a new edition of Bach's Sonatas and Partitas for Solo Violin for Schott Music. When Paganini's Violin Concerto No. 3, long believed lost, was rediscovered in 1971, Szeryng composed the cadenzas for it and recorded the work with the London Symphony Orchestra. He donated several of his valuable instruments, offering them to the city of Jerusalem, the Mexican state and Mexico. These violins are now played by the first violinists of the Israel Philharmonic, the National Symphony Orchestra of Mexico and the Monte-Carlo Philharmonic Orchestra.

Throughout his existence, Henryk Szeryng's life was shaped by music. He died in 1988, two days after a concert he gave in Kassel with conductor Myung-Whun Chung and the Orchester des Saarländischen Rundfunks. He played the very same Violin Concerto by Brahms as that of his early career. His tombstone in the cemetery of Monaco-Fontejg bears the last three measures of the Chaconne from Partita No. 2 by Bach.

### GÜNTER LUDWIG

Born in 1931 in Hesse, Germany, Günter Ludwig studied the piano with August Leopolder and Marguerite Long, and conducting with Kurt Thomas. In both 1954 and 1955 he won the third prize at the Ferruccio Busoni International Piano Competition in Bolzano, Italy. Ludwig worked together with conductors such as Georg Solti and Gunter Wand, with other violinists such as Arthur Grumiaux and Nathan Milstein, and chamber music ensembles such as the Amadeus Quartet as well as the Melos, Sine Nomine and La Salle Quartets. A committed pedagogue, passionate about music publishing, he taught for many years at the Hochschule für Musik Köln, sat on the juries of numerous international competitions, and occasionally worked for the Schott Music and Wiener Urtext Edition publishing companies.

### MARINUS FLIPSE

Born in 1908 in Wissenkerke, the Netherlands, into a family of musicians (his father was an organist, his mother a church organist and his sister an opera singer and well-known composer), Marinus Flipse displayed his musical talents at a very early age, following in the footsteps of his eldest brother, who gave him his first piano lessons. He went on to study in Lausanne, Vienna, Budapest and Paris, where he was taught by Alfred Cortot, with whom he extended his mastery of the French piano repertoire.

Before the outbreak of the second world war, he played with the Amsterdam Concertgebouw under the baton of Van Beinum. Because he played for the Nazi occupants, he was banned from stage appearances for several years afterwards. After the interruption in his career, Flipse performed with many other musical partners. Other violinists included Jacques Thibaud, Herman Krebbers and Theo Olof; he also played with cellist Samuel Bell. Flipse died in 1997.

### Bach: Partita No. 2 for Solo Violin in D Minor, BWV 1004

*"Johann Sebastian Bach's work is a Bible. Bach is the ultimate goal; it is where everything starts and everything ends. His music brings you closer to your own spirit, even to analysing your own spirit and soul. It has an incredible serenity. If people think that a chorale or an Adagio, a Cantilena produces this miracle, I would say that even fast movements, a Presto or a quick Allegro, can make you feel more cheerful, more secure, more vigorous. What moves musicians most is Bach's relationship with Bach, developed from an early age, with the work of the cantor of Leipzig."* Szeryng kept up his intimate association of Bach throughout his life and he played the composer's works frequently over the years. His concert programmes and recordings throughout his career bear this out.

Among Bach's compositions for violin, the Partita No. 2 for Solo Violin holds a special place. It is one of the instrumental dance suites, or variations, of the Baroque period (the word "Partita" comes to us from the Italian and originally designated free variations, called parts, of a popular song), but it has become a monument in the repertoire. What mainly gives the work its monumental aspect is the richness and variety of the writing. The polyphony used is uncommon for a solo violin, leading violinist and composer George Enescu (who considered the complete Partitas to be the "Himalayas of violinists") to say that "if one had to write them for a single violin and five more bows to give us enough force to express what we feel as such a moment." So fascinating was the last movement, the Chaconne, to musicians from all walks of life that it was transcribed for many instruments, not least by Brahms, who wrote a version for piano for the left hand only.

When Bach composed this Partita, he was kapellmeister at the court of Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Köthen, the brother-in-law of the Duke of Weimar. The prince was an outstanding musician. However, as a Calvinist, he would not allow religious music to be composed for the court. Yet music was one of his pillars: eighteen excellent instrumentalists played for the court, and the music budget comprised one-quarter of the principality's budget. This is how Köthen became one of Germany's most important musical centres of the time. The prince was a talented player of the harpsichord, the violin and viola da gamba. In 1717, he became a great patron of Johann Sebastian Bach – a long journey customarily taken by European aristocrats at the time – and travelled to Italy to complete his education. In Rome, the prince studied music with Johannes Heinichen, learned about Italian music, and returned home convinced that profane music should be developed and promoted in Germany. Thus it was that in Köthen, Bach found surroundings that were not only beneficial to him, but also propitious to his work as a composer. (The prince was godfather to one of Bach's sons.) During his sojourn there, he displayed extraordinary creativity in his work for instrumental music, with the composition of the Brandenburg Concertos, the Six Concertos for Violins, the first book of the Well-Tempered Clavier, the Cello Suites and the Partitas for Solo Violin.

Nevertheless, this period was marked by a drama in Bach's life: his first wife, Maria Barbara, died suddenly in 1720. According to some sources, the Partita No. 2 for Solo Violin was written to be a tombstone, a memorial piece for her. Since the very beginnings of Baroque music and the art of dancing, the D minor key was frequently associated with death, mourning and hope in resurrection. In the Chaconne, Bach incorporated two melodies taken from two choral works that accompanied funeral ceremonies: on the one hand, "Christus, der ist mein Leben, sterben ist mein Gewinn" (Christ for me is life, to die is gain, Lord) and "Christ lag in Todes Banden" (Christ lay in death's bonds).

The Partita comprises five movements: Allemande, Courante, Sarabande, Gigue and Chaconne. It can be likened to the instrumental suites of dances so popular during the baroque era. They have a sonata de chiesa structure (that Corelli codified); it is based on alternating slow and fast movements in the same key, but this work has nothing in common with the world of dance. From the very first movement, the melodic line that is developed makes it clear it is a lamentation. The rhythmic variety of the writing, the motifs that stand out in the bass register, a motif appears that returns hauntingly throughout the work until it reaches its full expression in the Chaconne. The lively tempo in the triple-meter dance, the second movement, imparts a particularly restless, painful tone to the piece. In the Sarabande, this pain turns slowly to introspection and the meditative.

### Franck: Sonata for Violin and Piano in A Major, FWV 8

In France, the period that followed the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 was characterized by a questioning of all established traditions, of all political regimes, with immense vitality and a love of music, as Franck's Sonata for Violin and Piano testifies. In 1871, Camille Saint-Saëns and Romain Bussine founded the Société Nationale de Musique, whose motto, "Ars Gallica", was in the desire to revive French music. Its motivation was to be Francophone, and its aim was to promote a national form of music to counter what was perceived as the dominant, so-called Germanic instrumental music – that is to say, the symphony and chamber music, which was considered the prerogative of the founding members of the Société. The years that followed saw their compositional activity focused on the domains reserved for the first members of the Société, namely chamber music.

In this sonata for violin and piano, Bach's Partita No. 2 for Solo Violin holds a special place in the repertoire. It is one of the instrumental dance suites, or variations, of the Baroque period (the word "Partita" comes to us from the Italian and originally designated free variations, called parts, of a popular song), but it has become a monument in the repertoire. Since the very beginnings of Baroque music and the art of dancing, the D minor key was frequently associated with death, mourning and hope in resurrection. In the Chaconne, Bach incorporated two melodies taken from two choral works that accompanied funeral ceremonies.

The Partita comprises five movements: Allemande, Courante, Sarabande, Gigue and Chaconne.

But if we examine the work more closely, the extraordinary inventiveness of the composer shines through and the innovative aspect of his musical language becomes palpable. The piano chords and the intervals on the violin of the first bars of the sonata are transformed into a long melody that is as fluid as it is sensual in its modulations. In turn, we examine anxiously, melodiously and undoubtedly, Franck uses these first motifs as the cornerstone for his work, built like a cathedral. D'Indy spoke of "holy mystery" when referring to these first bars. The listener faces a unique source from which all the themes and melodies of the first movement, as well as of the entire work, develop.

This spring becomes a torrent in the second movement, one of breath-taking compactness. The melody of the violin, with its frenzied clarity turning to intense, freedom-lyricism contrasts utterly with the piano's troubled, nervous richness whose bold modulations add drama to the musical discourse.

The third movement begins in more meditative and melancholic fashion. The piano introduces harmonies drenched in mystery; the long, lengthy lines seem to carry and reflect the course of the recitatives of the violin, the lengthy lines seem to both echo and imbued with something impalpable, even tragic. These lines lead us towards the reprise, variation and transformation of the initial elements of the sonata.

The last movement starts with dominant signs of return to the joy and serenity regained. The violin and piano respond to one another in a light-filled conversation. Franck re-uses some elements of the theme that surge forth again, joyfully animated – but it is as if these are put aside, overtaken by the general elation of the moment. The motifs of the beginning are so transformed as to become almost unrecognisable.

Throughout the innumerable meanderings of this Sonata for Violin and Piano in which Franck breathes new life into the genre, Henryk Szeryng has wrought a work that, in its natural, dynamic interpretation, the warmth of his emotion-filled timbre, and the ringing clarity of his articulation and intonations, he does perfect justice to a work that is essentially balancing between sensual melodies and experimental harmonies.

### Ravel: Tzigane

It is often said of Maurice Ravel's work that it was shaped by influences that were both historical (Couperin, Rameau, Liszt) and geographical (jazz from the USA, as well as Spanish and Hungarian music, of course). As philosopher Vladimir Jankelevitch said in his essay on the composer, "No influence can claim to have possessed him entirely."

When he composed this Concert Rhapsody for Violin and Orchestra, this "virtuoso piece in the Hungarian manner", as the subtitle has it, Ravel was undoubtedly possessed. During a stay in London in 1922, he attended the first performance of Béla Bartók's Sonata No. 1 for Violin and Piano, with the composer himself at the piano and the Hungarian violinist Jelly d'Arányi, grandniece of the great violinist Joseph Joachim, who also collaborated with Brahms. Ravel was fascinated and asked the violinist to play him the tunes of her country and teach him about Tzigane improvisations. Gaby Casadesus, the pianist who had travelled with Ravel, remembers that "D'Arányi, being Hungarian, didn't need to be asked twice and played passionately for at least two hours without stopping. She was sensational and Ravel was mad with joy... very shortly afterwards Tzigane was born." "Quickly, Ravel set to work and wrote it on Bartók about her," we have been inspired no less by wistful melancholy, coming straight to us from the Hungarian plains of György Kurtág. Since it will be for violin, why don't you call it Tzigane?"

Quite unlike the ethnomusicological research that Bartók undertook in his search for authentic motifs to compose his folk music-inspired works, it was "the Hungary of [his] dreams" that unfolds in this piece by Ravel, a country where both exuberance and the very personal imagination of the composer were showcased. In the words of Roland-Manuel, Ravel's student and biographer, it was "not authentic music, so no more realistic than the real thing." In addition, Ravel explored new sounds. Just a few years earlier, Georges Cocteau had suggested to him the idea of making a new mechanism for prepared piano whose sounds were reminiscent of the Hungarian cimbalom (the kithara). And so it was that Ravel originally composed this work for violin and kithara. But this costly new instrument that required complicated maintenance did not meet with much success and disappeared very quickly (a specimen from this period is still in existence, housed at the Musée des Arts et Métiers in Brussels, with a copy at Paris's Musée de la Musique). Ravel wrote two other versions of the piece, one with orchestra and the other with piano to replace the kithara. Tzigane premiered at the Aeolian Hall in London on 27 April 1924, with Jelly d'Arányi as dedicatee and inspiration of the work, on violin and Henri Gil-Marchex at the piano.

This one-movement composition that lasts for about ten minutes is characterized by its freedom of form as well as of tone, reminiscent of improvisation. The rhapsody lends itself well to such freedom – just like the Greek etymology, "one who strings songs together." The form had already been used by Liszt in homage to the music of his native country and to give free rein to his virtuosity. In Tzigane too, virtuosity reigns supreme, and in fact while Ravel was composing the work at Montfort-l'Amaury, Paganini's Caprices were on his desk. The diabolical difficulty, the dazzling virtuosity, Paganini's Caprices were on his desk. The diabolical difficulty, the dazzling virtuosity is visible in a narrative that is composed of bravura bristling through the score. There are double stops, formidable trills, harmonics, pizzicati on the left hand, and more. Far from being gratuitous, this virtuosity serves a work that profoundly regenerates the repertoire for violin at Ravel's time, displaying his idiosyncratic use of the timbre of each instrument to the full. Here are the purity of the lines, the refinement of the harmonies, the clarity of the musical discourse and sense of proportion – the genius of the outline, in Pierre Boulez' words – that so typify Ravel's style and make it so recognizable.

Tzigane begins in the form of a long declamation of 58 bars of solo violin on lento quasi cadenza tempo. Its character evokes both the bravura of concerto cadenzas and the leeway for improvisation they sometimes leave. In this declamation, contrasts play a major role: the work is a germinatory, plaintive, dramatic, then highly expressive and warm through the introduction with its double chord motifs. In the midst of a succession of improvised double trills the piano enters, almost supernaturally, as if to cast a spell. Then the spirit of dance, which, according to André Suarès the writer, "dresses all of Ravel's music," takes hold of the piece. Tzigane is metamorphosed into a Hungarian folk dance: the csárdás and the piano, over the repeated motifs whose writing technique evokes the cimbalom, becomes spider-like, weaving the melody into its web. The atmosphere becomes increasingly incandescent, and then the harmonics. After a passage that is relatively calm, with the piano playing the drone, the csárdás becomes increasingly frenetic. The piano and violin are in conversation, twisting and turning in a whirlwind that only ends with three final chords.

### Beethoven: Sonata for Violin and Piano No. 5 in F Major "Spring", Op. 24

Interrupted just as his career was blooming and with various composers, Henryk Szeryng responding to questions on the violin and with various composers, Szeryng said, "It's not a question of convincing somebody. It just seems to me a question of communicating warmth and true human passions, feeling to the very listener who is watching you, who is listening to you." Szeryng was a fellow ambassador for Beethoven's music as much as for Bach's. On both the stage and on his recordings, he transmitted the heroic generosity of the composer's work. There is no better way of describing this recording of the "Spring" sonata by Beethoven than with Szeryng's very own words: "warmth and true human passion". Here, he gives Beethoven's melodic lines their joyful clarity and imbues them with profound grace.

When Beethoven was composing this sonata, between 1800 and 1802, he was at the very pinnacle of his career as a pianist. He had become the most celebrated of the many musical jesters the Viennese were so fond of, thus earning himself the status of Vienna's greatest virtuoso. This was also a period of intellectual effervescence for him. He had the Greek classics, Shakespeare, as well as Goethe and Schiller, who heralded the Sturm und Drang literary and artistic movement that was apposed to the rationalism of the previous epoch and considered that emotions and the life of the soul were paramount. Beethoven's reading had a profound influence on him, fostering his idealism and passionate temperament. He also adopted the political ideals of the Enlightenment and the French Revolution, whose influence was spreading throughout Europe at the time. These were some of Beethoven's most productive years: he composed his first six string quartets, his first two symphonies, his first three piano concertos, and his major sonatas, the "Pathétique" and the "Moonlight".

Early in the nineteenth century, Beethoven was the pride and joy of Viennese musical life. Yet, in fact he was at the edge of an abyss: his hearing was deteriorating threateningly, to affect him in his very depths, both physically and spiritually. For some time, he had noticed the initial symptoms of deafness that was setting in inexorably. Around 1820, a bewildered him. Terrified, he tried to conceal it lest he be discovered by the public. Beethoven withdrew, isolating himself even though this meant he was wrongly seen as a misanthrope, a view that was deeply wounding for him. Certain that this handicap would mean he would have to renounce his career as a pianist sooner or later, even considering that he would have to abandon composing, he was submerged in despair and thought fleetingly of suicide. In a letter of October 1802 written to his brothers Karl and Johann but never sent, known as the Heiligenstadt Testament, Beethoven expresses not only his extreme desperation, but also his indomitable energy: "Oh Providence – you who see my inmost soul – you know that what fills it is love for man and a desire to do good! Oh men, when some day you read this, consider then that you have done me an injustice; someone who has had misfortune may console himself to find a similar case to his, who despite all the obstacles of Nature nevertheless did everything within his powers to become accepted among worthy artists and men."

The sonata comprises four movements, (Allegro con brio, Adagio cantabile, Scherzo: Allegro, Finale: Allegro). In the first movement, as soon as the theme of the violin, a sort of triple lie is set: this is full Sturm und Drang. The tempest and passion that Beethoven's reading imbued him with. Despite a clear melodic line on the violin, the dramatic – even pathétique – atmosphere is palpable. This makes the contrast with the elegant gentleness of the second movement: a brilliant cantabile melodic line on the violin is taken up again as if in little strokes, pizzicato, by the violin at the end. In the central section, arpeggio chords on the piano and long, sustained notes on the violin predominate.

The scherzo, written in a C major key that exudes joy, highlights the atmosphere of traditional festivities by using the three-beat tempo and the dynamics taken from the ländler of German folk music. Then the dramatic character returns in the last movement, introduced by the repeated bass notes at the piano. Again, tragedy has returned. This highly energetic movement combines the rondo with the sonata form, and the initial motifs articulated by both piano and violin ring out to echo, loop-like, the drama of the first movement.

Once again, Szeryng's performance makes his intimacy with Beethoven manifest. The energy of his interpretation of the sonata nevertheless captures the finest details of the drama of the work.

### Beethoven: Sonata for Violin and Piano No. 7 in C Minor "Eroica", Op. 30 No. 2

Nothing of the private tragedy Beethoven was living through is apparent in his Sonata No. 5 for Violin and Piano. While the work speaks to us of a musical triumph over adversity, and a triumph that prefigured many more in the decade to come. Dedicated to Count Moritz von Fries, one of Beethoven's patrons, it is most certainly one of Beethoven's best-known works for violin and piano. Later on, others named it "Spring" because of its luminosity and the tone of its F major key, often associated with Beethoven's work with nature and bucolic themes. (This is also the case of the "Pastoral Symphony", composed a few years later.)

Comprising four movements, (Allegro, Adagio molto espressivo, Scherzo: Allegro molto, Rondo: Allegro ma non troppo), the sonata is imbued with balance and a spirit of serenity. The first movement, imbued with a first theme introduced by the violin on a sonata form, into a dialogue with a spirited, generous second theme. In the second movement, the violin dialogues with the piano, which takes a back seat; it is here that we find the most beautiful pages of the sonata. In the Scherzo, a return to F major, the tone becomes impulsive, and here Beethoven paints the regate la gaieté, fait la part belle à l'atmosphère populaire en empruntant la rythmique à trois temps et les dynamiques au ländler du folklore musical allemand traditionnel. Le caractère dynamique fait son grand retour dans le dernier mouvement, introduit au piano par une note répétée à la basse : de nouveau, la tragédie est là. Le mouvement, très énergique, combine rondo et forme sonate, et les motifs initiaux énoncés tant au piano qu'au violon résonnent comme une réminiscence en boucle de la dramaturgie du premier mouvement.

Szeryng montre encore une fois sa proximité avec Beethoven que apparaît comme une évidence. L'énergie de sa lecture de la sonate parvient à saisir les moindres détails du drame de l'œuvre.

This sonata, dedicated to Tsar Alexander I of Russia, was composed between 1801 and 1802 and published in 1803 – the same period as the "Spring" sonata. Like the "Eroica", this sonata bears in its first movement the heroic quality of resistance to adversity and of triumph. Yet its character has nothing in common with "Spring", but is more like Piano Concerto No. 3, composed at about the same time, and with which it shares the dramatic tonality of C minor. In this work, Beethoven makes a definitive break with the Mozartian heritage. By increasing the density of his writing, he develops a new type of musical discourse based on an organic link between violin and piano that now looks directly ahead to the aesthetics of romanticism.

Despite a clear melodic line on the violin, the dramatic – even pathétique – atmosphere is palpable. This makes the contrast with the elegant gentleness of the second movement: a brilliant cantabile melodic line on the violin is taken up again as if in little strokes, pizzicato, by the violin at the end. In the central section, arpeggio chords on the piano and long, sustained notes on the violin predominate.

In the Scherzo, the F major key returns, the tone becomes impulsive, and here Beethoven paints the regate la gaieté, fait la part belle à l'atmosphère populaire en empruntant la rythmique à trois temps et les dynamiques au ländler du folklore musical allemand traditionnel. Le caractère dynamique fait son grand retour dans le dernier mouvement, introduit au piano par une note répétée à la basse : de nouveau, la tragédie est là. Le mouvement, très énergique, combine rondo et forme sonate, et les motifs initiaux énoncés tant au piano qu'au violon résonnent comme une réminiscence en boucle de la dramaturgie du premier mouvement.

Szeryng montre encore une fois sa proximité avec Beethoven que apparaît comme une évidence. L'énergie de sa lecture de la sonate parvient à saisir les moindres détails du drame de l'œuvre.

### Bach: Partita for Violin No. 3 in E Major BWV 1006, Prelude

The prelude of the Partita No. 3 is yet another remarkable piece Bach composed for solo violin. In many respects, it is as fascinating for musicians as it is for composers. Bach himself transcribed the entire Partita for solo lute, and Sergey Rachmaninov wrote a transcription for piano of the Prelude, Gavotte and Gigue. Bach himself also transcribed the Prelude by itself for organ with string orchestra, trumpets, timpani and oboe for the overture of his cantata, Wir danken dir, Gott, wir danken dir (We Thank Thee, Lord, We Thank Thee).

In allegro tempo, lively and cheerful, the piece gives pride of place to a number of technical difficulties, making it popular for encores. In fact, this recording by Szeryng was played as an encore. A blithe melody in sixteenth notes makes way for passages in campanella that imitate the tinkling of small bells, something that Vivaldi, during Bach's lifetime, was fond of (Bach transcribed some of Vivaldi's violin concertos for harpsichord). Here, the same E is played alternately on two strings.

Szeryng's deft finger work brings out the full brilliance of the Partita's clarity and joy. His energy, his attacks, his remarkably precise bowing and generous timbre once more bear testimony to the depth and intensity of his lifelong attachment to Bach.

*Anna Svenbro*
