### Edvard Grieg: Piano Concerto in A Minor, Op. 16

The nineteenth-century Edvard Grieg and the twentieth-century Manuel de Falla hail in common a singing sense of nationalism. Hardly a note Grieg composed does not betray his Norwegian background, just as a thoroughly Spanish flavor permeates Falla. Both, too, composed one major work for piano and orchestra. Grieg's A minor concerto is a voluptuous concerto of passion and supple, full of red blood and virtuoso high spirits. Falla's *Noches en los jardines de España* (Nights in the Gardens of Spain) on the other hand is "symphonic impressions for piano and orchestra" and is a subtle, restrained, prismantic score imbued as it were with the heat of his powers.

There is no point describing the Grieg concerto, which is known to everybody and is one of the half-dozen most popular works of its kind ever composed. Grieg completed it in 1868, the year after his marriage, during an idyllic summer vacation in Denmark. It was dedicated to Edmund Neupert, a pianist who was to give the world premiere in Copenhagen on April 3, 1869. Grieg was not present, and Neupert wrote him a letter describing its reception: "On Saturday your divine Concerto resounded in the great hall of the Casino. The triumph I achieved is beyond all words. Even as early as the cadenza in the first movement I felt certain of a real storm. The three dangerous critics, Gade, Rubinstein and Hartmann, sat in the stalls and applauded with all their might. I am to send you greetings from Rubinstein and say that he is anxious to have made a composition of such genius. He would like to make your acquaintance."

This, coming from Rubinstein, was flattery indeed. Anton Rubinstein was one of the greatest pianists of all time, a stern moralist and a musical martinet. His word literally carried life or death interest in the young Grieg; and in 1869 Grieg tucked the manuscript of the A minor concerto into the urn and went to Rome to meet the old master. There followed the kind of *tour de bête* story that only an artist could make up: Grieg was asked to come to the hotel where Rubinstein was staying with a piano. He was shown to a room, left the instrument, and proposed the manuscript on his piano and read it off at sight; Liszt, undoubtedly the greatest pianist at home ever seen, loved to show off his prowess, and Grieg was properly impressed as he later heard him in similar fashion.

"Winding [a pupil of Liszt's] and I were very anxious to see if he would really play my concerto at sight. I, for my part, considered it impossible," wrote a letter. At the work had fallen on stony ground for two years; but when Liszt did play it, he played it so well that the young composer was overwhelmed. "I heard him play the concerto," wrote Grieg later, "and never have I heard anything like it. When he had finished, I was so moved that I could hardly speak."

The Grieg concerto, like all great works of art, transcends its time and place. It speaks to us today with the same directness and power that it spoke to audiences in 1869. Its popularity is not accidental; it is a work of genuine inspiration, crafted with the hand of a master.

### Manuel de Falla: Nights in the Gardens of Spain

Like Grieg, Manuel de Falla was a skilled pianist; and, like Grieg, he took his function as a nationalistic composer very seriously. "Our music," he once said, "must be based on the natural music of the people, on their songs and dances, and on the characteristics of their language. In our dance and our rhythm we possess the strongest of traditions that none can obliterate. We have the ancient modes which, by virtue of their extraordinary inherent freedom, we can use as independent scales."

And in Nights in the Gardens of Spain Falla drew heavily upon Andalusian popular music and folk-songs, though they rarely are used in their original forms; and the orchestration frequently employs, and employs in a conventional manner, certain effects peculiar to the popular instruments used in those parts of Spain. The music has no pretension to any local description; it is merely expressive. But something more than sounds of festivals and dances has inspired these "evocations" in sound, "for melancholy and mystery have their part also."

Falla had spent the winter of 1907-08 in Paris with his wife. He made no secret of his admiration for the music of Debussy and Ravel; and, indeed, the influence of these two masters is apparent in much of his work. But Falla was never an imitator; he absorbed what he needed from the French masters and made it his own. His music remains essentially Spanish in character, even when it shows the influence of French impressionism.

The work that made him famous was the ballet *El Amor Brujo* (Love, the Magician), which he composed in 1915. It was an immediate success and established him as one of the leading composers of his generation. He followed this with *Nights in the Gardens of Spain*, which he had begun in 1909 but did not complete until 1915. The work was first performed in Madrid in 1916, with the composer conducting and José Cubiles as soloist.

The work consists of three movements, each evoking a different garden in Andalusia. The first movement, "En el Generalife," is named after the famous gardens overlooking the Alhambra in Granada. The second is "Danza lejana" (Distant Dance), and the last one is "En los jardines de la Sierra de Cordoba" (In the Gardens of the Sierra de Cordoba). The three movements are connected by recurring themes and motifs, creating a unified musical narrative.

### Frederic Chopin: Scherzos and Nouvelles Etudes

**Scherzo No. 1 in B Minor, Op. 20 & No. 3 in C Minor, Op. 39**

Beethoven had finally freed the scherzo from the Haydnesque "joke." Schumann had enriched it with the "parlando." Chopin had awakened the very soul of the instrument and had given it an individuality never attained before. To all this, Mendelssohn added lightness of touch. No one had been able to evoke at the piano those fleeting visions, like characters of a fairy tale, who slip from our hands and disappear at the very moment we think we have caught them.

Composers between Beethoven and Mendelssohn seems particularly pertinent in respect to Chopin's scherzos. The form was "invented" by Beethoven, who included it in his sonatas and symphonies as part of a "quasi whole." Its origin in dance-music remained obvious: apart from the Scherzo of the Ninth Symphony, all Beethoven scherzos were in 3/4 time, and the pattern was composed of detached units. In Chopin's hand the scherzo became a complex design; emancipated from the sonata it inherited the latter's spirit in its single movement itself, and as a character-piece attained an emotional intensity whose nervous tensions and vivid landscapes are far removed from the humor that is felt in the majority of Beethoven's scherzos.

The first scherzo, in B minor (Op. 20), was published in 1835 with a dedication to "M. Albrecht." The pianist was then twenty-five, living in Paris and deeply involved in the political turmoil that would result in the Polish uprising against Russian rule. The work reflects this turbulent period in Chopin's life, with its dramatic contrasts and emotional intensity.

There are eight bars of introduction filled out by chords, this is followed by the presentation of the two themes of the (main) section, which will constitute the principal ingredient of the movement. The scherzo is in sonata form, with a development section and a recapitulation. The central episode (trio) contrasts with the first part in its quiet movement and its D-flat key. Its theme is said to be a Polish Christmas carol. The theme is interrupted by a central passage in octaves, leading into the beginning of the complete reprise. The Scherzo comes to a close with a series of ascending scales, which bring the work to a thunderous conclusion.

The third scherzo, in C-sharp minor (Op. 39), was published in 1840 with a dedication to "my friend Adolf Gutmann." Its formal scheme is an extended sonata form of the extended first subject; its complement runs into the transition of the second subject (in D flat), one of the finest, most magically inspired ideas in the entire literature of the piano. The theme itself consists of a succession of sonorous, descending chords, set at the phrase-steps of the delicate tetrachord; an indecisive veil of sound descends from the upper reaches of the piano.

The coda chosen (in B flat) note: the romantic key distribution of C-sharp for the first subject and D-flat for the second — with D-flat in between — was one of Chopin's favorite broken-chord motifs with the chorale theme and its complement.

In the reprise the first subject is slightly altered to yield to the enlarged chorale theme here in the key of E major. The coda opens in E-flat major with another minor-melody, the chorale theme, but makes reference to the octave motif of the first subject as well. The piece concludes with a passage (always in octaves) of thundering octaves.

**Three Nouvelles Etudes**

Two years after the publication of the Etudes, Op. 25, Chopin was asked by the eminent pianist Moscheles and the eminent critic Fétis to contribute to a text on piano playing that they were working on. When he was unable to do so, he submitted instead the three études that were later published separately without opus number but which obviously are (as they are often called) "posthumous works."

The first of these (Andante in F minor) is a study in simplicity: a right-hand melody, a left-hand arpeggiated accompaniment, no problems whatsoever in technique (the "three against four" of the two hands is nothing — the whole so easy that the greatest artist is hard put to bring from it the concentrated essence of a perfect singing tone.

The second of the three (Allegretto in A flat major) is a "two against three" study of a similar kind, given a greater urgency musically by the printed indication "presto con leggierezza."

The last (Allegretto in D-flat major) has a theme consisting of a smooth legato melody and a clean staccato middle voice in the same hand and, even more demanding, the turning of the whole into a charming and unlabored waltz such as Chopin alone knew how writing.

### Piano Concerto No. 1 in E Minor, Op. 11

Chopin's Piano Concerto No. 1 in E Minor, Op. 11, was really the second in order of composition, but it was published before the F minor one and thus it is usually listed as "M. le Kekkiener," and therein lies a story. Chopin had not met the Frederic Kalbrenner (1784-1849), one of the great piano virtuosos and teachers of the day, when he composed the concerto in 1830. But Kalbrenner's name was well known to Chopin, who had heard him play in Paris in 1829. Kalbrenner was one of the most sought-after teachers in Paris, and Chopin was tempted to study with him. However, Chopin's father advised against it, and Chopin ultimately decided to continue his studies independently.

The two concertos were composed within a year of each other, but the E minor concerto was published first. This has led to some confusion in the numbering. The F minor concerto was actually composed first (in 1829), but the E minor concerto (composed in 1830) was published first (in 1833). Hence the numbering.

Anyway, when Chopin finally came to Paris, which was Kalbrenner's home at the time, one of the very first things he did was look up that great man, with the idea of taking lessons from him. Chopin had a decided artistic crush on the man; he wrote to a friend: "You would not believe how curious I was about Herz, Liszt, Hiller, etc." — "Herz, Liszt, Hiller, etc." — "all of these men are great, but none of them has the simplicity, the naturalness, the truth of Kalbrenner." And so on, page after page, letter after letter. Chopin played for Hiller, and Kalbrenner, from the depths of his wisdom, told Chopin that he lacked the most essential quality of a great artist: technique. He said that Chopin needed to study with him for three years to acquire the necessary technique. Fortunately, Chopin had the good sense to refuse. He knew that he had something that Kalbrenner could never teach him: originality, creativity, and a unique musical voice.

Chopin had some interesting words to say about his E minor concerto. In a letter to Titus he described the slow movement as "not meant to be loud; it's more of a romance, quiet, melancholy; it should give the impression of glancing tenderly at a place which brings to mind a thousand dear memories. It is a sort of meditation in beautiful spring weather, but by moonlight. That is why I have muted the accompaniment..." Apparently Chopin composed this movement with his first love, Konstancja Gładkowska, in mind.

The concerto was first performed in Warsaw on October 11, 1829, with Chopin as soloist. It was an immediate success and established him as one of the leading pianists and composers of his generation. The work has remained popular ever since, and is one of the most frequently performed piano concertos in the repertoire.

### Sonata No. 3 in B Minor, Op. 58

Chopin composed three sonatas: the first, in C minor, is a student work that is never played. The second, in B-flat minor, is perhaps the most popular, thanks to the third movement "Funeral March." The last one, in B minor, Op. 58, is his most ambitious effort at sonata form. Composed in 1844, it is a late-Chopin work that breathes the mysterious beauty typical of such mature pieces as it is a Piano Fantasy and the Ballade in the same key. Scholars have made the usual complaint about the first movement (Allegro maestoso) that it is academically lacking in a sense of development. But it is interesting to note that audiences have never complained. This movement is nearly all free, from the lunging, dramatic figure that opens it to the pair of fortissimo chords at the end.

The second subject, one of those long-phrased plastic melodies so peculiar to Chopin, offers a momentary relief, which the composer has never overridden. This movement is nearly all free, from the lunging, dramatic figure that opens it to the pair of fortissimo chords at the end.

The second movement, a scherzo, is a fleet-will-o'-the-wisp. Extremely difficult to play, it demands from the pianist artists of steel and a touch of velvet. Some interesting things are contained in the slow movement (Largo). It is a nocturne, very much in the manner of the field equivalent in mood to the "Funeral March" of the B-flat minor sonata. In the B section Chopin writes the cantabile (singing voice) under an arpeggiated figuration. Section here is an intimate Duologue. Quite different is the finale, a rondo with a curiously somber bravura. Chopin ever composed. Thundering E-sharp octaves set the pace at the opening, they lead directly into an astonishing bravura section. The finale is a tremendous tour de force, with runs, passages, leaps, and trills. Chopin does not even attempt anything like a formal development of these elements. He is content to repeat them virtually in toto three times, with but slight deviations. A glittering coda rounds out a work that is one of the major triumphs of piano literature.

This sonata, on the whole, is not for most pianists. It is too big; it takes a tremendous degree of art to integrate its architectural contours. To be sure, the notes themselves can readily be mastered, but Chopin's pianism involves far more than this; it is difficult to convey the full range of color, style, and nuance that robs a handful of artists can cope with.

### Berceuse in D-flat Major, Op. 57

Although most of Chopin's works were written in series (ballades, etudes, mazurkas, nocturnes, scherzi, and so on), the list includes several single examples of specialized forms. Some of these, like the Barcarolle, probably exhausted the composer's interest in that form. The Berceuse was a limited expressive medium for his own personality. But the Berceuse in D-flat Major, Op. 57, however, he must have felt that this sole essay completely exhausted the potentialities of the form itself. It is a limited one, of course, with its tradition-dictated "rocking" accompaniment and "lulling" melody, but Chopin transfigured it here in a matchless miniature of atmospheric poetry and kaleidoscopic interplay of chromatic colors — one of the supreme miracles of piano tone-weaving.

### Impromptu No. 2 in F-sharp Major, Op. 36

Although a quasi-improvisation is to be expected from this title, the second Impromptu in F-sharp Major, Op. 36, doesn't live up to its name as well as its companions, and especially the fourth piece in that series, better known as the Fantaisie-Impromptu. The present work is something of an enigma for Chopin analysts, for it begins like a nocturne (with a "carillon-bass" that recalls the flurrier the "balm" buried tones of Hauptmann's Sardinian Bell"), but turns into a ballade ending with "brutally" fortissimo octaves, which the same writer suggests are intended as a deliberately harsh "awakening from dreamland." Perhaps the best explanation of its contradictions is that it was a kind of experiment for its composer, working a transition from the straightforward appeal of salon-type pieces to the more complex tone-dramas of the ballades, scherzi, and the great Fantaisie in F Minor.

**Commissioers of piano playing Guiomar Novaes to such great practitioners as Cortot and Gieseking.** Like Hofmann towards the end of her career, Miss Novaes has a rather small repertoire; and, like Hofmann, what she plays she plays perfectly, with much the same kind of aristocratic approach and singing tone. Several things make her playing unique among today's pianists. Her tone, many believe, is in color and subtlety recalls the magic-note singing of the great romantic pianists of two generations ago (the generation, of course, in which Miss Novaes was trained); her technique seems always equal to every demand. Her extension of her fingers and she could do miraculous things with no apparent effort. She never strove for effect and she was no pounder; at all times her playing was intensely feminine, with its conciseness backed up by the most brilliant kind of technique. It is probable that her playing was never cut and dried. Seldom did she play the same piece of music the same way. Each performance offered a different point of view, and each time one approach seemed perfectly natural and inevitable.

Apparently her playing was perfectly formed at a very early age. When she was fourteen years old, and already famous, Les Adieux sonata was played for Philipp by another Conservatoire. "No, no," said Philipp. "The second movement is much too fast. Play it again." Novaes thought it over for a moment and then played it again — with somewhat different tempi but with exactly the same tempos. "Now it is right," said Philipp. Novaes never left his class. "He was a good teacher," she once remarked. Philipp considered her by far the best pupil of the hundreds he had turned to the concert stage. She did not change much musically after her Conservatoire days. Her tone, her repertoire, her whole approach, remained essentially the same. She came to this place of music through the years — all these were part of her make-up from the beginning.

Born in 1895, she was the seventeenth of nineteen children. At the age of four she was playing little minuets to visiting royalty in the palace at Rio. At eight she was sent to São Paulo. By the time she was seven she was studying with Luigi Chiaffarelli, an Italian who had been a pupil of Busoni. She heard her first concert at the age of eight. It was a joint recital by Pablo Casals and Harold Bauer. "I was so impressed I never forgot anything about that concert," she said.

As a child, Novaes also studied harmony, composition, and the organ, which she played at Sunday morning Mass. Her debut took place when she was eleven, called upon at short notice to substitute for an indisposed pianist. Dressed in a pretty frock with a huge bow in the back, she went on stage and played Gottschalk's *Brazilian National Anthem*, a ferociously difficult piece of neo-Lisztian writing.

Soon all São Paulo was watching the progress of its *Wunderkind*. Word of her gift to officials in the Brazilian government, who saw to it that she received a grant for four years of study in Europe. Philipp immediately sent her on her way to the Conservatoire. The examinations for two foreign students were open at the Conservatoire. She took the exam for the foreigners, the last to register, and found 187 prior applicants for the two openings. She was given the number 188, played for the examiners, and was accepted. Her program included Liszt's *Paganini Etude in E*, Chopin's A-flat Ballade, and Schumann's *Carnaval*. She took both jury, and the jury even asked her to repeat the Ballade.

During all this time, in her second year at the Conservatoire, she had a concert venture, having played with the Chatelet Orchestra under Gabriel Pierné (this was her official debut). Then Sir Henry Wood in England, and on tour in Italy, Switzerland, and Germany. She also met the great pianist Ignaz Paderewski, who was personally so impressed that he asked her to play for him. "He was a big pianist," said Novaes, "and Pugno."

She was offered many concert tours, but her parents decided to return to Brazil. She had no intention of coming to the United States but was talked into doing so by José Carlos Rodriguez, who was leaving for Washington at the request of President Wilson to serve as a neutral arbitrator. She spent the entire war in the United States. In 1922 she made her New York debut with Rodolphe. She made her first New York appearance at Aeolian Hall. From then on, her life was that of a touring pianist.

With the coming of long-playing records, she became a busy recording artist, putting virtually her entire active repertoire on records for Vox. At most she worked four or five hours daily when preparing a concert program. She thought that concentration was much more important than mechanical repetition. "You know that piece you asked me about? I practiced it for four hours. Much also can be done away from the piano. I studied the Beethoven G Major Concerto that way." It seems that Philipp gave her two weeks to prepare the concerto. She took it with her to Milly. She read it on the train, and kept reading it. At the first rehearsal, she said, she knew it perfectly.

**Charles Stansley**
