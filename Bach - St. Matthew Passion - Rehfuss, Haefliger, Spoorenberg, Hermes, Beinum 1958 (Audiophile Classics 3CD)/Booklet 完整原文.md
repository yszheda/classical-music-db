**The last of the great romantic conductors**

"Romanticism," was the word connecting both Mengelberg and Van Beinum. It all started with the tremendously popular Mengelberg being greatly influenced in his musical tastes by Mahler whom, as early as 1903, he stoutly promoted. Effectively, Mengelberg started the Mahler cult, which appeared in Holland, long before it started elsewhere in the world. This led to Mahler personally visiting The Netherlands, where he conducted his symphonies, and corrected some of his scores. Mengelberg always prepared the orchestras for Mahler, who eventually wrote to having found 'a second fatherland' in the Netherlands, where his works were performed as nowhere else 'thanks to Mengelberg.'

When listening to the Mengelberg trained Van Beinum conducting the recently rediscovered radio broadcast of Mahler VII, one instantly realizes that here we have a direct descendant of the great romantic tradition set by the likes of Wagner, Hans Richter, Hans von Bülow, and Arthur Nikisch – a tradition that subsequently passed from Mahler through Mengelberg to Van Beinum. The latter might truly be called, "the last of the line."

**The St. Matthew Passion**

Most peculiarly, we have today the St. Matthew Passion preserved in a richly sounding Mengelberg radio recording from April 1939, while virtually nothing of Bach remained of the Van Beinum years, except for a torso of his final, 1958 broadcast recording. Only recently, the complete broadcast, as published on these cd's, was rediscovered. This Van Beinum recording is effectively the first one in the new tradition of performing the integral Amsterdam work, rather than performing the cut Mendelssohn-Mengelberg version, that linked the work more closely to the popular oratorios of Handel.

It was precisely this cut version of Bach's Passion, that brought about heated discussions among critics and audiences, promise once made to his mother as a teenager, when he first appeared before the Concertgebouw Orchestra a guest conductor.

The latter started the tradition on April 8, 1899, based on the score that Mendelssohn-Bartholdy had rediscovered about half a century before.

Mengelberg personally supervised many productions until 1945, when he was replaced by Van Beinum, after a committee found Mengelberg's behaviour during the war lacking in moral conduct. Mengelberg was expelled, and died shortly before his 6 years in exile would have been over.

When, in 1947, Van Beinum finally stepped in, audiences were treated to an integral version of Bach's most famous Passion. Whereas the resemblances between van Beinum and Mengelberg have already been pointed out, there were also vast differences. Whereas Mengelberg was a near despotic ruler, Van Beinum proved a more democratic one; he wanted to bond with the orchestra rather than to dominate it. His musicians were given far greater freedom than they had experienced previously, even with Van Beinum, who had of course already more than a decade of joint leadership over the orchestra behind him. However, with Mengelberg being the elder and already legendary principal conductor, Mengelberg was in reality a sort of primus inter pares.

**On Van Beinum**

Perhaps one should also point out the differences in background between the two conductors, with Mengelberg having been a man who had a steep climb to fame, whereas Van Beinum had a far more provincial background. Van Beinum was born on September 3, 1900, in Arnhem, in the east of The Netherlands. He was born into a family of musicians, with his father being an accomplished double-bass player. He received his first lesson in violin from his brother Co, but graduated from the Amsterdam Conservatory with C. F. Evers as his principal instrument. His talent was remarkable enough to land him a position as string player with a local orchestra. By the early 1920's he had taken conducting lessons with Cornelis Donselaar, from where he progressed in 1927 to becoming the principal conductor in Haarlem. There he learned his craft, mastered the repertoire, while also taking a keen interest in (now forgotten) contemporary Dutch music. By June 30, 1929 he fulfilled the promise once made to his mother as a teenager, when he first appeared before the Concertgebouw Orchestra a guest conductor.

In 1931, he was appointed as second conductor of the Concertgebouw, next to Mengelberg, progressing to joint principal conductor by 1938. Of course, Mengelberg's strong points were not lost on Van Beinum, but whereas Mengelberg was largely oriented on German romantic repertoire, Van Beinum also promoted modern music, along with French impressionism, while proving a stout promoter of the works of Bruckner.

**Cleaned up like a painting**

As mentioned before, the first thing that strikes us today is the romantic mould in which Van Beinum crafts this mythical performance of the St. Matthew Passion, but when he first stepped in, on March 31, 1947, his conducting was rather seen as a move toward modernism and a break with tradition. Not only because of the choice to finally present the work as a whole, but also because his style was seen as more intimate, sober and true to the atmosphere of Bach. Critic Leo Hanekeet in *De Time* of 1947: "Sobriety, transparency, insight and a sensible restriction with respect to external expression marked Van Beinum's approach. He declined the individualistic features that put soloists in the foreground, and the window it opened to eternity. Moreover he respected Bach's orchestra writing and maintained the double-choir division of the chorus." Critic Gaffron wrote in *The Truth* of 1947: "Cleaned up like Rembrandt's 'Nightwatch', Bach finally appeared in Amsterdam, for those who hadn't seen him in Naarden." Inevitably some similarities with Mengelberg were also noted, although Van Beinum cleaned up the score and shunned using the crescendo, decrescendo, or ritardando of the romanticists. He also followed his predecessor in reading the score as a marvellous piece of music, rather than as a liturgy that served a religious function.

**A romantic passion**

For critics and audience alike, if raised in the romantic Mengelberg era, Van Beinum's groundbreaking approach demanded repeated hearings in order to appreciate his more clear-cut rendition, in which all chorals were executed at the same speed, without expressive liberties and exaggerated climaxes. And yet, when listening to this recording, hitherto only available in fragmentary form, it is clear that Van Beinum's performance was not even remotely what we consider a historical performance today. As TIOTH Publishers' 1999 publication *The St. Matthew Passion 100 years at the Concertgebouw* states, neither period instruments as viola da gamba, oboi d'amore and oboi da caccia could change the fact that both orchestra and chorus, were drilled by his predecessor. Thus Van Beinum's rendition is primarily a transitory one. While restoring many things that belonged to Bach's score, he maintained the warmly romantic approach of the pre-war period, although he shunned its most exaggerated liberties. If anything, Van Beinum's rediscovered 1958 performance shows his own very particular human rendition, and if was considered 'ascetic' then, it sounds wholly "romantic" today.

**The shadow of Mengelberg**

Some scholars suggest that Van Beinum was limited in his desire to clean up the Passion picture in Amsterdam by ghosts from the past. Not only did the audience cherish the memory of Mengelberg's conducting, but when his long lost April 1939 St. Matthew Passion broadcast made it on to long playing records in 1963, it set an undeniable standard. Pre war legends such as Willem Ravelli, Karl Erb, Jo Vincent and Laurens Bogtmans were still stars to be reckoned with after the war, and they were used to standing out in grand old fashion. Regardless, Van Beinum gradually won over the audience and, as of 1954, even his stoutest critics admitted that his approach had a place of its own, between the romantic one of Mengelberg and the historical one that was practiced in Naarden.

**The lost 1958 broadcast**

The culmination point of Van Beinum's approach was reached in April 1958, precisely in our historical radio recording, with Ernst Haefliger, Heinrich Rehfuss, Erna Spoorenberg, Annie Hermes en Simon van der Geest. The year thereafter, Van Beinum was already severely ill and unable to perform. He died on April 13, 1959. The 1958 broadcast, however, was a unique attempt to blend the Mengelberg approach with the more authentic one practiced in Naarden since 1931, under Anton van der Horst. Typically, with Spoorenberg and Hermes, Van Beinum did not only employ two of the attractions of the famous Naarden recordings of 1937, but also the two main staples of the tradition there. Therefore one might say, that in the 1958 performance, he finally succeeded in creating a Passion that was freer of the past and looked forward toward the future. Tellingly in this respect, is the fact that even by 1965, Van Beinum's successor Eugen Jochum would still work with the larger part of the 1958 cast: Haefliger, Rehfuss, and Spoorenberg. This says as much about their qualities as it says about their importance for the famous Dutch cast surrounding the St. Matthew Passion.

## 第 13 页 (scans/HIPPO_20221202_0012.jpg)

**The vocalists**

**Erna Spoorenberg** (11 April 1925-18 March 2004) was born in Yogyakarta, Java, then a Dutch colony, now part of Indonesia. As a child, she studied the piano with Julius Röntgen and, from the age of 14 onwards sang with, among others, the celebrated Dutch early 20th century soprano Aaltje Noordewier. At the outset of her career Spoorenberg was renowned for her exceptional range and extension, which was immediately clear from her 1947 Dutch Radio debut with Mozart's "Exsultate Jubilate." In 1949, Karl Böhm had better ears than those responsible for casting at the Dutch Opera, and she landed a guest contract with the Vienna State Opera, where she sang Norina, Launa (Der Rosenkavalier), Pamina and Queen of Night, Konstanze, Susanna, Eva, Fiordiligi, Frau Fluth, Adele, and Gilda, to mention but a few. Her spectacular success in Vienna eventually brought her to the attention of the Dutch Opera and inevitably she was made an offer. She made her debut there on September 15, 1955, as Violetta in La Traviata. She was not, however, given a solid position and continued her career abroad.

Legendary now are her performances in the Kirov and Bolshoi theatres in 1963 and 1965. In fact, a look at her impressive chronology makes the complaints about her burdened relationship with the Dutch Opera hard to understand. Today, a singer like Spoorenberg would laugh aloud about the home market, whilst flying from Moscow, to New York, to Vienna, or wherever else the best assignments were being offered. However, immediately after the war, being abroad was not what one just practiced. Travelling meant long absences from home and family, and a secure position in what was then an ensemble theatre, meant financial security. Perhaps it was for the best, since it presumably spurred her concert career, which culminated in her performances of the soprano part in Bach's St. Matthew Passion, after she appeared in the pioneering 1937 studio recording of the work, with the Dutch Bach Foundation conducted by Anton van der Horst. This recording confirmed her growing reputation, and spurred her broadcast performance of the role under Van Beinum in our publication here. Clarity and purity of tone, a secure top and a solid middle were the technical foundations of the voice upon which she built her very human interpretation. On April 16, 1970, she made her final appearance with the Dutch opera, after which a car accident curtailed her career. She then started a second career as a celebrated vocal coach.

## 第 14 页 (scans/HIPPO_20221202_0013.jpg)

**Ernst Haefliger** (8 July 1919 – 17 March 2007) was born in Davos, Switzerland, as a trusted singing with such renowned tenors as Fernando Carpi and Julius Patzak. He was famous as the Evangelist in Bach's St. Matthew Passion since his debut in the role in Zurich, as early as 1942. It was this performance that sparked his career and landed him several offers, first from the Düsseldorf Opera, where he sang from 1943 to 1952. When conductor Ferenc Fricsay heard him sing, he helped Haefliger to a dream debut at the Salzburg Festival, where he became a celebrity after creating Trissino in the world premiere of Ildebrando Pizzetti's Antigone. By 1952 Haefliger followed Fricsay to the Deutsche Oper Berlin, where, between 1952 and 1974, he sang all leading tenor parts in Mozart's operas, along with the lyrical tenor parts in Rossini's Barber of Seville and Le Comte Ory, and Smetana's Bartered Bride, to mention but a few. His hallmark role in opera might have been Tamino in Die Zauberflöte which he sang in his ten debut's in Glyndebourne in 1956, and the Lyric Opera of Chicago in 1966. Yet, his reputation today rests mainly on his supreme interpretation of German Lieder and oratorios. Haefliger's performances of the Evangelist in the St. Matthew Passion have been quoted as having set a worldwide standard, and he even toured the United States with a program that existed only of Bach's St. John and St. Matthew Passion, as a vocalist with a crystalline lyric tenor. He was a stylist by nature. In Amsterdam, this quality made him a celebrated Evangelist, not only in this performance under Van Beinum, but also in later years when Eugen Jochum took over.

**Bass-baritone Heinz Julius Rehfuss** (25 May 1917 – 27 June 1988) shared with Ernst Haefliger his Swiss identity, and a good deal of his musical background, since Rehfuss was also born into a musical family. His mother was the also Florentine Rehfuss-Peichert, his father was the baritone Carl Rehfuss. The latter was entirely responsible for his son's training. Rehfuss enjoyed a prestigious international career, after his debuts as a chorist, soloist, and stage hand for the 1938/39 season in Biel-Soleuthurn. From 1940 to 1952 he appeared in a good 80 roles at the Zurich Opera House, after which his international career took off. La Scala, The Paris Opera, Liceu Barcelona, and many other houses were graced with his mellifluous, slender bass-baritone sound. These qualities made him particularly suited to the stylistic and expressive demands of the Baroque genre, most notably in Bach's cantatas and the St. Matthew Passion. Yet, his was not a clean-cut timbre as was Haefliger's angelic tenor sound. Rehfuss had rather some characteristics of the old bass-baritone school, with a vibrant and instantly recognizable timbre – all features that can be fully appreciated in this Bach recording. Apart from the standard repertoire, Rehfuss also performed many works by modern composers such as Stravinsky, Milhaud, Britten, and Nono.

## 第 15 页 (scans/HIPPO_20221202_0014.jpg)

On 16 September 1906, **Johanna Sophia (Annie) Hermes** received her vocal training at the Antwerp Conservatory. After her debut in 1931, she limited her appearances mostly to concerts and oratorios, where she easily stood out with her warm coloured, passionate voice. Rather than a relic from the Mengelberg days, she serves to demonstrate Van Beinum's desire to mediate between Mengelberg's school and the standard set by the competition in Naarden, where Hermes had been the alto soloist for many years, before she came to the Concertgebouw on the wings of the previously mentioned 1937 recording under Anthon van der Horst.

**Bass-baritone David Hollestelle** (13 October 1916 – 21 April 2001) was born in Den Helder. He was initially an administrative employee with the Dutch Opera in 1966, as Tamino in a Zauberflöte with Cristina Deutekom. His was a lyrical tenor instrument, which distinguished itself by blending graciousness and liveliness. ## 封底内页 (scans/HIPPO_20221202_0008.jpg)

**Toonkunstkoor Amsterdam**

The legendary Toonkunstkoor Amsterdam (Vocal Art Choir Amsterdam) was erected in 1829 and participated in Mengelberg's very first St. Matthew Passion in 1899. Therefore, when reading about Van Beinum's apparent struggle to eliminate the remnants of the Mengelberg era in terms of interpretation, this choir is perpetually mentioned as the most difficult element in that process. They had been moulded by Mengelberg for 46 years, before Van Beinum stepped in, and were an institute by themselves. However, today the discussion seems a bit superfluous in the light of a performance as heartfelt, and moving as this one.

I would not say that this is "the best," the "definitive", the most "authentic" or whatever else is recorded of Bach's masterpiece, either in terms of the choir, or in terms of soloists and orchestra. Today, it stands out foremost as a thrilling document of a fascinating musical era that has now largely faded into the past. Perhaps, as has been suggested above, it was the last echo of the great 19th century tradition that ended with the likes of Mengelberg, Furtwängler, Knappertsbusch and Van Beinum.

*René Seghers*
