## Liner Notes Page 4-5 (German)

**Lebenserinnerungen und Debüt an der Metropolitan Opera, New York**

Wer über Michael Bohnen schreiben will, weiß kaum, womit er beginnen soll. Mit dem Opernsänger, dem begnadeten Bassbariton, der Oktober bis zum ersten Juli 1924 vier Wochen lang am Kölner Konservatorium studierte? Mit dem Schauspieler des Musterstücken? Mit dem großen Künstler? Mit dem intendanten des Staatstheaters des Musterschülers?

Michael Bohnen wurde am 2. Mai 1887 als jüngster Sohn eines Bäckers in Köln geboren. Der Vater stammte aus Baden-Baden, die Mutter war Französin. Mit 15 Jahren begann er eine Bäckerlehre. Die Lehrstelle war schwer. „Oft bin ich weinend eingeschlafen", bekannte er später. „Aber ich habe mich durchgebissen." Mit 18 Jahren bestand er die Aufnahmeprüfung am Kölner Konservatorium. Ein Jahr später, als Achtzehnjähriger, wechselte er an das Stern'sche Konservatorium nach Berlin. Dort erhielt er Gesangsunterricht bei Professor Richard Schulz-Dornburg. Der große Bassbariton erkannte sofort das Talent. „Sie werden einmal ein großer Sänger", prophezeite er seinem Schüler. Die Prophezeiung sollte sich erfüllen. Bohnen debütierte 1910 am Stadttheater Aachen als Don Fernando in Beethovens »Fidelio«. 1913 wurde er an die Berliner Hofoper engagiert, wo er bis 1923 blieb.

Von der von Kaiser Wilhelm II persönlich, nach einer Parsifal-Vorstellung, verliehenen Ernennung zum Königlichen Kammersänger, da war er erst 25 Jahre jung. Dann nach dem zum Weltkrieg: Die große Bundesverdienstmedaille 1. Klasse von Theodor Heuss, erster Bundespräsident der Nachkriegsdeutschland, verliehen. ## Liner Notes Page 12-13 (English)

**An extraordinary German and his début at the Met**

Anyone trying to write about Michael Bohnen faces problems where to begin. With the opera singer, the gifted bass-baritone whom the profile music commentator Oskar Bie described at the time as the only singing actor on the stage? With the operetta hero, the »Casanova« discovered by Max Reinhardt? With the film producer and sensational actor in silent movies whom the he became the most popular and highly paid actor of his time?

With the ring master who won it first prize in the Berlin Ring competition and then went on to sing Rudolf Caracciola and the famous Meier holding Wotan in particular stand out of Lord Corrith (1858-1925)? With the sculptor, with the photograph? Or perhaps with the talent of recognition he received from all over the German-speaking world for his services to German art? After a performance of Parsifal when he was only twenty-five, Kaiser Wilhelm II personally appeared in his royal Karrem. After the Second World War he received the first Class Order of Merit of the Federal Republic of Theodor Heuss, the first President of post-war Germany. He was awarded the Goethe Prize of Berlin and was made an honorary member of the Deutsche Oper in Berlin. At Christmas 1964 the Metropolitan Opera also granted him honorary membership with pension benefits – an honour accorded no other German singer. Perhaps it is best to outline his life chronologically. Michael Bohnen was born in the heart of Cologne on May 2, 1887, the youngest son of the Bohnen children. His father was a factory foreman and his mother was of French origin. Following his father's express wishes he served an apprenticeship as a smith at a Cologne iron foundry when he developed immense physical strength working at the forge.

By a remarkable coincidence, he passed the entrance examination at the Cologne Conservatory with the aria »Auch ich war ein Jüngling« from Lortzing's Der Waffenschmied. He was almost a kind of blacksmith. A year later, at the age of eighteen, his father allowed him to switch to the private singing school of Professor Richard Schulz-Dornburg, a famous voice trainer in Cologne. His three-octave range was spectacular as was his operatic début in 1910, at the age of 23, he was suddenly called out of a lesson; three hours later he was on stage at the Düsseldorf Opera, standing in for an indisposed bass as Kaspar in Weber's Der Freischütz. That resulted in glowing reviews and from there it was a direct contact, from which he was abruptly freed by Georg von Hüssler-Haneler; the artistic director of the Royal Prussian Theater and secured a brief stint in Wiesbaden. Bohnen was engaged at the Berlin Hofoper in 1913 in the role of a German in Parsifal.

Bohnen's international career began in 1920. He met the Metropolitan Opera impresario Gatti-Casazza whose personal and financial negotiations proved to be complicated, but ultimately he was convinced to sign. His Gatti-Casazza's persuasive tongue won in the end.

## Liner Notes Page 14-15 (English continuation)

What a bevy of wonderful singers Giulio Gatti-Casazza had managed to bring together at the 1922/1923 season at the Met! Among the Italians and baritones alone, at least ten were world-class: the Russian Feodor Chaliapin, the Spanish José Mardones, the Italian Enrico Caruso, Beniamino Gigli, Michael Bohnen and Arigo Pelati, the Americans Clarence Whitehill and Lawrence Tibbett, the Italians Giuseppe Danise, Giuseppe de Luca, Antonio Scotti and Titta Ruffo. Now the German Michael Bohnen joined that illustrious company. However, an initial press statement read: »Michael Bohnen returns to the German Pig.« Anyone acquainted with Bohnen knew there was little chance that he would just swallow the insult. Yet instead of packing his bags and going home as might have been predicted, his reaction was »just wait, I'll show 'em!« And so he did. His performance had audiences raving and reviewers were lavish with their eulogies, praising the exceptional quality of his voice and marvelling at its metallic sheen and its reserve power. The force of his dramatic declamation and declamatory words would serve him well in every role.

Mephisto: Bohnen noticed that his idol and friend Chaliapin had a strangely hoarse voice that was no longer what it had once been in the upper and lower ranges. The great bass was after all over 50 years old and had ruled the stage for many decades. But his acting was still unsurpassed and made up for whatever failings singers might have noticed that day. Michael Bohnen announced his intention to dismiss Chaliapin because, in those days, star performance in only four roles, he was much too expensive. With growing indignation, Bohnen heard him go on to say that Chaliapin was far too old and his voice cracked. Bohnen sprang up and spoke passionately in defense of his voice: »Gentlemen, I will never sing my friend Chaliapin in the back,« he said. »I will sing his Boris or Don Quixotte.« Regrettably Bohnen was true to his word even after Chaliapin's death in April 1938. The Metropolitan did not leave the Met until the end of the 1928/1929 season. Bohnen's diary therefor also numbered although the fifteen-year contract with the Met would not expire before 1937. For various reasons he became a citizen other American contracts and bonds in 1932.

The falsetto of the voice of the great bass singer Mary Lewis, who was hopelessly infatuated with Bohnen, may have been one of the reasons. The strong man had one of the reasons. Being plagued by homesickness. He gave away all he owned in America, his possessions, a house and priceless works of art, for only 65 dollars in ready cash and left for Germany without saying a word to anyone.

Gatti-Casazza had by now left because still in Germany, Bohnen now began adding operetta roles to his opera repertoire. His concerts, which he gave in Germany at the meantime expanded to seventy-eight roles. He was now at the pinnacle of his career who could not subordinate himself or be controlled, but whatever he did, he did properly. He was not a man of half measures. His performances continued to be marked by explosive power. Whenever a storm broke out, he was ready for it. We do not know why he never again entered a recording studio after having made recordings of two arias from Hamlet by Ambroise Thomas in 1924. His versatility did not fail him, as did his voice. Yet he still had the technical knowhow to compensate all the losses of his voice. Like all of the great, he needed the challenge of facing an audience.

Bohnen's earlier friendship with Chaliapin was advantageous to him in May 1945 after the war. The Russian bass had been born in Kazan in 1873. The Soviet government commanded the city and likewise an avid admirer of Chaliapin, begged him to help get Bohnen's musical life going again. As vice-president of the newly formed Chamber of Artists, with the actor Paul Wegener as president, Michael Bohnen was responsible for the reintegration of German artists into working life. The musicians' authorities then appointed him director of the German Opera.
