## Il tenore di stile e di poesia

Il dopoguerra si rivelò nell'Italia tutta da ricostruire un'autentica linea di spartiacque storico e presente non solo nella realtà sociale ma anche in quella del teatro d'opera, che con la nuova generazione di cantanti inaugurò un'infinita schiera inversioni di marcia. Mentre sul versante femminile un fenomeno come la Callas avrebbe spazzato via le diverse categorie vocali del soprano, in quello maschile si vennero a verificare una netta differenziazione tra tenore leggero, lirico, lirico-spinto e drammatico. Una sorta di specializzazione in ogni precipua area del repertorio che precedentemente non era mai stata così tassativa, visto che da Caruso a Gigli i maggiori tenori erano soliti alternare sia Nemorino che Cavaradossi, sia Edgardo che Radames, ma in quegli anni riuscì invano a Borgonzi. Certo, si dirà, non a tutti era concessa questa versatilità, determinata non solo da padronanza tecnica ma soprattutto dalla vocalità a disposizione, che nei vertici casi poté forzare i limiti, finché però ogni regola è regola quasi costante di voci troppo velocemente spinte in un repertorio al di sopra della loro portata.

Ciò garantì d'altro lato l'ideale collocazione di una voce nei giusti ruoli, creando un clima vocale di guardia al professionismo rigoroso più che al serialismo di un debito produttivo, come a dire che per un tenore lirico-leggero era meglio aspirare a divenire un fuoriclasse come Almaviva piuttosto che cimentarsi in un improbabile Otello. Questo il clima generale, senza peraltro far eccezione per qualche incursione giovanile in Tosca, spaziò al massimo più al Werther — e più recentemente l'avrebbero meravigliosamente messo in pratica Kraus e Flórez, mentre ad esempio Tagliavini e tanto più Di Stefano, nell'infanzia di tenore di assoluta classe e all'epoca senza confronti, lo capirono immediatamente tutti i maggiori direttori d'orchestra e i più importanti teatri, tant'è che già dopo le prime affermazioni al nascete Sperimentale di Spoleto in *Don Pasquale* e Werther gli si aprirono le porte dell'Opera di Roma (*Barbiere* e *Matrimonio segreto*) e del San Carlo di Napoli (*L'infanzia di Cristo di Berlioz*), mentre la Scala l'avrebbe dapprima ingaggiato nel 1950 per una tournée a Londra con il *Falstaff* (poi voluto anche al Festival di Edimburgo) e solo dopo il Piermarini per un *Elisir* accolto da vivissimo successo. Il felice connubio con il teatro milanese sarebbe durato poco ancora pochi anni (tra le ultime apparizioni importanti vanno ricordate nel 1951 la *Cenerentola* e la *Sonnambula*, in uno spettacolo terzetto Callas-Bernstein-Visconti e il *Turco in Italia*, sempre con la Callas), perché nel frattempo l'attività di Valletti si era progressivamente proiettata Oltreoceano. Prima nei teatri dell'America Latina, a partire dal 1951 con *Città del Messico l'Elisir* e di seguito con *Traviata* ancora con la Callas), Rio de Janeiro e San Paulo del Brasile, quindi negli Stati Uniti con il debutto nel '53 a San Francisco in *Werther* e a New York del *Don Giovanni*. Al Met la sua presenza sarebbe stata saltuaria, per quanto sempre stupenda; il suo ruolo ideale di battaglia avrebbe affiancato Ferrando nei *Così fan tutte*, Tamino nel *Flauto magico* e Alfred in *Die Fledermaus*. Per quanto poi sporadiche, le sue apparizioni europee non avrebbero mancato di prestigio, come nel caso del memorabile *I due Foscari* alla Scala con Karajan nel 1960, che rappresentò anche il suo esordio al Festival di Salisburgo.

La virtù stautunense non fu del tutto immotivata, come nel caso di altre voci italiane (p.es. la Tebaldi), che sopportavano male il clima americano. Valletti ebbe pubblico più affezionato e fedele, meno critico e soprattutto culturalmente più aperto. Lo dimostra il fatto che mentre nei recital italiani — caso emblematico i celebri *Martini e Rossi radiofonici* — la romanza operistica era tassativa, nelle sale da concerto americane uno stilista come Valletti, che padroneggiava più di una lingua e vantava un interesse rarissimo per il repertorio liederistico, poteva permettersi l'abbandono edulcorato e lacrimoso di un tempo ormai datato, come nel "Sogno" della *Manon* preferisce esaltare la parola e i colori rispettati ai languori sospiristi cari a un'altra epoca. Regge l'ostica tessitura di "Mi par d'udire ancor" aiutandosi giustamente con un uso di mezzi toni, perfettamente stile e lontani da qualsivoglia compiacimento edonistico, mentre lascia l'esecuzione di riferimento in una pagina legata ad un'opera che mai avrebbe affrontato a scena, *Luisa Miller*. Il suo "Quando le sere al placido", per l'abbandono sentimentale, le toccanti mezzevoci, il legato superlativo, oltre al fatto di essere cantato dal vivo, dà punti alla famosa incisione di Tagliavini e addirittura – per un critico esigente come Celletti – mette a dura prova anche le interpretazioni leggendarie di Giuseppe Anselmi e Aureliano Pertile.

Nella splendida "O fiamma soave" della *Donna del lago* – recupero rossiniano non effettuato nel 1958 da un Serafin dalle forbici e dallo stile pesanti, quindi neppure pianistico per poter essere preso in considerazione dal gusto filologico moderno – Valletti riesce comunque a imporre la linea elegante del cantare, la fusione fonetica, anche se sotto l'aspetto del virtuosismo e dell'estensione fa quello che può, in ogni caso molto per l'epoca. Ma è in particolare nell'aria dai *Quattro rusteghi* – appartenente insieme a quella del *Falstaff* diretto da De Sabata alle esecuzioni scaligere – a emergere la poetica delle piccole cose che fu di questa pagina un gioiello e di Valletti un artista ineffabile, in cui l'attenzione al senso non perde mai di vista l'intimo valore della parola, come il suo raffinato gusto di musicista trovò sempre il suo punto di contatto con le ragioni del personaggio e del cuore, nella quale egli è da tempo immerso.

**Davide Annachini**

## A Tenor With Style And Poetry

Italy's emergence from the wreckage of World War II marked a clear dividing line between past and present, not only in society as a whole but also in the world of opera, where a new generation of cantanti inaugurated a new direction. The Callas phenomenon swept away the differences between one sort of soprano and another, yet tenors became clearly divided into light, lirico-spinto and dramatic. Specialization to this degree had never been so marked: the major tenors from Caruso to Gigli would happily alternate between Nemorino and Cavaradossi, or Edgardo and Radames but only Bergonzi managed this sort of versatility in the post-war years. Of course, not everyone could be so versatile since it was not simply a matter of technique but of the voice itself. In that era, no one attempted to make singers do what nature told them they could not, although unfortunately nowadays singers are pushed too quickly into a repertoire beyond the capabilities of their own cards. These divisions were used to ensure that a voice would be cast in the right role, which led to a perfectly decent appreciation for the sensationalism of a do-or-die debut, meaning that a light tenor should aspire to be an outstanding Almaviva rather than tackle the unlikely role of Manrico. Schipa realized this perfectly and others with a few youthful stabs at Tosca, but its outer limits as Werther and more recently so did Kraus and Florez to splendid effect. By contrast, Tagliavini and even more Di Stefano were so anxious to make their mark in the lirico-spinto stakes that they would soon ruin the superb qualities with which Mother Nature had endowed them. After World War II, throughout the 1950s and for much of the 1960s, the benchmark tenor for this repertoire was undoubtedly Cesare Valletti (Rome, 18/12/1922 – Genoa, 13/5/2000). Beyond all anything but limiting his repertoire and enabling him to be an all-round artist rather than more specialist in a particular genre.

Valletti's mother had qualified at the Santa Cecilia college of music and his father was a doctor who had moved with the family to Africa due to work and brought them back to Italy just before the outbreak of World War II. Valletti had taken singing lessons not from Schipa (as is sometimes supposed) but from Bucci Brunacci after the name of Bacci Brunacci. After a short time in the military, he made his first appearance at the Piccini Opera House in Bari as Alfredo in *La Traviata*, a key role in his future career. Valletti would soon assemble all the characters that featured in his repertoire: Almaviva, Lindoro, Don Ottavio, Elvino, Nemorino, Ernesto, Edgardo, Fenton, Pira, Federigo, L'Arlesiana, Des Grieux and Werther. Nonetheless, he also appeared in a wide range of other operas because his impeccable style, whatever the composer, made him near-indispensable. Valletti could handle the *Cenerentola*, and not just the role of Don Ramiro, but also Clorinda with the moon *Il crudo* (Milan, Rai, 1956) – or the niche-Rossini of *Signor Bruschino*, *Conventio* and *La Gazza ladra*, the Rossini of *Il Turco in Italia* (Rome, Teatro dell'Opera, 1951 and 1956 and Gavazzeni) or of *La Donna del lago* (Florence, 1958). He appeared in Wolf-Ferrari's *I Quattro rusteghi* and *Giacomo*, Mozart's less familiar *Bastiano e Bastiana*, *L'Impresario*, *La Betulia liberata* and also *Idomeneo* (Idamante at the Maggio Musicale Fiorentino in 1962) and Monteverdi's *Il Ritorno di Ulisse in patria*, the Combattimento di Tancredi e Clorinda and also *Incoronazione di Poppea* which, as Nerone, he made his final appearance in 1967 at the Caramoor Festival in the USA.

Valletti's versatility was such that he faced a number of great vocal abilities because although he sang a wide variety of characters, they all fell within the light tenore range. What made him one of the most perfect and individual singers to emerge at the time was his sobriety elegant, highly musical approach to performance and the attention to colour and accent of his noble delivery, utterly unlike the mannerisms of the airy-fairy little tenors in vogue before the war. And while audiences acclaimed Valletti's early sitting as the sort of revelation that could only come from a great singer, he remained, Valletti always sounded as though his voice were printed on some stamp on what he did. Which is why he indeed might have had something of Schipa's intimacy and melancholy but none of that singer's ecstatic languor which was a little too much for a decidedly modern era. He eschewed the sort of euphoria of the economic boom. And also why Valletti opened the way for Kraus, with his aristocratic and poetically romantic approach to interpretation, avoiding the tear-jerking sentimentality which both Gigli and Tagliavini piled on throughout that repertoire. Some critics at the time, like Rodolfo Celletti, claimed Valletti did not have Schipa's more compact timbre, especially in the treble, but his measured singing and unfussy approach to expression make him one of the great tenors who, in his day, was unsurpassed. All the major Italian conductors were anxious to work with him immediately following his early successes at the then-new Sperimentale di Spoleto in *Don Pasquale* and *Werther*, Valletti appeared at the Rome Opera (*Barbiere* and *Matrimonio segreto*), at San Carlo in Naples (*L'Enfance du Christ* by Berlioz) and La Scala began with his 1950 *Falstaff* in London (with several more performances conducted by De Sabata), and in 1951 at the Piermarini in a hugely successful *Elisir*. Valletti's association with La Scala lasted only a few more years, then his career became more international. His last important appearances included a spectacular combination of Callas, Bernstein and Visconti and *Il Turco in Italia*, again with Callas), because meanwhile he had begun to work more and more overseas. From 1951, he was in Latin America in Mexico City, (*Elisir* then *Traviata* again with Callas), Rio de Janeiro and Sao Paulo, then he made his US debut in 1953 in San Francisco in *Werther* and in New York in *Don Giovanni*. Valletti was a regular at The Met for seven consecutive seasons, adding Ferrando in *Così fan tutte*, Tamino in *The Magic Flute* and Alfred in *Die Fledermaus* to his extensive range of characters. His European appearances were more sporadic, though no less prestigious, such as the memorable *I due Foscari* conducted by Karajan in 1960, which was also Valletti's debut at the Salzburg Festival.

His concentration on the US was not without reason. Many other Italian singers – Tebaldi is a case in point – especially at the Met found an audience that was warmer, more sympathetic, less physically and vocally critical than in Italy. This was certainly the time – the famous *Martini e Rossi* radio programmes are emblematic in this respect – had to feature a *romanza* from an opera, while US venues were happy to let Valletti, who had a knowledge of more than one language and which, in reality mere interest in *lieder* repertoire, show the other side of the coin, which in any case, was as important a opera singing. No other Italian tenor of the era could have brought that off and probably no Italian tenor of today could either, which is why he places by Schumann are so valuable. Valletti's singing in these pieces is crucial to clear and his subtle accents and colouring prove what a fine chamber performer he was. Recorded in 1960, his version of Op. 48 *Dichterliebe* was followed by a record (now out of print) of Lieder, demonstrating his rare artistry and putting him on a par with the greatest international interpreters, beyond the confines of Italian provincialism.

In his 78 rpm recordings and his two subsequent microgrooves on Cetra in the early 1950s we have several gems, some of which are truly rare in a fine, ecstastic tone, variety of colours and highly elegant, controlled line are almost beyond comparison. For instance, the charming *serenata* from *Barber* replace style arguments with an inspiritedly direct approach and legato coloratura. In keeping with the performance practice of the time, some agility-testing passages are cut, but what Valletti was in the first to make a difference of that lightweight walk of a rondò "Cessò di più resistere" and to emerge unscathed when anyone else would have suffered permanent harm. Still, as far as virtuoso performances go, since the era of John McCormack no Mozart tenor had been capable of the impeccable vocal line needed for "Il mio tesoro" all in one breath. Yet Valletti not only handled it with perfect aplomb and no trace of exhibitionism but even allowed himself the luxury of a fermata at the end, which paradoxically added drama to the singing of this class. However, what truly touches the listener is the arching phrases in "Bella fiamma innamorata" in *Lucia*, the ineffable nuances of "O nature" in *Werther*, the accent on analysis and passion in the aria from *Manon*, the fresh approach to description in the piece as phrasing in Schipa's "Printemps" and a "Furtiva lagrima" with continuing highly poetic color passages and a vibrant duet with Simionato from the finale of *La Favorita*, that Valletti tops off with a C that rings out effortlessly. So much for any reservations about a piece that was once a little too much. With the vastness of the Arena in Verona, as in the 1956 staging of *Barber*. The young Valletti offers further proof of his ability in the treble range in the numbers from *Rigoletto* recorded for an American TV programme. His nonchalant yet crisply delivered "La donna è mobile" is perfect as a showcase for Valletti's formidable vocal technique, favoured by the tenors of yesteryear as though it were no more difficult than humming some little tune. Which contrasts nicely with those popularizers who bludgeon the piece into submission. And Valletti's attention to detail and atmosphere in the aria are most in evidence in his 1951-1961 *Martini e Rossi* performances of some of the most characteristic numbers in his repertoire in which he takes on those legendary benchmarks from the previous generation, Gigli, Schipa and Tagliavini, putting his own stamp on things. Thus in "Lamento di Federico" his approach is passionately romantic, stressing obsession and desperation and avoids any outdated sugary tear-jerking abandon. In "Sogno" from *Manon* he focuses on clarity and detail in words and shading rather than the swooning and sighing in favour in earlier times. He handles the demanding tessitura of "Mi par d'udire ancor" and quite rightly gives himself a little help with several mixed sounds perfectly in keeping with the piece and far removed from any sort of hedonistic complacency. Valletti's greatest moment comes with a piece from an opera which he never actually performed on stage, *Luisa Miller*. His "Quando le sere al placido", with its emotional abandon, its touching *mezzevoci* and its superlative legato, plus the fact that it is sung live, more than holds its own against the famous recording by Tagliavini. Indeed, a demanding critic like Celletti claimed it made life difficult for the legendary performances by Giuseppe Anselmi and Aureliano Pertile.

The splendid "O fiamma soave" from Rossini's *Donna del lago* made in 1958 by a Serafin as heavy-handed with the cuts as with his baton, hence wholly unsuitable for today's "original instruments" tastes, Valletti manages to make the piece his own with his elegant line and ecstatic expression. As far as virtuosity and extension go, the singer does what he can which, for the era, is a great deal. And particularly in the aria from *I Quattro rusteghi*, performed, like *Falstaff*, under De Sabata at La Scala, Valletti's attention to the poetry of small things makes the piece a gem. Valletti's care over a vocal line was never at the expense of the meaning of the words, just as he always combined feeling for the music with feeling for the character. And therein lay his artistry.

**Davide Annachini**
