## Understanding Mascagni
### by Remo Mazzetti, Jr.

Fifty years after his death, Pietro Mascagni (b. Dec. 15, 1863; d. Aug. 2, 1945) remains one of the most misunderstood and underrated of all post-Verdian Italian composers. Despite the enormous popularity of his first published opera, *Cavalleria Rusticana* (1890), and occasional revivals of *L'Amico Fritz* (1891) and *Iris* (1898), the rest of his later operas are practically unknown to the majority of opera lovers.

A major stumbling block to a true understanding of Mascagni's significant operatic legacy comes from an unfair comparison of his works to those of his great friend, Giacomo Puccini (1858-1924), arguably the greatest of all post-Verdian Italian opera composers. Fortunately for posterity, both composers have left clear explanations of their respective artistic goals.

Mascagni wrote of his ninth opera, *Amica* (1905), "My artistic ideals are summarized in the concept of absolute alliance of words and music, in such a way that the music becomes the words, becomes the expression...." To this end, Mascagni sought out libretti with a minimum of action, preferring plots that focus on inner conflict, and characters with a strong emotional intensity. He strove to combine the precise musical expression for each word of the text (literally word by word), Mascagni gave life to these otherwise overtly static dramas.

Puccini's aesthetic was almost the opposite. In his own words, "In the theater, words must be the daughter, action the mother. I am a theatrical writer." Thus, Puccini favored libretti with swift, logical action, emphasizing the larger musical phrase rather than the precise expression of the words of the text. Not surprisingly, Puccini's operas move at a much quicker pace than those of Mascagni. Mascagni slows his works down deliberately, utilizing a great deal of "slow-moving" music. This crucial stylistic dichotomy is apparent in the two composers' different approaches to the vocal line.

Puccini's approach to opera was to bring it as close as possible to the style of the *verismo* (realism) school, which favored direct, emotional expression. Puccini's vocal lines often soar in long, lyrical phrases, while Mascagni's tend to be more declamatory, following the natural rhythms of speech.

The Prelude to *I Rantzau* is given a dynamic reading in which Mascagni deftly relates the tumultuous tempo changes and welds the piece into a powerful tone-poem. (Incidentally, in this performance, Mascagni cuts 23 measures from the opening section.) The composer/conductor brings a restrained intensity to the excerpt from Act III of *Guglielmo Ratcliff*, known as Ratcliff's Dream, which represents an hallucination by the troubled title character. *Guglielmo Ratcliff* was Mascagni's own favorite of his operas. It is interesting to note that this excerpt may well be the most beautiful orchestral music he ever wrote.

With *Iris*, we come to one of Mascagni's three greatest works (alongside *Guglielmo Ratcliff* and *Parisina*). In *Iris*, Mascagni achieved an unprecedented level of subtlety and sophistication in his orchestral and vocal technique; it was also a score on which he lavished great care. The performances of the two passages from Act I which I represent Mascagni the conductor at his very best. In the long introduction, he begins slowly and with great deliberation, phrasing each detail and gradually weaving these details into a larger whole, culminating in the glorious "Hymn of the Sun" (here performed without chorus). The brief *Visione Lirica* (1923), a contemplation of Bellini's *Sonnambula*, is an example of Mascagni's highest and most characteristic style. Those intimately familiar with Mascagni's output may choose a musical quote from the composer's equally contemplative opera *Isabeau* (1911).

The brief choral work *Il Canto del lavoro*, presumably written for Mussolini, brings us into the unhappy chapter in Italian history. Like many Italians (and others around the world), Mascagni admired Mussolini in the early stages of his leadership, but became increasingly disillusioned with him by the late 1930s. Historian John Lukacs explains Mussolini's reputation in that early period of his rule, which had begun in 1922:

> Italy under Mussolini's rule had become a bright, prosperous and powerful country. This impression was shared by many men and women beyond Italy, including Winston Churchill.... There were American members of congress who declared during the depression that what America needed was a Mussolini.

Thus, the *Canto del lavoro*, written in 1928, by no means links Mascagni to Mussolini's infamous fascist regime. Nor should the song itself be stigmatized on the basis of events unforeseen at the time of its composition. In point of fact, Mascagni's 1928 recording of the piece remained in the English record catalogs after 1945, and was even joined by a second recording of the work by the Royal Marine Band! The music itself is robust yet lyric, possibly betraying Mascagni's political ambivalence. This disc presents the orchestral version of the *Canto*, which was intended to serve as an accompaniment for performances of the chorus where the orchestra was not available.

The program ends with a powerful performance of Rossini's *William Tell* overture. Along with his pupil Ponchielli, Rossini remained one of Mascagni's musical "gods"; he paid homage to all three composers in his comic masterpiece *Le Maschere* (1901, rev. 1931).

Perhaps the time has come for a fresh re-evaluation of Pietro Mascagni's substantial operatic legacy, through recordings and concerts (and, one hopes, full staged recordings) of all sixteen of his operas. An authentic performing style could be derived, in part, from a careful study of the small but significant recorded legacy left to us by the composer himself. This CD enshrines an important step towards these goals.
