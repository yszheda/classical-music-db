# The piano between XVIII and XIX century
## by Angelo Valastro

A genius who lived in the shade of fashionable genres, more famous as a virtuous man than as a composer, Johann Sebastian Bach published his first work, the Partita in B flat major BWV 825) only in 1726. Six years later, the complete collection of six Partitas was published with the title "Clavier Übung, consisting of Preludes, Allemandes, Courantes, Sarabands, Gigues, Minuets and other 'Gallantries' composed by J. S. Bach - Cappelmeister of the Sachsen-Weissenfels Court and conductor of the Leipzig Chorus - to delight music connoisseurs. Opus 1. Published by the author in 1731". With the term Clavier Übung - where Übung means "amusement" more than "exercise" - and with the definition Partita at the head of each Suite, Bach ideally joins Kuhnau, his predecessor at the Thomasschule, who, about forty years before, had used these terms in the titles of two of his music collections. As Bach's first biographer - Forkel - points out, the astonishment aroused in the world of music was enormous: nothing so complicated and daring had ever been heard before.

After the French and the English, the Partitas are the third series of Suites composed by the master. The Suite - a very fashionable genre of the time - was originally composed of four dances only (the German Allemande; the French Courante, the Spanish Saraband; the probably Irish Gigue). With Bach it rises to artistic dignity for the first time. Like the French masters, and himself in the English Suites, Bach enriches the original form: each Partita is preceded by an introductory piece - the only one that has not the form of a dance (Prelude, Symphony, Fantasia, Overture, Praeambolum, Toccata) - and a series of "Gallantries" is added to the canonical four dances: minuets, arias, gavottes etc.; they are a sort of "crowning" of the Saraband, being played before the gigue and sometimes (4th and 6th Partitas) also after the Courante.

The harmonic order of the Partitas, B flat major (B, like "Bach!"), C minor, A minor, D major, E minor) goes on following the scale up and down alternatively, with a balance of major and minor" [G. V. Dadelsen].

The fourth Partita opens with a superb Ouverture of "French" style; the Allemande that runs on a harmonic and rhythmic ground that is full of subtle shades and sudden developments, and the Courante or "French" style with its lively dactylic parenthesis, lead to the first "Gallantry", an Aria of folk simplicity, a prelude, by contrast, to the sublime Saraband. This piece "contains important elements of a sort of condensed Sonata: the exposition - with an initial and a final theme -, the richly modulated development and a complete reprise. Despite the small size of each section and the fact that the themes are not clearly separated from one another, the germs of future forms can already be felt clearly" (K. Geiringer). The second "Gallantry", a brief and elegant Minuet, leads to the brilliant conclusive Gigue, a worthy crowning of this wonderful sound structure.

## Page 2 (scans/HIPPO_20230319_0002.jpg)

"His imagination was burning, his feelings even violent: his body was weak and ill. Who can sound the sufferings deriving from this contrast? They must have been terrible, but he never showed them! [...] He poured his soul into his music like the other men pour theires into prayers: with all the effusions of their hearts, inexpressed sadness, ineffable regrets that pious souls express in their dialogues with God. He said in his works what they say on their knees: mysteries of passion and grief that men can understand without words because they cannot be expressed in words. [...] Neither adventures nor complications marked his life, his feelings and impressions were the most important events of his. Far from spiring to the orchestra uproar, he just reproduced his thought on the ivory keyboard and he lost nothing of his energy ..." [F. Liszt]

The lyrical and introspective aspects, as well as the dramatic and almost "visionary" elements of the work of the Polish master, are well-exemplified in the pieces included in this CD.

The three Nocturnes Op. 9 were composed by Chopin in 1830-31 and published two years later in London by Wessel who, with great indignation of the author, entitled the collection *Murmuring of the Seine*. In the third one, in B major, the strongly chromatic parenthesis penetrates into the soul of the listener and, being enriched of new seductive ornaments, it flows into a B minor "agitato" that goes on incessantly on a drawing of dichromatic tercets till it rejoins the initial theme and dissolves in an ethereal cascade of notes.

The two Nocturnes Op. 15 were composed in 1830-31 too, while the third one dates back to 1833. The second one, in F sharp major, is one of the most extraordinary of the collection. The melody winds sweetly in an endless filigree and on a harmonic ground that portends future horizons in the central "double movement".

Both Nocturnes Op. 27 date back to 1834-1835. The second one, in D flat major, was considered by Robert Schumann as an "ideal model" of Nocturne, for its noble linearity, "the most ecstatic music one can imagine".

Both Nocturnes Op. 48, date back to 1841; i.e. to the author's full maturity. They were composed "in one of the first moments of intimate dissent that were the prelude to his breaking off with George Sand" (A. Cortot): Chopin is not satisfied about it; his music - he writes - "his evil" by that time.

The author himself speaks of "written torments" and, as a matter of fact, the C minor Nocturne is one of the most significant examples of a dramatic and visionary Chopin, what Adorno describes as the "luxury of grief". The initial theme - "lento", in countertime -, the "poco più lento" - with its arpeggios -, the tremendous cascades of octaves and the "double mouvement" - where the initial theme re-appears in an almost hallucinated atmosphere that vanishes only at the last bar - create a sense of tragical ineluctability.

## Page 3 (scans/HIPPO_20230319_0003.jpg)

The Nocturne Op. 72 No. 1 was published in 1855, but its composition dates back to 1827; similarly, the Nocturne in C sharp minor - published in 1875 - should date back to 1830. Both of them lead to "that interior psychological world where Chopin's trepid and subjective lyricism is best expressed" (M. Mila).

In the Scherzo in B minor Op. 20 - composed in 1831-32 and published in 1835 by Wessel with another "stupid title", *The Infernal Banquet*, which, once again aroused the author's indignation - both faces of the Polish master are present in a very simple ABA structure: "the melancholically tender atmosphere of the central part (a Polish folk melody that used to be sung at Christmas) contrasts with the flaming impulse of the Scherzo and carries the ineffable sweetness of regret and memory" (A. Cortot).

Chopin's last piece included in this CD, the Studio Op. 25 No. 7 "lento", published in 1837 and generally regarded as a simple "left hand study", actually demands a very sensitive performer.

Cortot rightly observed that "the poetic conception and the actual technical interest of this Study lie in the well-balanced distribution of the sound in both parts and in the exact touch that connects both parts but does not confuse their individual expressive values".

The two Rhapsodies Op. 79, composed in 1879 and published by Simrock one year later were devoted to Elisabet von Herzogemberg: "May I put Your (...) name at the head of those pranks?" the master wrote her in May 1880.

Both pieces emanate a heroic and chivalrous fascination, very close to the ballad spirit.

The G minor Rhapsody Op. 79 No. 2, which concludes this recording, is a perfect example of this: the density of the writing, the constant passion that animates it, together with that "lateness" which is the mark of all Brahms' production, are extremely fascinating even today: the harmonic language is always surprising in its continuity of ideas, in that constant renewal of the invention that never finds a point of rest, and in the profound melancholy that filters through even the most energetic pages, the melancholy that is the other face of the heroism of the mind.

This CD was recorded in 1969, when Alexis Weissenberg was already one of the great names on the international piano scene. Born in Bulgaria in 1929, he began studying music with his father, then entered the State Conservatory of Sofia, and at the age of sixteen he moved from his native country first to Jerusalem and then, a year later, to the United States, to complete his studies at the Juilliard School in New York. He immediately won the first prize at the Leventritt Competition, which marked his debut on the concert stage. Since then his career has never known interruptions: he has played in all the most important concert halls of the world, with the most prestigious conductors and orchestras, he has left an impressive discography, characterized by a large repertoire that goes from the baroque to contemporary music.

## Page 4 (scans/HIPPO_20230319_0004.jpg)

We should not be surprised, therefore, by the extraordinary stylistic coherence of this record: the homogeneity that connects the performances of Bach, Chopin and Brahms, far from being the result of an arbitrary imposing of expressive categories, is the fruit of a deep reflection on the "internal affinities" that cross the music written for the piano between the eighteenth and the nineteenth centuries, affinities that Weissenberg has the merit of bringing out clearly without forcing the natural diversity of the languages.

The articulation is always clear, the phrasing is never heavy, the touch is crystalline and beautiful even in the most forte passages, the left hand is never bulky, the agogic is always well measured: these are the characteristics that make this record, despite the years that have passed, still extremely modern in the best sense of the word, that is, capable of speaking with extraordinary immediacy to the listener of today.

Alexis Weissenberg's approach to the piano is the result of a long and careful reflection: today, when extremes are fashionable, when the search for effect at all costs is the order of the day, this record, made with such naturalness, with such a simple and direct approach, is a salutary reminder of how much can be obtained from an instrument like the piano when it is approached with respect, with love and with a profound knowledge of its expressive possibilities.

### ALEXIS WEISSENBERG

1929: Born December 26 in Sofia, Bulgaria. Began to study the piano with his father when he was three.

1945: After studying at the State Conservatory of Sofia with Pancho Vladigerov, he emigrated to Palestine with his family. He continues his studies there with Leo Kestenberg.

1946: He moved to the USA, entered the Juilliard School of Music in New York where he studied with Olga Samaroff-Stokowski.

1947: Won the First Prize at the Leventritt Competition. It's the beginning of an intense international career: he plays with the most important orchestras of the world, under the direction of the greatest conductors.

1955-1962: He retired from the concert scene. He lived in Switzerland, dedicated himself to study.

---

## Page 5 (scans/HIPPO_20230319_0005.jpg)

1962: He made a sensational comeback at Carnegie Hall in New York, beginning the most successful period of his career. From this moment on, he performed regularly in all the major concert halls of Europe, USA, Japan and Australia. His tours in the former Soviet Union were also highly successful.

He started an intense recording activity, which led him to work with many record companies, leaving to posterity a wide discography, which includes works by composers from different eras, from Bach to contemporary authors.

Particularly important are his complete recordings of the piano works by Brahms and Chopin, to which are added numerous records dedicated to Schubert, Schumann, Beethoven, Rachmaninov, Prokofiev etc.

Alexis Weissenberg died on January 8, 2012 in Lugano, Switzerland.
