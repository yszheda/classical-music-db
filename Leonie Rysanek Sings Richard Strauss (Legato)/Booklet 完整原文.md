## Inner Page 2

[Photo: Leonie Rysanek portrait]

Nothing could contrast more strongly with the barbaric tragedy of *ELEKTRA* and *SALOME* than the sophisticated comedy of *DER ROSENKAVALIER*. Native Austrian Leonie Rysanek enjoyed a long love affair with the Vienna State Opera, venue of the performance recorded in the final selections on this disc, and she understood perfectly the bittersweet spirit that is at the heart of the character of the Marschallin. Too often we hear this music "talked" or crooned: no danger of that here, for even the rococo historical period and the bittersweet waltz melodies of Strauss do not temp Rysanek to be mannered or arch. Her Marie Therese is, rather, a flesh and blood woman who loves not wisely but too well. The "climax" of the celebrated trio is for once literally that: a veritable orgasm of emotion, with Rysanek crying out the pain and loss of the Marschallin in a ringing high B that must have left the gala audience prostrate. "Unique" is a word we hear altogether too frequently these days. It really should be reserved for the phenomenal, the unforgettable, the once-in-a-lifetime. That is, for Leonie Rysanek.

### Biography

Leonie Rysanek (1926-1998) enjoyed a reputation as an unusually versatile soprano, but it is as a peerless interpreter of the heroines of Richard Strauss that she is primarily celebrated. Her distinctive voice boasted a top register of glowing thrust, tireless power, and radiant bloom, a perfect instrument for the ecstatic but treacherously difficult Straussian soprano line. In addition, Mme. Rysanek's heartfelt acting and magnetic stage presence brought warmth and excitement to the larger-than-life characters of Ariadne, Helena, Chrysothemis, Salome and the Marschallin we hear in this tribute to her art. Leonie Rysanek had the honor of starring in four Metropolitan Opera premieres, including *ARIADNE AUF NAXOS*, taking on the dual roles of the Primadonna (in hilariously accented English) and Ariadne at the request of Strauss's disciple Karl Böhm. In a performance from 1966, Rysanek is heard in the heroine's "Es gibt ein Reich," an unusually wide ranging piece that displays both the soprano's smoky low register and her effortless high pianissimo. An early Rysanek role was *DIE AEGYPTISCHE HELENA*, and it is clear from the loud applause at the finish of the great second act aria "Zweite Brautnacht!" that the Munich audience have discovered a new star. Strauss depicts the ecstasy of Helena with a vocal line incorporating precipitous leaps, sudden changes of key, rhythm, and dynamics, and of course the trademark long-breathed phrases soaring above the staff. The climax of the aria is a stratospheric melisma rising to several high Bs and even a high C-sharp, child's play for Rysanek.

Chrysothemis in *ELEKTRA* was at one time generally considered rather secondary to the "star" roles of Elektra and Klytemnestra in the same opera. When Leonie Rysanek sang this part, she held her own, even when matched against such legendary divas as Birgit Nilsson and Regina Resnik, as she was in the 1966 Vienna performance from which we hear "Ich kann nicht sitzen." The text of this monologue speaks of the character's longing to lead a peaceful existence as a wife and mother; but when Rysanek sings it, domestic life sounds as thrilling as Elektra's bloody dreams of vengeance!

Leonie Rysanek learned the role of Salome in the early 1960s, but she wisely waited another decade before performing the part on stage. In the interim her voice developed into a richer, more exotically-colored middle register and an almost terrifying power on top; in addition, she had grown as a vocal actress, with a flair for expressive use of words and rhythm. Many otherwise fine interpreters of Salome run out of steam toward the end of the punishing final scene and have to "fake" the last few pages. Rysanek, on the other hand, seems to grow vocally stronger measure by measure, with enough power in reserve to pull out one final stop for her final rapturous cry of "Ich habe deinen mund geküsst, Jokanann!"
