## 内页文章（英文）
### Liner Notes by Gwendolen Webster

Frederick Rzewski (1938-2021) began to fuse progressive and traditional music into overtly political works during the revolutionary Sixties. This new recording presents key examples of a genre he made his own. Here he employs the age-old practice of using the singable (work, protest and gospel songs) as a basis for variations that run a gamut of emotions yet never forget their roots. Some songs convey guarded hope, others brood on the dank engine rooms of capitalism, all confront us with issues that remain acute. Increasingly our lives are impacted by the casualties of poverty, pollution, oppression, exploitation, and war. As an age of complacency crumbles around us, we are discovering - as Rzewski did in the Sixties - that we're all political now.

Simply the titles of his wordless North American Ballads reveal their theme: the plight of society's downtrodden. Hope flickers through their lives, but resignation prevails. The naivety of Dreadful Memories fades under heavy bass lines and chromatic cascades, dissolving in wistful reflection. Which side are you on? another miners' song, vacillates between pensive and fraught, tonal and atonal, regular and irregular, clashing and wrenching apart. Snatches of the melody serve as prompts, and an improvising pianist joins the fray till banality halts the conflict. Down by the Riverside meanders through the disarmingly easy-going, the murkily contrapuntal and the tuneful, even hymnic, evoking both the soldier's burden of weaponry and his resolve to abandon his murderous duties. The strands of Winnsboro Cotton Mill Blues spin a relentless din, converging in maniacal union. An aching blues emerges, only to disintegrate as machinery resumes control.

The Yiddish Mayn Yingele (My Little Son) articulates the heartbreak of a worker crushed by a pitiless routine that bars him from any prospect of parental joy. Bouts of nervous counterpoint shift though dark melancholy, stifled anger, unfulfilled longing and dreary frustration. Rzewski wrote it on the 50th anniversary of Kristallnacht, which initiated the Nazi's policy of systematic persecution.

The People United Will Never Be Defeated consists of variations on a Chilean resistance song embedded in a complex format that requires extreme pianistic virtuosity and stamina. Echoes of the old Brecht/Eisler Solidarity Song are threaded into the score, diverse styles woven into its fabric. The revolution was never going to be easy.

At the root of all these pieces lies a perception of absence. Here we have society's losers, beset by misery, craving justice and equality. All harbour a tragic awareness of the unattainable, even The People United, for despite its rousing finale, it remains the anthem of a lost cause. The singers too are long dead, and in the absence of language, we are reliant on the music's immediacy. Rzewski offsets the void with a rich musical vocabulary and the revival of memories that lend form to absence, thus creating the pianist's arena. Do we respond as sympathetic listeners or empathetic activists? Composers, Rzewski stated, were unlikely to influence politics. But it was his conviction that 'you have to write as if you could'.
