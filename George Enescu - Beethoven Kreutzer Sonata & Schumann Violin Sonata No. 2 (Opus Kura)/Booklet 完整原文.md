## Enesco: his last recordings
### By Shigeo Itakura

I must start more than half a century ago. In Japan, not many records about violinists are available from before the war. Records of the violin were limited to those of Heifetz, Elman, Kreisler, and Szigeti. Among them, Enesco's recordings were very difficult to obtain. In the US, Yehudi Menuhin's "Violin Sonata No. 4" and Chausson's "Poème" were issued by Ruth Tolushka and Jeanne Power. Menuhin and Enesco were pupils of the same teacher, and Enesco was also admired by Menuhin. Beethoven's "Kreutzer" sonata is very rare though, as is the Schumann violin sonata which was once issued by USB Heritage. I was very pleased that these fine performances have now been reissued by Opus Kura as such good sound.

Enesco's "Kreutzer" has received both positive and negative criticism since the LP was first reissued. Of his extraordinarily unique tone and interpretation, I must say, frankly: "This is an old-fashioned way of playing." However, his performance displays a brilliant violinist who used legato and bold bowing, which added a sensuous influence to the music, resulting in a 20th-century work with a unique temperament and character. The "Kreutzer" sonata that I knew from Heifetz's 1930s performance was completely different from Enesco's interpretation. It was in 1952, when he was already past 70, that he performed this sonata. His tone is warm and rich, and his phrasing is expressive, full of life. The Schumann sonata is similarly deeply felt, with a warmth and tenderness that is unique to Enesco's style.
