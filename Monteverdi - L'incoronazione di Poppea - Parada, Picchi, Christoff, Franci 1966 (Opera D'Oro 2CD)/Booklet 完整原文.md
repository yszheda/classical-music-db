## L'incoronazione di Poppea

### by Claudio Monteverdi

*L'incoronazione di Poppea* (The Coronation of Poppea) is the oldest opera still firmly in the active repertory of opera houses around the world. It is also the first known opera based on historical source materials. The excellent libretto, which accounts for a good deal of the opera's theatrical appeal, was written by Giovanni Francesco Busenello, who based the story on incidents related by Tacitus and Seutonius, ancient Roman historians. Its first performance was during the Carnival of 1643 in Venice. After that it was neglected and nearly forgotten for nearly three centuries until attempts were made to revive it in the 1920s. By then, unfortunately, only two fragmentary manuscripts, one from Naples and one from Venice, survived. There are striking differences between them, apparently reflecting later revisions and editings by other hands.

In the early 1960s, British conductor and harpsichordist Raymond Leppard prepared an edition with a rich orchestral accompaniment that held the stage until the Early Music movement began insisting on more authentic reconstructions of the score. These efforts still are very much in flux, with competing theories as how best to represent Monteverdi's intentions. Ironically, the composer's intentions may be forever uncertain, especially as modern scholarship has ascertained almost beyond doubt that some of the surviving music is not by Monteverdi at all, but represents interpolations by his contemporaries Cavalli and Sacrati, and possibly others.

The standard realization used in most contemporary productions is laid out in a Prologue and three acts. The abridged edition used on this historic recording telescopes the action into two acts, the first act ending with the death of Seneca, which was originally in the middle of Act Two.

## THE STORY

**Act I.** Returning from the field, the warrior Otho is dismayed early one morning to find his lover Poppea entertaining Nero in her palace, surrounded by the emperor's grumbling guards. Inside, Poppea cajoles Nero into banishing his wife, the empress Octavia, to prove he really loves her as he claims. After Nero has sneaked out of the palace, Poppea's old nurse Arnalta warns her that the empress is onto the affair and could be vengeful; but Poppea laughs that both Cupid and the goddess Fortune are on her side.

In the Golden House, Octavia laments her humiliation and plots her revenge. The philosopher Seneca tries to assuage the empress with comforting nostrums. With stiff courtesy she dismisses his advice for what it is, empty rhetoric. After that her impish page threatens to set the old man's beard on fire if he doesn't come up with a plan of action instead of mere fine talk. Nero, too, consults Seneca, announcing that he has decided to repudiate Octavia and marry Poppea. When Seneca warns him of adverse public and Senatorial opinion, Nero angrily asserts his absolute prerogative, "Right or wrong," he shouts, "this very day Poppea shall be my wife." After a sensuous love duet with Poppea, Nero sends an order to Seneca to kill himself. Reproached by Otho, Poppea coldly gloats that she has a higher destiny with royalty. Otho regrets he ever loved her, and to spite her offers his love to Drusilla, a courtesan whom he formerly spurned. Meanwhile, Seneca prepares himself philosophically for death. Liberto, captain of the guard, delivers the royal death warrant with sincere regret, and Seneca bids farewell to life as his friends and relatives lament.

**Act II.** Nero and his friend Lucan rejoice over the death of Seneca, drinking themselves under the table. Octavia orders Otho, on pain of death, to murder Poppea. Still in love with Poppea, he agrees only under duress. He badgers Drusilla into lending him one of her gowns as a disguise. Poppea prepares to take a nap in the garden, ordering Arnalta to allow no one to disturb her except close friends like Drusilla. Arnalta sings a lullaby to her mistress. Torn by conflicting emotions, Otho sneaks up upon the dreaming Poppea, but is saved from making the fatal decision by the intercession of Cupid. Drusilla is arrested and dragged before Nero, who accuses her of attempted murder. She confesses in order to protect Otho, but Otho convinces the emperor that Drusilla is lying on his behalf. Nero sends Otho into exile. He offers to spare Drusilla in consideration of her selfless courage, but she insists on joining Otho and her wish is granted.

Nero and Poppea exult that at last there are no further impediments to their marriage. Octavia bids a bitter farewell to Rome as she is placed on a ship to be carried far out to sea. With much pomp, Poppea is crowned empress of Rome. After the ceremony, Nero and Poppea are left alone to gaze lovingly into each other's eyes and sing a soft, rapturous duet.
