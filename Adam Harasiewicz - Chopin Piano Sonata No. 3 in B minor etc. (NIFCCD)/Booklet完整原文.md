### 波兰语传记 (页14)

Adam Harasiewicz

Nagrodzony na konkursie młodych talentów w Rzeszowie [1947], studia pianistyczne odbył w Państwowej Wyższej Szkole Muzycznej w Krakowie pod kierunkiem Zygmunta Dreszera, który w 1956 roku uhonorował go dyplomem z wyróżnieniem. W 1955 roku zdobył pierwszą nagrodę w VI Międzynarodowym Konkursie im. Fryderyka Chopina w Warszawie [1955], otrzymał nagrodę specjalną Funduszu im. Fryderyka Chopina za wybitne osiągnięcia w propagowaniu muzyki Chopina za granicą oraz nagrodę specjalną Funduszu im. Ignacego Jana Paderewskiego. Rok później otrzymał II nagrodę w Międzynarodowym Konkursie im. Marguerite Long i Jacques'a Thibaud w Paryżu [1956]. Dyplom Ministra Kultury i Sztuki w 1960 roku, za wybitne propagowanie muzyki Chopina, otrzymał od Filharmonii Nowosądeckiej im. Ludwika Mierosławskiego nadanie mu tytułu honoris causa. Od 1956 roku koncertuje na wszystkich kontynentach, w największych salach koncertowych Europy, Stanów Zjednoczonych, Japonii i Australii. Występuje również w telewizji. Prowadził kursy mistrzowskie interpretacji chopinowskiej dla Narodowego Instytutu Fryderyka Chopina oraz w Międzynarodowej Akademii Chopinowskiej w Dusznikach. Prowadził klasy mistrzowskie w wielu krajach Europy, Stanów Zjednoczonych, Kanady, Australii, Chin, Argentyny, Japonii. Artysta został odznaczony Krzyżem Oficerskim Orderu Odrodzenia Polski [1996] i złotym medalem "Gloria Artis" [2008].

### 英语传记 (页15)

Adam Harasiewicz

Top Prize Winner in the Young Talents Competition in Rzeszów [1947], he studied piano at the State Higher School of Music in Kraków under the direction of Zygmunt Dreszer. In 1956 he graduated with honours. He won first prize in the 6th International Chopin Piano Competition in Warsaw [1955]. Two years later he was awarded the Paderewski Foundation Prize and the Harriet Cohen International Music Award for outstanding achievement in piano and in 1960, the Gold Medal awarded by the Ignacy Jan Paderewski Foundation in New York for his contribution to the propagation of Chopin's works abroad. In 1958 he won 2nd prize at the Long-Thibaud Competition in Paris. In 1958 he inaugurated the Chopin Year marking the 150th anniversary of the composer's birth by performing Chopin's Concerto with the New York Philharmonic Orchestra at Carnegie Hall under Leonard Bernstein. He has appeared with the world's best orchestras under the direction of celebrated conductors in renowned concert halls across Europe, the United States and Asia. He has also recorded for television. He has been awarded the Silver Cross of Merit from the Minister of Culture and Art of Poland [Chopin Competition prize in 1955, 2000, 2005, 2010] and in Italy and Japan. He has made recording discs, mostly issued by Philips and Decca, with Chopin's works among them. For The Fryderyk Chopin Institute and Polskie Radio he recorded works by Szymanowski. He has led master classes in the interpretation of Chopin's works in Austria, Germany, Italy, France and Japan. The pianist has been awarded the Officer's Cross of the Order of Polonia Restituta from the Polish President [1996] and the Golden Medal of Merit to Culture "Gloria Artis" [2008].

## 波兰语文本 (Image 0004, pp. 40–41)

Romantyczność Chopina...

Z dzisiejszej perspektywy twórczość Chopina jawi się niczym monument – wręcz wyjątkowym w historii muzyki. Skomponowana na jedynym tylko instrumencie, a zarazem zaiste uboga w swej deklaracji, gatunkowo nader skromna, a – by tak rzec – wciąż po nowa. Z perspektywy wielowiekowej dziejów muzyki lokuje się jej jedyność: przynależność do epoki romantyzmu, przy czym ten u Chopina ma niezwykłą osobność. Wystarczy stwierdzić, iż w klasycznym repertuarze, dziś tak powszechnym, bodaj nigdzie nie ma tak wielu pozycji jak u niego. Choć z perspektywy klasycznej tradycji XIX stulecie jawi się – w uproszczeniu – epoką dominacji muzyki symfonicznej i operowej, Chopin przerasta i te obszary przynależnością duchową.

Jej początki tkwią w prekursorstwie, „melodramatyczności" stylu brillant – błyskotliwego, eleganckiego kompozytorskiego wirtuoza, pianistycznego klawiszowego wzorców – datowanego okresu wirtuozerii, na który składały się m.in. z Études op. 10, to kompozycje z pogranicza sztuk, oparte o wybitną literaturę. W tym okresie u niego zaledwie – chęć odtworzenia rzekomo – nie tylko samego utworu, lecz i scenerii, w jakiej ten był wykonywany, ba! niekiedy nawet i wizualizacji! Przykład pierwszy, fantastyczna Ballada, instrumentalną małą formą narracyjną, a nawet z wizją Baśka(?) w tle. W literackim dorobku, w tym w baśka(?) na tym tle – zgodnie z poglądami poetów – literatury i muzyki: „muzyka z literaturą, ta pierwsza zaś muzyka – trafia wprost do serca, bez pośrednictwa rozumu" (brak dalszej części zdania ucięte).

## 英语文本 (Image 0007, pp. 44–45)

### Chopin's romanticism

From our present-day perspective, the oeuvre of Chopin might seem monolithic—even exceptionally so in the history of music. Concentrated on just a single instrument, it is essentially confined to two decades, stylistically consistently and remarkably uniform, and also, due to the absence of a stage work or orchestral composition, fundamentally one-dimensional rather than constantly turning new sides. In the eyes of his contemporaries, however, it was far from homogeneous. All the experiments and forays into unknown territory which Chopin made in the course of the decades are documented in works that remained quite shocking for nearly the whole of the nineteenth century. However, within his quantitatively rather modest oeuvre (in comparison with other great composers of that time), Chopin travelled a vast space.

Its beginning lies in the pre-Romantic "Brilliant" style—dazzling, elegant concert pieces in virtuoso accordance with Classical models—which would soon be subsumed by the more advanced trends of the early and high romanticism. Pure and profound, yet detached from a wide range of phenomena that were crucial to the Romantics. Above all, a purely instrumental work can have

a narrative flow and even be called a Ballade. But why would it be based on some literary content? For Chopin, music was a value in itself, a supreme, autotelic value. On the one hand, he was a follower of an aesthetic tradition that valued the supreme ideal of art in the abstract nature of music, reaching listeners' feelings directly (without the use of concrete words or images). But Chopin applied that principle with radical consistency. He admired the Lieder of Schubert, which is evident even in his earliest works, and he was not at all alone: Schumann, Mendelssohn, Liszt, and even more so, Berlioz—each of the great protagonists of the epoch turned primarily to literature and borrowed its themes. Schubert, Schumann and Berlioz also composed operas, while Mendelssohn and Liszt provided texts for hundreds of songs, or even leit to opera libretti. For Chopin, however, literature could only ever serve as an equivalent (if not a model), or else to provoke the lacking of his works with any text.

Paradoxically, his oeuvre is particularly distinct in the Piano Sonata in F flat minor and the Piano Concerto in F minor, the most 'symphonic' of his works. Rooted Chopin was in the aesthetics of art music, although at the same time it is a thoroughly Romantic work, not devoid of a narrative character. Also Classical form was very important for him. His most significant works, comprising four movements, are either sonatas or ballades. Each of the two movements, in accordance with the Classical rule, is a dramaturgical response to the previous movement, yet the dynamic and the mood of the emotions that are stirred, as well as their scale, turn out to be truly Romantic. Each and every sonata, for example, departs from the very beginning, with a short, four-note motif, racing, drawing down like lightning, draws us in at once in medias res, tracing a predatory, agility-filled

None of Chopin's immediate predecessors managed to compose a new, Romantic sonata of lasting value. The problem was, in the last analysis, a formal one. The sonata was perceived in this period as belonging to the German Romantic tradition, crowned with the thirty-two works in the recently deceased [1827] Beethoven's grandiose in the sonata, however, became an iron mould. Schumann did not attempt to resolve the conundrum; Liszt waited for a long time to tackle the sonata until after Chopin's death, creating a work that was in many respects an epigone. Berlioz, who never quite managed to achieve the epic style, did not reject the Classical thesis of the sonata. His only symphony [L'Italie] is tinged with lyrical expression; he was unable to connect that with emotions that he had not previously suspected in them.

And yet there is something which, though not justifying, does explain the persistent linking of Romantic music with the literary ballads of the Romantics. The essence of the Ballade lies in the narration of the fate of its heroes, equally crucial: the shattering of the predictable canon, the "pleasure of surprise" that was so essential to the Romantics, distinguishing them from the Classical tradition. All this, along with the characteristic presentation of mysterious events [during Chopin's last years in Warsaw, Mickiewicz's Ballades were the subject of heated discussion] Maurycy Mochnacki wrote that "mystery" —

And he departed from those models, meanwhile, in the ballades. The name, which is an innovation of Chopin's in instrumental music, emphasizes the narrative quality that was so dear to the Romantics. Of course, an epic character can also be traced in the tone poems, but here it manifests itself in a different way: not through programmatic content, and yet apart from the actual idea of recounting, "spinning out" a narrative, Chopin's ballade possesses no programme whatsoever. This becomes apparent when listening to the two masterpieces of the genre [the Second {G minor} and Fourth {F minor} ballades] that are both, in their different ways, based on a modification of fragments of themes, which are heard in various registers and keys, in an increasingly rich texture, and in a range of dynamics. This work—greater in form and scope than even the two scherzos—was for a long time the most popular work in the keyboard literature. To the history of its recordings belong the first [by de Pachmann], the second [by Joseffy], and the third [by Rosenthal]: then the Fourth was the work most often performed, while they were usually sought in the poetry of Mickiewicz. The A flat major was associated with the legend of the mermaid, beautiful Lenore from Heine's poem.

The programme of our disc has two more powerful accents. The first is the Polonaise in F sharp minor, Op. 71, No. 1 — a work of Chopin's teenage years, with an astonishing transformation of the idea of national dances [Chopin to the publisher Mechetti: "it's sort of polonaise, but it's more of a fantasy" — the tris is a mazarik]. The idea of this work is an extraordinary one: after two rather short slow movements, the forces evoked by the virtuosic octaves are dramatically driven through to the contemplation of a chorale-like theme, concluding with an intricate, airily falling figuration.

The main works are interleaved with four nocturnes, the selection of which suggests motion around the axis of the key of B — in its major or minor version. The two earliest nocturnes [Op. 9 and Op. 15] belong to a world in which Chopin is not yet free from the seductions of Italian vocal music and the decidedly sentimental composer John Field, painting tender moonlit scenes that are the best fruits of that aesthetic. With time, however, Chopin would transcend these — the Nocturne in E minor, Op. 72 No. 1 and the Nocturne in B major, Op. 27, and avoid the path of "moonlit" lyricism and become saturated with an imperial glow of fortissimo. When even that spell is no longer enough for Chopin, the nocturnes develop in the direction of the poetic tone: among their number is one of the

last nocturnes, in B major, from Op. 62, which at the same time refers to the form of the grand-da capo aria, serving a return of the first section in a decorative way. With time Chopin's nocturnes cease to be merely beautiful and, even more importantly, purely ornamental in significance: it complements the narrative situation just enough for Walter Scott's motto that one must "amuse imagination, not satisfy it", published in the Polish press in 1829, to remain current.
