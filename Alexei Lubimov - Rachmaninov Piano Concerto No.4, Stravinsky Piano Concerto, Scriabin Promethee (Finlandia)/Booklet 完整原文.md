## Sergei Rachmaninov (1873-1943)
### Piano Concerto no. 4 in G minor, op. 40

When Sergei Rachmaninov fled the Russian revolution around Christmas 1917, settling in the United States in November the following year, he brought virtually nothing with him in the way of material possessions. As a pianist, his immediate concern was to support himself financially, and he embarked on an intensive programme of concerts to support his family. His extensive composing career almost came to an end, and he was unable to concentrate on writing music for many years.

It was not until 1926 that he was able to take a break from touring and resume work in his new country. Eight years had elapsed since Rachmaninov had fled his homeland, and he had written a single work since then. Among the sketches that he had brought with him were materials that may have been scraps of the Fourth Piano Concerto. He is known to have worked on the piece during his last summer in Russia in 1917. However, he left work on what was to be finished at first in 1914. Rachmaninov completed the work in 1926, after approximately three years of work, and in a letter to composer Nikolai Medtner he joked that it would probably better to be performed over several evenings! The work is dedicated to Leopold Godowsky.

Rachmaninov played the solo piano part himself at the premiere in Philadelphia on March 18, 1927. The orchestra was conducted by Leopold Stokowski. Rachmaninov's authorship as a pianist was not enough to convince the critics, who considered the piece a relic from the previous century. Rachmaninov was not satisfied with the work, either: he returned to it in the summer of 1927 and again in the summer of 1941, cutting a total of nearly 200 bars of music. Despite these alterations, the Piano Concerto No. 4 has often been overshadowed by its more popular siblings, the Second and Third. In the middle of the 1990s, the Fourth is now more subdued and austere, yet not without warmth.

The first movement begins with a short orchestral introduction, followed by the sonorous main theme presented by the piano. A flowing figural transition leads to the lyrical and harmonious second theme. In the development section, the music becomes more turbulent with the piano and orchestra in a mighty climax. The recapitulation presents the main themes in an inverted and transformed order. The piano floats the music into the mood of the second movement. The mood darkens, and there is a darkly flowing outburst lifted by Rachmaninov from his earlier piano piece of his *Etudes-Tableaux* in G minor (1911). The slow movement moves from an earlier piano piece where special features include a quote from the Rachmaninov theme that appears in many of Rachmaninov's compositions. The theme here is surprisingly airy and almost improvisatory. There seems to be no emphasis on meter or rhythm, in keeping with the romantic tradition, these lines incorporate enumerations made by the composer himself, taken from authentic sources.

This recording incorporates emendations made by the composer himself, taken from authentic sources.

## Igor Stravinsky (1882-1971)
### Concerto for piano and wind instruments

Igor Stravinsky often wrote his music at and on the terms of the piano, even if there was no piano in the actual work. However Stravinsky was no virtuoso pianist—Rimsky-Korsakov, Prokofiev or Prokofiev. His keyboard skills were rather modest, and his abilities as a performer at the piano works in public for a period of about fifteen years. His early works of his period as a student was playing the piano works of Rimsky-Korsakov, he concentrated on conducting performances and writing.

Stravinsky's career as a concert pianist began with the Concerto for Two Pianos (1935), completed in 1931. Sergei Koussevitzky insisted Stravinsky write a concerto. He was nearing completion and suggested that the title should include the dedication to Koussevitzky. Stravinsky, who had previously appeared as a soloist with the Boston Symphony Orchestra in 1925, was hesitant at first but later, even before the conductor's commission, began to write. With Koussevitzky's proposal and began to put his keyboard skills to good use, he composed the work with material such as the etudes of Czerny and scales.

The premiere, which Koussevitzky conducted in Paris in 1924, was not received well though Stravinsky discussed it positively in his autobiography (1936). By the time the concerto came to the slow movement, Stravinsky felt that he had completely forgotten how the movement was supposed to start. He whispered the problem to Koussevitzky, who glanced at the score and hummed the opening bars to Stravinsky, who then immediately recalled the piano part and proceeded with the performance. Stravinsky reserved the right to perform the piece for himself for a period of five years, giving himself an opportunity to present the work to a large number of audiences. In the five-year period he played the work nearly 40 times all over Europe and America, both for his personal use, the Capriccio for Piano and Orchestra.

The Concerto for Piano and Wind Instruments is one of Stravinsky's neoclassical pieces from the 1920s in which wind instruments play a major role—other such works are Symphonies of Wind Instruments (1920), the opera Mavra (1922) and the Octet (1923). On the other hand Stravinsky continued to occupy the composer in such works as the ballets *Le Sacre du printemps* (1913) and *Les noces* (1923). The concerto is, typically for Stravinsky of the 1920s and 1930s, a composition of contrasts and extremes: it could equally well be described as dry and objective as emotional. The movements give the impression of a group of jazz or ragtime.

The work is in three movements. The rhythmic first movement begins with a brilliant piano opening with a solemn slow processional introduction. A more sprightly toccata with an added dimension of profundity in the coda where the introduction of the first movement is recalled before the final spark.

## Alexander Scriabin (1872-1915)
### Prométhée, Le poème du feu, op. 60

In Greek mythology, Prometheus was a Titan who stole the secret of fire from Zeus, the head god, and gave it to mankind. Zeus brought a terrible vengeance on Prometheus: he chained him to a rock in the Caucasus Mountains. Every day an eagle came to peck at his liver, which was torn apart by the bird's beak, then regenerated during the night. Prometheus was finally freed by Hercules. It is with relief that we read those verses of Aeschylus in which Prometheus is at last liberated after all his other labours, kills the bird and frees Prometheus.

It is not surprising that Prometheus was a fascinating figure for many composers. He has served for Beethoven and Liszt before him. Scriabin's poem is also connected to Nietzsche's Zarathustra, a distant hero bringing the light of higher consciousness to man, while liberating his own suffering. At the same time, this figure blinded by his own brilliance, has an almost Faustian dimension. His dream was to produce a grand work that would encompass all arts, in which music, theatre, poetry, drama and dance – but also science and religion – could form an organic unity.

Scriabin remained unverified: Scriabin's all-embracing Utopian vision seem bewildering from today's point of view, but it would be incorrect to dismiss him in terms of the century various mystical movements were quite widespread.

In keeping with the mystical dimension of his outlook on his music, *Prometheus* increasingly moved away from the traditional tonality. *Prometheus, Le poème du feu* (Poem of Fire), completed in 1910, is one of the most manifestations of Scriabin's pioneering musical occupation. It is written for a triple woodwind, quadruple woodwind, eight horns, five trumpets, three trombones, tuba, timpani, cymbal, tam-tam, organ, piano, mixed choir and a solo piano that plays a prominent part in the whole of the grand style of Mysteriorum; there is a part for a light show that, however, was not included in the premiere of the work in Moscow in 1911, conducted by Serge Koussevitzky.

Scriabin wrote an exhaustive and exhaustive philosophically oriented programme for *Prométhée*. According to this programme, the music depicts the evolution of the spirit from chaos. The chord heard in the opening bars, the so-called "mystic chord", contains the notes C, F#, B, E, A, D. The chord is a symphony of chords constructed out of short motifs. Scriabin uses his own orchestra here to paint impressionistic pictures of the birth of the spirit, celebrating surfaces of colour and to conjure forth fantastic figures and scenes. The music moves from the ethereal to the earthly, from darkness to light. The work concludes with an apotheosis: a grandiose flourish.

## Alexei Lubimov

Pianist Alexei Lubimov, born in Moscow in 1944, began his music studies at the Central School of Music in its native city, and continued in the Moscow Conservatory as one of the last disciples of Heinrich Neuhaus. At that stage he was noted in musical circles for his enormous musicality and his exceptional technique.

In 1969, his career received a real driving work from John Cage and Terry Riley. In the following years, he was one of the first Soviet musicians to perform 20th century works, piano pieces by composers such as Stockhausen, Webern, and John Cage, and was one of the pioneers of the early music movement in Soviet Union. He also frequently played works by Mozart, Gallus, and Beethoven. It seems only logical that Alexei Lubimov should become founder and moving spirit of the Moscow "Opus Ensemble", a group devoted to avant-garde music festival. In the former Soviet Union, the life of a musician devoted to contemporary Western music was not without problems - concerts were cancelled or postponed, culminated in his being prohibited to travel abroad, a ban that lasted seven years. Making good use of this time, Alexei Lubimov studied old technologies on period instruments, founding the Moscow "Opus Quartet" in 1988, and gave recitals with such artists as Oleg Grivenko, the Moscow Chamber Academy, and Mark Pekarsky. He has also made significant ensembles that focus on historical performance.

In 1987, the international career of Alexei Lubimov began as soloist with major orchestras not only in the USA, Europe and Japan, giving acclaimed performances with major orchestras such as the Munich, St. Petersburg and Helsinki Philharmonic Orchestras, the Israel and Los Angeles Philharmonic Orchestras, the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra in London, the Swedish Radio Symphony, the Weimarer Staatskapelle, the Orchestre Philharmonique de Radio France, the Toronto Symphony Orchestra and the Deutsches Sinfonie-Orchester Berlin. His partners have included Vladimir Ashkenazy, Frans Brüggen, Veronique Zanetti, Kirill Kondrashin, Christopher Hogwood, Sir Alexander Gibson, Andrew Litton, Yutaka Sado, Mikhail Pletnev, Gennady Rozhdestvensky, Jukka-Pekka Saraste, Leif Segerstam, Klaus Weise and von Pascoal Terfel.

Alexei Lubimov has also appeared frequently with period instrument ensembles such as the Amsterdam Baroque Orchestra, Collegium Vocale Gent, Orchester Akademie and the Collegium Vocale Gent.

Lubimov's work has been released in Russia, Germany, France and Great Britain, by labels as Melodiya, Erato, Virgin Classics, BMG/Arcana, Teldec, and Finlandia. He appears regularly with Nordermønt, Denmark's Kronos String Quartet, and the Arditti Quartet.

He has made a total of over 30 recordings, many of which have received awards such as the "Grand Prix du Disque", "Diapason d'Or", "Choc - Le Monde de la Musique". These recordings cover a repertoire that ranges from Frescobaldi, Bach, Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Chopin, Beethoven and Brahms to music of the 20th century. He also has a busy recording schedule for 1997.

## Jukka-Pekka Saraste

The 1996-1997 season marks Jukka-Pekka Saraste's third year as Music Director of the Toronto Symphony Orchestra. The first two years of his tenure have seen an ever-increasing artistic and critical success. Highlights of the 1996-1997 season include performances of the three Brahms symphonies and an all-Russian program featuring soloists Sarah Chang and Vladimir Ashkenazy. The season also includes an orchestral explanation of artistic collaboration and education with the local community.

Saraste has signed an exclusive recording contract with Warner Classics/Finlandia Records under which he will record six discs with the Toronto Symphony Orchestra. The three-year agreement includes the first multi-year recording agreement in the Orchestra's corporate history and represents a real breakthrough for an American Record. First agreement with a North American record company.

Born in 1956 in Imatra, Finland, Mr. Saraste began his musical career in the orchestra of the Finnish Radio. From the age of 12 he was encouraged by his teachers to study conducting. In 1978 he joined the Finnish Radio Symphony Orchestra as a violinist, studying with Jorma Panula and Eri Klas, and in 1981 he won the first prize in the Scandinavian Conductors' Competition.

Since his professional conducting debut with the Finnish Radio Symphony Orchestra in 1983, his reputation has grown as one of the most gifted conductors of his generation. He made his U.S. debut in 1986, and in 1987 the Helsinki Philharmonic in several concerts during his tenure with the Orchestra. As a co-founder of the Avant-Garde Orchestra, Saraste helped establish Finland's first modern ensemble devoted to contemporary music. In his post as principal conductor of the Edinburgh-based Scottish Chamber Orchestra (1987-1991), Saraste also toured through Europe and the United States, including performances for Carnegie Hall.

Though the Toronto Symphony Orchestra has become his primary North American conducting activities, he continues to serve as Principal Guest Conductor of the Norwegian Radio Orchestra, a position he has held since 1987, runs an annual summer music festival in addition to his appearances with the PSO in Helsinki. Saraste has toured Europe, the United States and Japan, and has recorded for the Finnish Radio and the German Radio. He has also been the recipient of numerous medals and decorations, including the Order of the Lion of Finland and the Finnish State Music Award. Saraste was named Conductor of the Year by the Finnish Music Council in 1991.

Mr. Saraste continues to demand internationally as a guest conductor. He has appeared with the Boston Symphony Orchestra at Tanglewood, and has also worked with the Cleveland, Detroit and Minnesota Orchestras, the Royal Stockholm, the Vienna Symphony Orchestra, the Rotterdam Philharmonic Orchestra, the Orchestre de la Suisse Romande, the Bavarian Radio Symphony, the Swedish Radio and Santa Cecilia of Rome. He has also conducted the Orchestra of the Eighteenth Century with a grand piano flourish in 1997.

Saraste resides in Toronto with his family. He announced in fall 1996 to extend his contract as Principal Conductor of the TSO until the year 2000.
