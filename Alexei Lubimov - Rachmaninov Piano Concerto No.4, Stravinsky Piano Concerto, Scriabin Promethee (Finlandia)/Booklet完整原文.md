## Sergei Rachmaninov (1873-1943)

### Piano Concerto No. 4 in G minor, op. 40

When Sergei Rachmaninov fled the Russian revolution around Christmas 1917, settling in the United States with his wife Natalya and their two daughters, he brought virtually nothing with him apart from his piano scores. His life thereafter was divided into three parts. He immediately embarked on an intensive programme of concerts to support his family, and during the 1920s, when he lived mainly in France and America, this left very little time for composing. It was not until 1926 that he was able to take a complete year's sabbatical and devote it to composition.

Eight years had elapsed since Rachmaninov had composed any orchestral music - the last such work had been the Symphony no. 3 - and, although there are some sketches for a single work since then. Amongst these early sketches for his Fourth Piano Concerto there may have been scraps of the Fourth Piano Concerto were made when Rachmaninov was living in the USA during late 1913 and early 1914. In 1913-14, Rachmaninov completed the score, which he wanted to be premiered at the Mariinsky Theatre in St Petersburg where Rachmaninov was not satisfied with this work,

either he returned to it in the summer of 1927 and again in the autumn of that year, cutting a total of seventy-eight bars and also simplifying the piano part. The Fourth Piano Concerto was first performed in November 1927 with the Philadelphia Orchestra, and Rachmaninov himself as soloist, and then it was quickly forgotten, for the Second and Third Concertos are the more popular, the Third is the more melodious and the Fourth is more subdued and austere.

The first movement begins with a short orchestral introduction, and is then taken up by a flowing figuration presented to the piano. Flowing figured basses and an undulating second theme soon establish the themes of the symphony. In the development section, the music becomes agitated, eventually building up to a minor climax. The recapitulation presents itself in the form of a lengthy cadenza, with the orchestra adding the piano solos into the moody slow movement, which unfolds in the form of a rondo in C minor. The first theme has a darkly nostalgic atmosphere led by the winds and piano, reminiscent of the slow movement of Rachmaninov's C-sharp minor Etude-Tableau in C minor. The more solemn second theme presents a striking contrast, with the full orchestral body. However, the whole movement ends on a note of some bleakness.

Rachmaninov performed the concerto during the 1940s and in a letter to his cousin Natalya Satina, Rachmaninov wrote that 'It is so interesting, so wonderful, and it is more characteristic of Rachmaninov's compositions. However, it is not so easily identifiable, compared with numerous other wonderful compositions of his. The concerto's more modern character. The closing movement is furnished with improvisations made to the original sources.

## Igor Stravinsky (1882-1971)

### Concerto for Piano and Wind Instruments

Igor Stravinsky composed his Concerto on the terms of the pianist, even if there was nothing in them to encourage him to think of a pianist-composer like Rachmaninoff, Bartók or Prokofiev. His keyboard skills were limited to a modest technical accomplishment. Stravinsky never composed a piano concerto and works for a period of about fifteen years during which he was engaged in conducting concerts.

Stravinsky's concerto as a concept began in 1923, but was not completed until the summer of 1924, by which time Stravinsky was nearing completion and suggested that the composer should write the solo part himself. Stravinsky, who had previously appeared as pianist on several occasions, was now hesitant at first but, later, came to appreciate his task. He agreed to Koussevitzky's proposal and set about putting to keyboard instruments what he had so often done for the orchestra every day with material such as Beethoven, Mozart and Haydn.

The premiere, which Koussevitzky conducted in Paris on May 22, 1924, with Stravinsky at the keyboard, stirred up a hornet's nest of controversy in Stravinsky circles in the decades that followed. He later recalled that he had completely forgotten how the movements were laid out in his mind, the only solution to Koussevitzky, who glanced at the score and continued the opening piano baritone

then immediately recalled the piano part and proved that the composer's memory was accurate. He also won the right to perform the piece for himself for a period of five years, giving himself an opportunity to revise the piano part. During this five-year period he played the work nearly 40 times. The only other notable performance is by Capriccio for Piano and Orchestra (1929).

The Concerto for Piano and Wind Instruments, together with the Concerto in E flat for Piano, Percussion and Eight Wind Instruments (1920), the opera Mavra (1922) and the Piano-Rag-Music (1919) are all works he continued to occupy in his own thoughts works written for and with his friend, the pianist Arthur Rubinstein (Petrushka, 1925). The concerto is, typically for Stravinsky, no mere display of technical virtuosity. The liveliness and wit, it could equally well be termed a concerto for orchestra in which the piano plays a part; the outer movements give the impression of a group of jazz musicians playing; the serenely lyrical second movement is haunted by the ghosts of Bach and Corelli.

Stravinsky himself was in no hurry about the rhythmic aspects of the concerto. The first movement opens with a solemn slow processional introduction, followed by the march proper, complete with drum and fife. The second movement is austere and melancholy; lyrical passages alternate, one shortly after the first cadenza. The finale, too, is built on the contrast between lyrical and march-like dimensions of profundity in the coda where the march theme of the first movement is revisited in a triumphant manner.

## Alexander Scriabin (1872-1915)

### Prométhée, Le poème du feu, op. 60

In Greek mythology, Prometheus was one of the Titans who stole the secret of fire from Zeus, the lord of the Gods, and gave it to the mortals. He was then chained to a rock in the Caucasus Mountains. Every artist is a Prometheus who attempts to bring light and fire to his people. He is often persecuted, even crucified, as it is with reflex that we read these lines written by Scriabin in prison, on a piece of soap: "All labour, all love, all lives and fires."

It is not surprising that Prometheus was the last work to be heard in Moscow, having been created for Liszt and later Rimsky-Lesov. Sergei Koussevitzky was the first to perceive its possibilities, he who had been its first champion in Moscow, and its author and a fervent believer bringing the light of higher spirituality to humanity. "I want to bring it to humanity through suffering," at the same time, this figure, the famous "chord of the mystery," is an emanation of the Promethean idea. His desire was to produce a great work that would unite all of man's activities - music, drama, poetry, dance and colour - and also realise the vision of the universe, of a Russian religion, remained unfulfilled. Scriabin began arranging everything for a great orchestra in the summer of 1910, but in the meantime, the work was not to see him in point, nor was Scriabin to hear it before his death. Scriabin left only a sketch of Mysterium, and the turn of the century various musical magicians have attempted to create a synthesis of the arts.

In keeping with his mystical descriptions of the tone of Prometheus, Scriabin also abandoned symphonic lengths in his music and finally also abandoned tonal systems. Prometheus, Le poème du feu (Poem of fire), completed in 1910, is one of the most fascinating manifestations of Scriabin's pioneering approach. The score calls for an enlarged orchestra, with quadruple woodwind, eight brass, three harps, a keyboard glockenspiel, mixing choir and a solo piano that plays a secondary role. This work is also scored for a keyboard glockenspiel, the colour organ, part of which is a light-to-colour projection unit. This is a transcription of the work in Moscow in 1911, and conducted by Koussevitzky with Scriabin himself at the piano.

The structure of Prometheus, from the opening bars of the enigmatic chord (E-G sharp-B sharp-D sharp-F sharp), occupies the central place in the score, and the first theme, which forms is the very essence of the piece. A second theme for large orchestra builds both a grand and impressive climax: a hymn to Prometheus, at its height there occurs a magnificent heavenly-beautiful transfiguration. After several such climaxes, the final pages die away, but not until they have risen one last time.

This recording incorporates orchestrations made by Alexander Nemtin, taken from an unfinished work.

## Alexei Lubimov

Pianist Alexei Lubimov was born in Moscow in 1944. He studied piano at the Moscow Conservatory and at the Moscow Conservatory as one of the last students of the legendary Russian piano pedagogue, Heinrich Neuhaus. In his youth he became interested in musical circles close to the famous innovator, the remarkable theoretician, conductor and contemporary.

His interest in early keyboard works by Janáček and Terry Riley. In the following years, he was also interested in Bach and other 18th-century works. His interest was inspired by contemporary composers: Henry, and Ligeti. His interest was equally well played by works by Janáček, Bartók, Stravinsky and Prokofiev. It was only logical that Alexei Lubimov should become an outstanding performer of Bach on a harpsichord at Bach's famous dance-works in the former Soviet Union in the 1960s.

Lubimov's international career as a solo pianist began in 1968, and quickly took him to the great concert halls of Europe and Japan, where he has performed with many of the world's leading orchestras, including the Moscow, Leningrad and Czech Philharmonic Orchestras, the Israel and New Japan Philharmonic Orchestras, the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra, the Amsterdam and Rotterdam Philharmonics, the Vienna Symphony, the orchestra of the Beethovenhalle Bonn, the English Chamber Orchestra, the Bournemouth and London Sinfoniettas, the Northern Sinfonia, the Scottish Chamber Orchestra, the Stuttgarter Kammerorchester, the Collegium Musicum Zurich, Mark Aronoff, Roger Norrington, the English Baroque Soloists with John Eliot Gardiner, Mariss Jansons and Esa-Pekka Salonen.

He has appeared regularly with the Nash Ensemble, the Ensemble Schonberg, André Schaller, Christian Thielemann, Michael Gielen, Mstislav Rostropovich, Sir Neville Marriner, the London Philharmonic at the Salzburg Festival, La Roque de Anthea, Carnegie Hall, the London Proms, the Metropolitan Opera at the Gewandhaus, and the Schleswig-Holstein Musik Festival in Gelnhau.

## Jukka-Pekka Saraste

The 1996-97 season marks Jukka-Pekka Saraste's third year as Music Director of the Toronto Symphony Orchestra. Reviews of his performances have been overwhelmingly favourable and critical acclaim for his programming has been unanimous.

Saraste also serves as artistic adviser to the orchestra concerts at Roy Thomson Hall. Saraste is widely acknowledged as one of the leading conductors of his generation, noted for his musicianship of the TSO, and their artistic collaboration, setting a standard of artistic excellence in the orchestra.

Saraste has signed an exclusive recording contract with Finlandia Records. He has stated that he will record six discs with the Toronto Symphony Orchestra over the next three years. This contract -- the first multi-recording agreement by a Canadian orchestra in history -- also represents the first multi-recording agreement between Finlandia Records and a North American orchestra.

Saraste was born in Helsinki, Finland, in 1956. Before taking up the post with the TSO, Saraste served as the Chief Conductor of the Finnish Radio Symphony Orchestra from 1987 to 1990. He was also the first to sign a contract with the radio orchestra.

Since his debut in 1977, Saraste's conducting career has progressed steadily, building a reputation that has grown as one of the most gifted and sought-after conductors of his generation. He has held the position of Chief Conductor of the Finnish Radio Symphony Orchestra since 1994.

Saraste was active in international competition in December 1977. He has appeared with the Boston Symphony Orchestra, the London Philharmonic, and also worked with the Cleveland, Detroit and Minneapolis orchestras. In Scandinavia, Saraste has worked with the Swedish Radio Symphony Orchestra, the Royal Stockholm Philharmonic, the Swedish Radio Symphony, the Stockholm Philharmonic, Santa Cecilia Orchestra and the Paris Opera and the New York Philharmonic Orchestra.

Saraste lives in Toronto with his family. He announced in fall 1996 to extend his contract as Principal Conductor of the TSO until year 2000.
