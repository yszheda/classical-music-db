## Liner Notes by Harry Halbreich

### MESSIAEN: CHRONOCHROMIE · LA VILLE D'EN HAUT ET EXSPECTO RESURRECTIONEM MORTUORUM

In Messiaen's view – or certainly in his mind's eye – harmony was colour, and music was coloured time: hence the title of Chronochromie (1959-60), which was his first work commissioned by the state following his composition of the Turangalîla symphony (1946-48), and which came two years before his experiment with the limited durations.

"This time includes ways of imitating bird-song and ways of measuring duration..." For Messiaen, "syntholapist and rhythmician" as he called himself, time was more a flame than a flow – more like that of a bird, exists in the continuous present. Balance, states and repetition, then, occur far more than progress and development: and Chronochromie our is cast in balanced blocks rather than movements. There are seven of them, obeying a structural principle that is of the strophe-antistrophe-epode of choral odes in Greek drama, but with two strophe-antistrophe pairs doubled and the whole placed between an introduction and concluding coda.

The time frames of the strophes are created by permutations (systematically listed here of 32 different musical durations – from one demi-semiquaver (23rd note) to 32 to a whole (whole note) – marked out on three layers by percussionists with singing glasses. Against these phirrhythms gags, French birds call out on woodwind and tuned percussion instruments. Other French birds are heard in the opening parts of the antistrophe; the song thrush on continued woodwind, sounding "gay, alive, coloured", in alternation with the skylark on xylophone, marimba and glockenspiel over a background of bells, cymbals and gongs. Each of the six strophes closes with segments of chorale and of plain-chant (sung in the first instance by strings, in the second by Messiaen birds in attendance. The epoch consists of birdsong without the literalism of the opening: it is a tangle of 18 songs, again French, heard from safe settings.

Messiaen uses the full resources of his orchestra only in the introduction and coda, which are related as the two strophes or the two antistrophes are linked in that sameness is mixed with difference. At the start the woodwind section is augmented by two fixed birdcage, that of the Swedish cappy. They come from Messiaen's own collection and his tape archive of birdsong; they alternate with the precise birdsongs from woodwind and tuned percussion. The woodwind group includes three Japanese birds (the narcissus flycatcher and the bush warbler). Also in the whole orchestra there are huge blocks representing rocks, after which a mountain stream in perpetual motion and its echo; changing on the basset quotation from the composer's own accordion. There are, he said, "several circling movements", giving rise to solo violas and cellos: this is the moving mass that rolls and flows on. The little subsidiary waves of white foam and of sheaves of droplets are provided by harp-glissandi, glockenspiel, trills and tremolos and pizzicatos from the double basses. A trilled chord stationary on the violins and changing on the basses expresses the colour of the resonance against the rocks which, sometimes that resonance generates a powerful deep bass: this is the bassoon and the tuba with the bellum notes. "Ev returns from the composer's beloved mountains to the percussionists-played blocks of granite, and then the white-tailed eagle, again a Swedish bird, provides a coloured close. The music uncolours the seconds that end the first way.

Chronochromie imagines a natural world without people a world of birds, mountains and changelings. Et exspecto resurrectionem mortuorum (1964), which was Messiaen's next large-scale work, is concerned rather with the world to come. The instrumentation – for orchestral woodwind and brass with huge battery of percussion – was dictated by the commissioner, André Malraux, then French Minister of Cultural Affairs. Its first performance was given in the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris, with Messiaen himself conducting. "I was asked for an ideal environment, because of the work's special character, and this monument, the most complex with the diademest of blues, seemed to me to be perfect." This again, then, is coloured time: a coloured time whose white heat comes from the faith of Messiaen the Catholic in the final resurrection and the life of the world to come.

There are five sections, beginning with a prayer from the depths of which this monumental orchestral is capable. In section two there is the resurrection music: a six-note strain of lightning followed by a four-note chord repeated 35 times, representing the brass instruments that successively renewed in a melody from the third act of Parsifal ("Durch der Wunde Schlag"), by solo woodwinds, and a trumpet-led chorale: over-arched by bells in an ancient Italian rhythm. The third part, too, is threshold, representing the voice of Christ as that of an Ammonian bird on the full bellum note: he speaks in the bassoon and on the bells, and as a massive chord in crescendos. More complex is the fourth section, though again this is music in grand blocks. Triple strokes from tam-tams make the woodwind resemble, as a pattern of change ringing on peals, and the distant melody of the stars". Bells play the part again, in the double basses, woodwinds, and the song thrush. Other birds sound, including the song thrush (which Messiaen combines with the melody of the first part, and a sequence of chords prepares for the chorale of the last, "enormous, unanimous and thunderous" as Messiaen puts it.

La Ville d'en haut (1987), scored for a similar wind/percussion forces, turns to the sound colours of Paradise – of the heavenly city. The work has two parts. First there is a slow, almost motionless introduction by four solo trombones, then an alternation between the full wind with chord and bird music. The birds of paradise are here (golden oriole, starling, song thrush, blackbird), also the bird of the Resurrection – the hoopoe, and also the blackcap and wren. The second part opens with the song thrush (which Messiaen combines with the melody of the first part, and a sequence of chords prepares for the chorale of the last, "enormous, unanimous and thunderous" as Messiaen puts it.

## WHAT IS 4D AUDIO RECORDING?

**4D AUDIO RECORDING** is an overall concept in sound-recording from Deutsche Grammophon. It is based on the notion that the sound image obtained in a microphone signal should correspond as closely as possible to the listener's experience of the real-world music, allowing for a comprehensive, harmonious representation of music-flows using all available modern technical means.

**4D AUDIO RECORDING** makes use of technologies which, in this configuration, are unique in the recording industry. A number of the individual components were developed in cooperation with the technical universities of Göttingen, Munich and Berlin, and the German firm Sennheiser. Deutsche Grammophon's own Recording Centre, the 4D Audio Recording team incorporates all these technical innovations:

- **Remote-controlled microphone pre-amplifier**
- **21-bit digital floating converter-digital converter**
- **Pre-amplification of the recording signal**
- **4D digital mixing-Authorised Bit imaging**

**4D AUDIO RECORDING** (engineer digital mix) in a highly sophisticated set of monitoring rooms is an extremely high standard of the recording process. In Deutsche Grammophon, the technology was used for the first time in 1993 and has since become standard for all our recordings.

**4D AUDIO RECORDING** offers for the realisation of his or her idea the best ideal technical conditions for the realisation of his or her idea of a faithful, natural-sounding reproduction of the music and the musical substance of the performer who has his own technical processing of the sound controlled by the artist.

### THE 4 TECHNICAL DIMENSIONS

**Remote-controlled microphone pre-amplifier**
The use of a remote-controlled microphone pre-amplifier makes it possible to place the microphone amplifier which, with a minimum of noise and distortion, supplies the A/D converter with an analog signal from the microphone, as close as possible to the microphone. This is the only way to ensure that the signal path is as short as possible, thus ensuring the highest possible sound quality.

**21-bit digital floating analogue-digital converter**
The 21-bit floating A/D converter used by Deutsche Grammophon offers a dynamic range of over 110 dB. This represents a significant improvement over the conventional 16-bit converter, which has a dynamic range of about 96 dB. The 21-bit converter thus enables a much more accurate and detailed reproduction of the original sound.

**Pre-amplification of the recording signal**
The pre-amplification of the recording signal is an important step in the recording process. It ensures that the signal from the microphone is amplified to the optimal level for the A/D converter, thus ensuring the highest possible sound quality.

**4D AUDIO RECORDING** (engineer digital mix)
