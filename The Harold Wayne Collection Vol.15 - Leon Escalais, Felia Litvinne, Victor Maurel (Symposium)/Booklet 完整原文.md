## 内页说明

Dr. Wayne writes: The two most extraordinary items on this disc are, of course the Escalais unpublished titles. They were preserved in Turin by a descendent of one of the Milan recordists and both came to me in 1967.

Litvinne's Paris Fonotipias are rare, but not nearly so rare as the Cornuberts, Davids and Gailhards, not to mention the Kinens and Adinis. We are intending, incidentally, to produce the "Paris" set soon. Her Odeons of "Samson"/"Favorite" and "Cid/Africaine" were findable, but the "Trovatore" double sided disc, especially in this condition, is a major rarity. "Tristan" I never found and indeed neither did Robert Bauer.

Maurel's Fonotipia matrices were extensively reissued in the 30s. However, originals are a different case altogether. The rarest, in my experience, is 'A year ago' and the 'Au temps du grand roi'/'Rondel de l'adieu' coupling.

Symposium Records acknowledges gratefully the assistance of Dr. Paul Lewis with this production.

the French school of singing was typified by several unique characteristics. First and foremost was the great attention given to perfect diction; all the finest French singers paid particular attention to this. Great importance was also given to vocal agility, and while the trills and decorations of a Flancion are well known to most record collectors, even singers of lesser status, such as Belhomme, could match him in this respect.

Here we have examples by three of the most distinguished artists who excelled in this distinct vocal genre.

Leon Escalais was unique in some ways, most examples show him as an artist who could sing with daring virtuosity. He is, of course, able to sing the aria from Boito's "Mefistofele" with beautifully modulated tone and in all his work his diction is exemplary. It is difficult to understand why the two unpublished titles included here were never issued, for they show an artist and recitalist not always to be found in his published recordings.

Félia Litvinne was born in 1860 in St. Petersburg, her father being Russian and her mother a French-Canadian. She was brought up under the very strong French influence of her mother, and always regarded herself as being French at heart. She studied singing in Paris with Mme Marthe-Banderall and Pauline Viardot, and was essentially an exponent of the French school. Her voice was a dramatic soprano of great range and power, and she also possessed a coloratura technique unusual in such a large voice. P. G. Hurst, writing of Litvinne's London recitals in 1903, described her voice as no longer a soprano but more a mezzo-soprano and it is for that reason that she recorded 'Mon coeur s'ouvre à ta voix' and 'O mon Fernand'. He obviously had never heard her recordings of the two arias from 'Il Trovatore' made at the same time, which show her still at the height of her powers. Her unusually powerful register enabled her to sing many roles normally associated with lower voices but she was particularly well known for her 'Isolde' and continued to sing this role throughout her career. She was acclaimed as the best singer of Wagner since the great Mlle Falcon, who had a short but brilliant career in the 1830s.

Litvinne's admirer M. Vie et ami wrote: "Ceci fait toujours mouvoir l'âme. Ma voix si belle si chaude n'est presque plus. J'ai fait quelques disques très beaux. Je dis toujours en tremblant le 'Gramophone' 'Cecile Félita Litvinne'." (This always moves the soul. My voice so beautiful so warm is hardly any more. I have made some very fine discs, and I'm always trembling in saying the gramophone 'Here Miss Félita Litvinne'.) She taught singing in Paris after her retirement from the stage in 1910s, and among her many successful pupils were Germaine Lubin and Nina Koshetz.

Victor Maurel always presents a problem to record collectors. His discs are of the very greatest historical importance, but there is no denying that the voice by 1903, when he made his first records, shows very obvious signs of wear. Although he was the creator of the Iago in the first performances of Verdi's Otello and Falstaff, and was regarded as the greatest singing actor of his time, he taught singing and wrote books on vocal technique in which he advised that the voice should be placed 'en avant', and he gave many public lectures on the subject. His pupils included Edmond Clément, Enrico Caruso, and Mattia Battistini. Together with his daughter Blanche, he taught singing. Maurel himself was taught by the famous Duprez. Maurel's voice was well into old age, whilst Maurel's was very brilliant in his youth. Many of his contemporaries thought his voice was not particularly beautiful and could not compete with that of Jean Lassalle, his great rival at the Paris Opéra. He was nevertheless an extremely accomplished operatic artist and all of his records, even in some rather undistinguished sound, show him to be a remarkably intelligent and musical singer. It was surely with good reason that Verdi chose Maurel to create Iago and Falstaff, despite the availability of many other fine baritones. He was indeed a unique genius and a master of his art.

**Le Trouvère** - "O toi seul espoir" This is better known by its Italian title "Di quella pira". Here Escalais is in far better condition than in some of his later Odeon recordings. He displays his exciting high notes and the performance is certainly one of his best efforts. It is a pleasure to make these available.

**Othello** - "Tout m'abandonne" This extract from Otello includes the recitative and the aria, again better known as "Ora e per sempre addio". Maurel's diction is superb, and this is a good opportunity to demonstrate his ability in highly dramatic music.

**Polyeucte** - "Source délicieuse" The opera is one of Gounod's least known works and this gives a good opportunity to appreciate this piece. In fact it is by Gounod and was composed in 1878. Escalais sings with his usual verve and expressively, and with some lovely piano phrases which make it clear that he was more than just a strident operatic tenor.

**Le Trouvère** - "Supplice infame" This is of course the famous "Di quella pira" cabaletta. The safety of his mother and vows to avenge her. Escalais revels in this showpiece and his diction and the top G which is taken as written and is a must for any tenor who can deliver the goods, is splendidly given.

**Hérodiade** - "Adieu donc vains objets" Here John the Baptist is saying farewell to the vanities of the world and commends himself to the loving care of God. Why this record was published must remain a mystery. It is beautifully recorded and splendidly sung, with deep involvement by Escalais, superb diction, and singing high G notes.

**Mephistopheles** - "De ces champs, de ces bois" Here the aged Faust bids farewell to "the beauties of nature." The aria is perfectly phrased and beautifully sung. Escalais reveals his sensitive lyric tone and phrasing. Again I cannot understand why this title was never issued as the performance is most affecting.

**Cavalleria Rusticana** - "Voi lo sapete Mamma" Most of Litvinne's operatic recordings are sung in French but here she uses the original Italian. The singing is superb, her huge voice entirely under control and with trills that are beautifully controlled, but dramatically used. Her voice is unusually powerful and was recorded around 1905-6.

**Lohengrin** - "Le rêve d'Elsa" Elsa describes a dream in which a knight in shining armor appeared to her and vowed to be her champion. Litvinne's singing is deeply felt with long legato lines and a tone of lovely soft radiance. Normally I loathe Wagner sung in French but here it sounds perfectly natural.

**Aida** - "I sacri numi" This is the second part of the big Aida scene generally known as "Ritorna vincitor", but here given in French. Both her distress is described as she prays for victory and devotion to her father and her native country. She begs the gods to help her in this impossible situation and is sung with a variety of emotion entirely devoid of any tremolo.

**L'Africaine** - "Air du sommeil" In this aria the Indian queen, Sélika, nursed the sleeping Vasco da Gama with whom she is in love and listens rapturously to his singing. Her singing of this recitative is beautifully sung with an agility unusual in a dramatic soprano voice.

**Samson et Dalila** - "Mon coeur s'ouvre à ta voix" Litvinne sings as Dalila in this aria in which Delilah summons up all her seductive charms to seduce Samson and this is well sung, and there she sings a beautifully even middle and chest registers, but here she sounds rather uninvolved.

**La Favorite** - "O mon Fernand" In this aria Leonora expresses her deep feelings. She has been cast out by her kinsmen but is true to her young novice, Fernand. She is afraid that if she tells him of her past he will reject her, and so she sings this poignant aria of confession and plea for understanding.

**Le Trouvère** - "La nuit calme et serene" Any suggestion that Litvinne's voice had deepened before she made these Odeons is quickly dispelled on listening to the duet from Il Trovatore she recorded with Edmond Clément in 1905. Here she is still very much the coloratura soprano she always was. Leonora tells her companion Ines that she has fallen in love with the troubador she secretly loves.

**Carmen** - "Habanera" In a way this is a disappointment. Litvinne sings the music beautifully but one feels that she is an imperfect Sulite and therefore it would have been better with greater dignity. When she sings "Si tu m'aimes, prends garde à toi" you feel that she means it from her heart and mind, one feels that she is not quite the Carmen we know.

**Le Trouvère** - "Brise d'amour" This aria ("D'amor sull'ali rosee" in Italian) Leonora is waiting to meet her lover in his prison, as the friar to confess him in his cell. It is a superb version showing Litvinne at her very best, with beautiful control of phrasing, and the steady flow of lovely tone, make this one of the soprano's greatest contributions to the gramophone.

**L'Africaine** - "Sur mes genoux" This is almost identical with Number 10, so the choice of the version is difficult. If one is good both are good, possibly the voice is a little more forward in this version.

**Le Cid** - "Pleurez mes yeux" Here Chimène expresses her unhappiness. Her lover Rodrigo has killed her father in honor at the thought that she must renounce her lover. This again is a very fine version, and the recording here is vastly superior to that on the T discs of the same solo.

**Otello** - "Era la notte" Iago tells Otello that he was sleeping next to Cassio, who was talking in his sleep and addressing Desdemona with words of love. Here is Maurel as Iago the consummate villain of historical importance, since he was chosen by Verdi to create the role of Iago. Maurel tells the story with the greatest skill and his diction is exemplary. Although in full voice the tone is very undistinguished and he has a good command of the music. He voices the cruel words in a most effective manner. As an interpretation it is superb.

**Marechiare** (Tosti) It seems probable that a number of songs which Maurel recorded featured in his concert programmes. He maintains the rhythm well and the voice sounds too full and sonorous as recorded, and it is certainly not one of his best records.

**Don Giovanni** - "Serenata" Maurel was famous for his Don Giovanni, and I imagine he would have paid more attention to the words, and I imagine he would have paid more attention to an accompaniment in the Serenade. Here the pianist has to change tempi repeatedly and this is a curious thing. The result is a curiously uneven performance. It sounds very under-rehearsed.

**Le rondel de l'adieu** (de Lara) Maurel's immaculate diction and his wide experience of the lied enables him to bring out the best of this charming song which starts with the words, "Partir, c'est mourir un peu" (Parting is to die a little).

**Mandolinata** (Paladilhe) This is another pleasant trifle and may well have been another of his concert pieces. It shows a personality and light-hearted approach make this an attractive record.

**Ninon** (Tosti) This is my favourite among Maurel's non-operatic titles. The words are by Paul Verlaine and the song is most significant with meaning. The heart of the song is summed up in the final verse which ends "Ninon, when do you wake of life . . . you who have no love".

**Falstaff** - "Quand'ero Paggio" This is my favourite among Maurel's operatic recordings. It is in fact a short aria that he repeats twice, first in Italian and then in French, in response to the pleas of his master. Maurel creates a most convincing picture of the fat knight, and one is reminded again of the great artist he was.

**A year ago** (d'Hardelot) I suppose Maurel learned this rather maudlin ballad in English for his British and American audiences. It deserves more repeats. There are some nice touches in the accompaniment part. Maurel's English diction is very good and I am prepared to say that this is one of his most successful records.

**Au temps du grand roi** (Tosti) This is one of the records made about the same time as the previous record apply equally to this one, which is sung in French. Maurel was master of the material, which he transformed into something quite charming by his great art.

John Freestone
