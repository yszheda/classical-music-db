## COMMENTARY

**Cataloguing the compositions of Béla Bartók (1881-1945) is rather complicated.** Bartók himself began to number his compositions three times—always starting over again with Op. 1. The first two series of opus numbers cover compositions from his childhood and student years. He started to number for the third (and last) time with his professionally mature works after graduating from the Music Academy of Budapest in 1903. This continued only for the first twenty (or perhaps twenty-one) opus; however, before he stopped composing for good. There exist two catalogues of Bartók's music: one is an attempt to compile a unified chronological list of Bartók's works, of which the most reliable and most widely known is that of András Szöllösy (abbreviated Sz.).

**The two Rhapsodies for violin and piano** (arranged for violin and orchestra) were both written in 1928, although the second was thoroughly revised by the composer in 1944. Bartók dedicated these pieces to significant Hungarian violinists of the day, both of whom were also his close friends. The **Rhapsody No. 1 for Violin and Piano/Orchestra** was dedicated to József Szigeti (1892-1973), with whom Bartók often performed in concert as a pianist.

**The Rhapsody No. 2 for Violin and Piano/Orchestra** was dedicated to Zoltán Székely (1903-2001) who was a student of the celebrated Jenő Hubay (his father was the originator of the Budapest College of Music and later became Bartók's friend and regular playing partner). Both the rhapsodies have their roots in Hungarian folk music, specifically in the Csárdás form. Both have its typical two-part structure with its contrasting and accelerating slow-fast (lassú-friss) tempo scheme; both use a series of typical folk scales and harmonic and typical Bartók playing techniques. Both rhapsodies were conceived as showy, virtuosic concert numbers. Their popularity with both performers and audiences is so great that they have not only originated a large number of arrangements but also transcriptions for solo violin.

**The Concerto No. 2 for Violin and Orchestra** was long thought to be Bartók's only violin concerto, and it wasn't until the posthumous publication of the earlier violin concerto in 1956 that the proper numbering was revealed. Bartók wrote it in the years 1937-38, again for his friend Zoltán Székely, who premiered it in October 1939 in Amsterdam with the Concertgebouw Orchestra conducted by Bruno Walter. Varied in its tone: from the standpoint of expression, the concerto is extremely detailed, its music is reserved, rhythmic, constantly alternating between different styles and characters. The theme of the concerto is the work itself. The solo part is extremely virtuosic, the orchestra part is very rich and colorful, the musical material is very varied.

The work is in three movements, but the middle movement is a set of variations on a theme, and the finale uses material from the first movement. The first movement is in sonata form, but with many elements of variation technique. The second movement is a theme and variations. The finale is a sonata-rondo form.

**The Concerto for Violin and Orchestra No. 1** in 1907-08 at the time when he was only 26. The original title was "Tempo di Verbunkos," and although Bartók later changed it to the newer official "Allegro non troppo," its characteristic verbunkos rhythm of its principal theme remains. The work was first performed in Paris on May 14, 1958—it wasn't until 50 years after it was composed! Bartók accepted the score which Bartók had dedicated to her, but the work remained unplayed until 1958, when she gave its premiere at the Paris Conservatory. The premiere was received with enthusiasm, but it has remained one of the least frequently performed of Bartók's orchestral works.

**The Sonata for Violin and Piano, Op. Posth** is the first of Bartók's three sonatas for this instrumentation. It was written in 1903, towards the end of Bartók's years as a composition student at the College of Music and Ballet in Budapest. Like the other early chamber music by Bartók, the work is clearly influenced by the Brahmsian romantic idiom, and while the occasional folk tune is quoted, it does not achieve the synthesis of folk and classical elements that characterizes the mature Bartók style. The work is in three movements: I. Allegro moderato (molto rubato), II. Andante, III. Vivace.

**André Gertler (1907-1998)** occupies a special position among the ranks of his generation's leading violinists thanks to his marked preference for modern music. He was born in Budapest, embarked on his studies as a concert virtuoso at the age of thirteen, and completed his musical training at the age of seventeen, studying violin under Jenő Hubay and composition under Zoltán Kodály at the Liszt Academy of Music. He became closely acquainted with Béla Bartók, with whom he appeared in a series of joint concerts between 1925 and 1938, and premiered a number of Bartók's works including the European premiere of Bartók's Sonata for Solo Violin in London and Violin Concerto No. 2 in Paris. In 1928 he settled in Belgium, where he formed his own string quartet. He was also an active teacher, starting to teach at Brussels Conservatory in 1940 and, a few years later, at the Royal Academy of Music in London. In the 1950s and '60s he also became a regular performer at the Prague Spring Festival. At the same time he was an active recording artist who won numerous distinctions for his Supraphon recordings, e.g. the 1965 complete works of Bartók (featuring pianist Diane Andersen as well as Czech musicians) for which the project won the 1966 Grand Prix du Disque de l'Académie Charles Cros in Paris. Gertler's other distinctions in the Supraphon catalogue include his recordings of compositions by Darius Milhaud and George Enescu.

**44 Duets for Two Violins (Sz 98)** is a collection of miniatures primarily inspired by folk music. Bartók arranged them into four large groups. The collection as a whole presents a sort of symbolically conceived series of moods—the first group being almost exclusively joyful, the second is of a lyrical character, the third is of a more serious and melancholic nature, and the fourth is of a predominantly technical character. The duets were written in 1931, and were first published in 1933.

**The Sonata for Violin Solo (Sz 117, 1944)** was composed in 1944 for Yehudi Menuhin, who had been a close friend of Bartók's since the late 1930s. Menuhin had requested a work for solo violin, and Bartók, who was in failing health and living in the United States, composed this sonata between March 8 and April 26, 1944. The work is in four movements: I. Tempo di ciaccona, II. Fuga: Risoluto, non troppo vivo, III. Melodia: Adagio, IV. Presto.

**The Sonata for Violin and Piano No. 1 (Sz 75, 1921)** and **No. 2 (Sz 76, 1922)** represent Bartók's mature style in his chamber music for violin and piano. Both sonatas are characterized by their rhythmic vitality, harmonic richness, and the integration of folk music elements with modern compositional techniques.

**Contrasts for Violin, Clarinet and Piano (Sz 111, 1938)** was commissioned by Benny Goodman and Joseph Szigeti. The work is in three movements: I. Verbunkos (Recruiting Dance), II. Pihenő (Relaxation), III. Sebes (Rapid). The work explores the contrasting timbres and capabilities of the three instruments.

**Sonatina for Violin and Piano on Folk Themes from Transylvania** is an arrangement by André Gertler of Bartók's original piano sonatina (Sz 55, 1915). The work is based on Romanian folk melodies from Transylvania and is in three movements: I. Désparaszt (Dance from Dés), II. Mosorató (Bagpipe Dance), III. Finale: Allegro vivace.

**Hungarian Folksongs for Violin and Piano (Sz 42, 1908-09)** are arrangements of Hungarian folk songs. Bartók collected these melodies during his field research in Hungary and the surrounding regions.
