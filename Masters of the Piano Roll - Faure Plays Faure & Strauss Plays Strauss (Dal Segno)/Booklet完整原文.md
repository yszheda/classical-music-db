## Introduction

Listening to these rare recordings, to hear Stravinsky, Ravel, Debussy, Mahler, Horowitz and others play their own music with the touch, accents and subbtleties of long ago is not so much astounding but sometimes ghostly. All the works in *Masters of the Piano Roll* collection are original recordings by both composers and pianists.

You will be amazed at the quality and clarity of these performances which were recorded by the musicians for Ampico, Duo-Art and Welte Mignon reproducing pianos between 1904 and 1935. Not only for the historian, but for all lovers of music, *Masters of the Piano Roll* collection is an extraordinary experience. There is a richness of tone and immediacy that was never captured by early gramophone records. It should be an essential inclusion in the libraries of all who appreciate fine music.

*Masters of the Piano Roll* collection is rich in composer interpretations – that is, compositions played by the composers themselves. It is significant that, at the turn of the twentieth century, the greatest living composers of Russia, Hungary, Germany and France chose recording for the reproducing piano as their vehicle to reach future generations.

Each rendition is an original recording and was signed by the pianist to authenticate the quality of the recorded work. Many of these rare recordings are in this collection, with many of the composers being heard as their own interpreters in no other way.

This collection, which originates from Australian musicologist Denis Condon, is historically important for its' vast representation of music from the four great piano roll manufacturers, namely: Welte Mignon first marketed in 1904, Ampico in 1911, Duo-Art in 1914 and Hupfield's Triphonola in 1919. The recordings in this album were made by a device with felt covered aluminium 'fingers' which can read the paper rolls and when attached to any modern day piano, can reproduce the music from the original 'recording' (paper rolls). This enables us to have Debussy playing Debussy, Mahler playing Mahler etc. on a brand new piano and in a digital recording.

## Composer Biographies

### Richard Strauss (1864-1949)

Born the son of Franz Strauss who was an excellent horn player, Richard Strauss had his first piano lessons at the age of four and by the age of six, he had written his first composition which was a Christmas song.

In 1882 he studied philosophy and aesthetics at the University of Munich and by the time he graduated he had amassed a considerable body of works. In 1884 he was appointed assistant music director to the Meiningen Court Orchestra, a role he was to stay with him until the end of his life. While he was in Munich and established as the third conductor at Munich Opera, he met the singer Pauline de Ahna whom he soon married and wrote his first major success *Don Juan*.

Between 1891 and 1911 he composed many of his most famous tone poems and operas among them *Till Eulenspiegel*, *Also Sprach Zarathustra*, *Don Quixote*, *Ein Heldenleben*, *Salome* and *Der Rosenkavalier*. During this time many of these works were performed in London but were shocking to some who had been brought up in a world of Brahms and Wagner.

Over the years Strauss continued his career as Chief Conductor to the Berlin Philharmonic and Director of Vienna Opera where in 1935, as an objection to the Nazi regime and their interference with the arts, he resigned his position. He did however manage to continue to have his own works performed. The crowning moment of his musical career is considered by many to be the composition of *Four Last Songs* and at the time of his death in 1949 he had written a fifth but sadly he never lived to hear any of them performed.

Strauss made a mere handful of roll recordings, mainly for Welte Mignon. His performance of his own *Dance of the Seven Veils* leaves no doubt as to his accomplishment as a pianist.

### Gabriel Fauré (1845-1924)

Fauré was the sixth child born to a family living in Parmiers in south west France where his father was the village schoolmaster. He was put out to nurse until the age of four and missed the warmth and intimacy of family life. By the age of nine his obvious musical talent was noticed by Louis Niedermeyer who educated him at the Ecole Niedermeyer free of charge. There he befriended Saint-Saëns with whom he was to have a lifelong friendship.

In 1866 he took his first post as organist at Rennes and from 1871-1873 he became better established as a Parisian organist in his own church of Saint Honoré d'Eylau. During this time he also acted as assistant to both Widor at St. Sulpice and Saint-Saëns at the Madeleine where he was later appointed assistant organist and choirmaster.

As a composer the piano was Fauré's other principal instrument. Among his compositions were two piano quartets and solo works including impromptus, barcarolles and nocturnes. Perhaps his greatest influences were Schumann and Chopin although his style remained characteristically French, arguably the most so of all 19th century French composers.

His appointment as Musical Director at the Conservatoire in 1905 afforded him little time for composition and by 1910 his increasing deafness had become a serious handicap. After spending the latter years of his life in Annecy he returned to Paris where he died in 1924.

In this recording, Fauré plays his own arrangement of the *Pavane Op.60*. It is one of twenty-two rolls he recorded which consisted entirely of his own music except for Debussy's *Fileuse* from *Pelleas and Melisande*.

### Contemporaneous quotes

So convincing was the reproductive quality of this early method of recording (25 years before electronic recording was invented) that it inspired the following statements from these composers:

> "As the reproducing piano plays it, it seems to be that I am performing it myself."
> — Artur Rubinstein

> "The Ampico reproducing piano is superlatice in its capacity to preserve beautiful tone."
> — Sergei Rachmaninov

> "The reproducing piano reproduces the living soul of the artist and has not an equal"
> — Gustav Mahler

> "I am astounded by the reproducing piano and join all the admiration and praise for this instrument."
> — Edvard Grieg

And the press had the following to say:

> "Impossible to tell when [Einstein] was playing with his own hands and when by means of the reproducing piano!"
> — Los Angeles Times

> "A listener could not have told that the piano was not actually being played by hand. The reproducing piano gave a faithful repetition of all the pianist's moods and phrasing!"
> — New York Tribune
