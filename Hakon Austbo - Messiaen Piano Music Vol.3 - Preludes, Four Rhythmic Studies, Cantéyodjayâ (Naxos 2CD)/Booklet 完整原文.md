### Notes on the Music

**Olivier Messiaen**

Olivier Messiaen is among the most influential figures in the music of the twentieth century. As first alarming and shocking audiences, he later won an unassailable position, respected at home in France and admired for his achievements internationally. Messiaen is a composer of intensely personal, emotional and inspired by a deep Catholic philosophy. He entered the Paris Conservatoire at 13 and ten years later entered the organ class of Marcel Dupré. Commissions came from the French government and in 1931 he was appointed organist at La Trinité in Paris, a position he held for the next 40 years. He was to become the most influential French composer in the second half of the 20th century, demonstrating his brilliant use of the orchestra in one of his great masterpieces, the *Turangalîla-Symphonie*, and adding to the great tradition of French organ music. His innovative works for the piano, with their vast range of fresh and new sonorities, have become part of the regular recital repertoire, setting a challenge for the virtuosity of the performer.

As a composer his attention was now turned to the organ. He had written, particularly in 1930, in a number of styles, and in 1935 he wrote his first work for organ. In 1940, as a prisoner-of-war in Silesia, he wrote the *Quatuor pour la fin du Temps* (*Quartet for the End of Time*), returning, on his release in 1942, to the Conservatoire, where he taught harmony, and then, from 1947, music analysis. He had a strong influence on the following years through teaching and composing. There followed works such as *Visions de l'Amen* and *Vingt regards sur l'enfant Jésus*.

**Préludes**

Messiaen's very personal musical language was derived from a number of sources. His interest in bird-song is first discernible in the *Quatuor pour la fin du Temps* (*Quartet for the End of Time*) and *Cantéyodjayâ* (Catalogue of Birds). The eight *Préludes* were published in 1929, when Messiaen was still a student at the Paris Conservatoire. Like the title-sometimes suggested Debussy, the eight *Préludes* suggest different poetic images. *La colombe* (The Dove), is evocative in its opening section of cooing of a dove, followed by a more agitated central section, with a peaceful ending. The second piece, *Chant d'extase dans une paysage triste* (Song of Ecstasy in a Sad Landscape) is similarly character in mood, the theme of the opening section returns in a final coda. *Le nombre léger* (The Light Number) is characterised by the lightness of its touch, with a short introduction framing a choral-like section which is given in two-part counterpoint. *Instants défunts* (Dead Instants), is more meditative in mood, framing in turn a central section that presents its material in a series of short motifs. *Les sons impalpables du rêve* (The Impalpable Sounds of the Dream) is similarly fragmentary in material returns, again framing the material of the opening section, with a final return to the mood of the introduction. The use of canon in the final section of *Le nombre léger* is given its material in the upper register. The piece ends with a coda. *Les sons impalpables du rêve* (The Impalpable Sounds of the Dream) is notable for its chant-like repetitions. **Four Rhythmic Studies**

The next stage in Messiaen's development is represented by the four *Rhythmic Studies*, published in 1950. The first, *Île de feu I* (Isle of Fire I), is dedicated to the French actress and singer Yvonne Le Bouthier. The piece is a toccata-like study in semiquavers, with a dynamic rhythm, followed by a tenderly evocative passage. The material develops to a climax, with an intervening section that returns to the opening phrase. The second study, *Mode de valeurs et d'intensités* (Mode of Durations and Intensities), is an essay in total serialism, with a theme in form. It is followed by *Un reflet dans le vent* (A Reflection in the Wind), a piece of great beauty, whose structure, perhaps obscured by the illustrative title, is based on a series of variations.

**Cantéyodjayâ**

*Cantéyodjayâ* takes its title from the not-yet-existent city of the same name, an imaginary Indian town framed here as a recurrent refrain. In the first group of refrains the rhythm is gradually expanded while in the second section makes use of on durations in a series of patterns which are played *sans ralentir* (without slowing down). The work is based on a series of rhythmic patterns which are determined by durations and intensities, to be augmented by addition or diminished by subtraction.

Successive 'interventions' are played simultaneously in a complex texture with interweaving material. The piece includes a *coda* in which the material is gradually expanded.

*Cantéyodjayâ* is written in Hindu rhythms, relying on the principles of *addition* and *diminution* found in the *Sangîtaranmakara* of Sarhgadeva. ### About the Artist

**Håkon Austbø**

Of Norwegian origin, the pianist Håkon Austbø performed extensively in his own country before continuing his studies at the Paris Conservatoire and the Ecole Normale de Musique. In 1970 he won the Concours National de la Guilde Française des Artistes Solistes and in 1971 drew international attention with the unanimous award of first prize at the Olivier Messiaen Competition for Contemporary Music in Royan. He furthered his studies at the New York Juilliard School, the Munich Staatliche Hochschule für Musik and in London. He won awards in Munich in 1974 and at the Paris Ravel Competition in 1975 and in the same year as member of the Trio du Nord at the UNESCO Rostrum in Bratislava. Håkon Austbø is at present based in the Netherlands and enjoys an active concert career throughout Europe and in North America.
