## RENATA TEBALDI (1922-2004)
*By George Hall*

During the decades of the 1950s and 1960s, two sopranos in particular commanded the attention of international opera audiences, especially in the Italian repertoire. One of them was the Greek-American Maria Callas, and the other her Italian contemporary, Renata Tebaldi.

It would be possible to make much of the rivalry between the two, which was endlessly recycled by the media and by partisan fans — though not without occasional interventions and ripostes from the two sopranos themselves. But in fact their repertoires, though containing some common, centric items, were as dissimilar as their voices and their artistic personalities. Both enjoyed outstanding careers and both left substantial recorded legacies that continue to be enjoyed and admired. And from a historical perspective both made a significant contribution to the world of opera of their time.

Renata Tebaldi was born in the Italian seaside town of Pesaro, in the Marche region of Italy, on 1 February 1922. Her father was a cellist, but her parents had already separated when she was born and a later attempt at reconciliation proved short lived. She was essentially brought up by her mother, Giuseppina, whose death in 1957 was a severe blow. Unlike Callas, Tebaldi otherwise succeeded in keeping her private life private, concentrating on her art and career. But the warmth felt for her by her numerous fans — especially during the New York years — was a sincere response to a stage personality that apparently had a naturalness and a genuine warmth.

Tebaldi played the piano as a child but was more interested in her voice. Her introduction to the formerly well-known soprano Carmen Melis, herself a verismo specialist, led to studies at the Pesaro Conservatory. Here she developed a technique that would stand her in good stead over the succeeding decades — though, like many singers, she went through a couple of periods of vocal crisis later in her career that would cause her to pause and retrain her instrument. Melis arranged her debut in the relatively small role of Elena (Helen of Troy) in Boito's *Mefistofele* in Rovigo in 1944, after which Tebaldi withdrew for a period of further study.

Her breakthrough came with the re-opening of La Scala, Milan — a victim of wartime bombing — in 1946, a prestigious event conducted by Arturo Toscanini. After a successful audition Tebaldi was selected as a soloist in the Prayer from Rossini's *Mose in Egitto* in which she joined the veteran bass Tancredi Pasero and the well-established tenor Giovanni Malipiero. The timing was perfect. As the Italian opera houses gradually got back into gear in the aftermath of the war, such a high-profile appearance, to be followed by others at the same theatre, put Tebaldi into a prime position, along with a handful of other singers, to slot into place as a member of a new and much-heralded generation of singers.

She started to sing widely in Italy in a broad repertory, some of which she soon relinquished (though the odd valuable trace of it remains on these discs). Wagner's *Lohengrin* and *Die Meistersinger* (at La Scala), as well as *Tannhäuser* (at the San Carlo in Naples), were all operas she sang on stage, in Italian, in these early years. So were such diverse pieces as Handel's *Giulio Cesare*, Spontini's *Fernand Cortez*, Rossini's *L'assedio di Corinto* and Verdi's *Giovanna d'Arco*. These would all be given up as she steadily began to select and focus on her core repertoire. Unlike Callas's specialisation on the bel canto operas, which she approached in a unique manner, Tebaldi must have sensed that her individual strengths of line and tone were better deployed in the lyric repertory, and particularly in its subsection (or expansion) as a fully-fledged lirico spinto soprano.

Tebaldi's chosen field became, and remained, centred on Verdi, especially on the later, more complex roles of Leonora in *La forza del destino*, *Aida*, Desdemona and Alice Ford, plus the bulk of the Puccini roles — notably *Manon Lescaut*, Mimi, Tosca, Butterfly and later Minnie in *La fanciulla del West*. To these she added Ponchielli's vocally diverse (but still essentially — if not archetypally — lirico spinto) *La Gioconda*, and some of the more popular works of Puccini's lesser contemporaries, notably Catalani's *La Wally*, Maddalena in Giordano's *Andrea Chénier* and the title role in his *Fedora*, and — a personal favourite — the role of the French tragedienne in Cilea's *Adriana Lecouvreur*. Both vocally and temperamentally these suited Tebaldi very well, earning her a long-standing position as a leading soprano at La Scala during the 1950s. (Ironically, she eventually relinquished that stage to the burgeoning phenomenon of Callas at a point when her rival was not far away from the beginnings of her vocal decline.) Later she established herself at the Met in New York before another audience that expected and demanded the best. That she was so successful for so long at both these glamorous venues is a testament to her vocal and artistic command, and equally to her ability to engage with an audience.

As with Callas at EMI, Tebaldi was also fortunate in her record company, Decca, which recorded the bulk of her stage roles plus a selection of other items, including, notably, a late collection of songs with Joan Sutherland's husband, Richard Bonynge, at the piano in a repertoire that he knew extremely well.

After her brief but momentous entry to La Scala under Toscanini in May 1946, she returned in *Mefistofele* (singing both Margherita and Elena) and in *Lohengrin* in the summer season given by the company at Milan's Palazzo dello Sport. Thereafter, Milanese audiences heard her in *La Bohème* and *Die Meistersinger* in 1946-47; *Faust, Otello* and *Andrea Chénier* in 1948-49; and in *Aida* the following season in a run of performances she shared with Callas. Further new operas at La Scala would include *La traviata* and *Falstaff* in 1950-51; *Tosca* and *Adriana Lecouvreur* in 1952-53; *La Wally* and *Eugene Onegin* in 1953-54; and *La forza* in 1954-55. The San Carlo was her second Italian home in these years, and audiences there also heard her in Refice's *Cecilia* (1952-53) and *Le nozze di Figaro* (1953-54). Other items that appeared fleetingly in her repertoire were Donna Elvira in *Don Giovanni*, Suzel in Mascagni's *L'amico Fritz*, the title role in Spontini's *Olympia*, and — an entirely forgotten work — *Salammbò* by the futurist turned-traditionalist, Franco Casavola (1891-1955).

Her appearances at the Met — where she sang regularly from 1955 to 1973, with a couple of small breaks — demonstrate the command she held over her core repertory in those years (though she also made appearances with other American companies — notably those of Chicago and San Francisco, returning at this period more rarely to Italy, but notably to the San Carlo). In her years as a leading Met soprano Tebaldi sang forty-five performances as Tosca, thirty-six as Mimi, thirty-two as *La Gioconda*, and twenty-five as both Desdemona in *Otello* and Adriana Lecouvreur. Going further down the list we find *La traviata* (twenty-one), Maddalena in Chénier (twenty), Puccini's Manon (thirteen) and Verdi's Amelia in *Simon Boccanegra* (eleven), with single figures for such roles as Butterfly (eight), Alice Ford (seven), Leonora in *Forza* and Minnie (both five), and a surprisingly low total for *Aida* (four). These roles, totalling 267 Met appearances in all over seventeen seasons, amount in themselves to a major operatic career, and define Tebaldi's impact and artistic essence in the years of her greatest fame.

Unlike many operatic artists, Tebaldi chose a good time to relinquish her stage career, and she did not sing in concert for very long afterwards. After all, she had had an ample innings and could look back to significant successes and very few failures. If her temperament was not as fiery as that of Callas, then that too paid its dividends in the consistency and durability of her vocalism in a strenuous repertoire that she presented to the highest standards. Yet there was an essential simplicity and sincerity common, by all accounts, both to Tebaldi the woman and to Tebaldi the artist that found their way, again and again, to the hearts of the public. She expressed it eloquently in an interview with Elizabeth Forbes of *Opera* magazine in 1991:

> I don't have any kind of cattivi pensieri, any bad thoughts, only wonderful memories. My soul was so near to my singing, it was really an enjoyment for myself ... The most wonderful thing you can do is to give to other people the gifts you receive from God ... When you are able to give something you receive it back many times.

Renata Tebaldi died at her home in San Marino on 19 December 2004 at the age of eighty-two. What remains of her now, artistically, are her audiences' memories, a lot of documentation, and — most importantly — recordings, of which, fortunately, there are many.
