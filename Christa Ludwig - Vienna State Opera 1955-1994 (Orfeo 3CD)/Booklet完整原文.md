## 第七页 / Text continued (HIPPO_20241217_0007.jpg)

Among Christa Ludwig's most frequently performed roles was Octavian in *Der Rosenkavalier*. "I never liked playing the part of this playboy," she later recalled, "as he understands absolutely nothing of what the Marschallin is saying to him. From start to finish I didn't enjoy singing the part either as he has only a few beautiful phrases but suddenly has to hit high notes without any preparation. In a role like this, you have to forget your femininity, and for me that was impossible. I hated trouser roles in general!"

And yet we recall very well how convincing Christa Ludwig was in such roles. Her Cherubino had adolescent desires, her Octavian the charm of an irresistible lover, her Composer in *Ariadne auf Naxos* the delightful defiance of the rebellious artist. Can singers have such a low opinion of the impression that they leave? Can aversions to certain stage characters be so easily concealed? When Christa Ludwig claimed that "singers have no character", she was referring to the need to adapt to the individual character of the role that she was playing and ideally embody that part as though it were a second self. After all, audiences demand a perfect illusion when the curtain rises. They want to see and hear if the actor onstage can achieve a good disguise. At the very least they demand good acting. Ideally they are given the illusion of an identity that has indeed been transformed beyond recognition.

## 第八页 / Text continued (HIPPO_20241217_0008.jpg)

Time and again Christa Ludwig achieved this aim. Not as Carmen ("I'm personally the exact opposite of Carmen, but it's a role I wanted to sing") or as Rosina in Rossini's *Il barbiere di Siviglia*, for playfulness was not her forte. Like every true singing actor, she was at her most powerful whenever the thoughts, actions and emotions of the individual whom she was portraying did not have to be merely theatrical, but she was able to recreate them and perhaps even feel them on the strength of her own emotions. Among the characters that immediately come to mind in this context are Beethoven's Leonore (which she sang only seven times in Vienna, but always unforgettable), the Marschallin, Claire Zachanassian in Gottfried von Einem's *Der Besuch der alten Dame*, Dorabella, Berg's Marie, Clairon in *Capriccio* and Wagner's Kundry. From this point of view I have to admit that Ludwig was right: although I can still recall her in trouser roles, it is the great female characters that she played that have tended to overshadow them. I also recall the wit of her Mistress Quickly in *Falstaff* and, of course, the role that caused her a vocal crisis but which none the less revealed one of the most human of all her characters, Eboli in Verdi's *Don Carlo*.

The singer has made no secret of the fact that she has had something of a love affair with the Vienna State Opera. "In 1955 it was the first real major opera house in my life. And when I appeared on the stage of the reopened house for the first time as Octavian in the December of that year, I immediately fell in love with it and had the feeling that I could sing well there."

The feeling was not deceptive, and it was shared by the audience: Christa Ludwig sang well and was loved.
Karl Löbl

## 第十页 / Biographical note (HIPPO_20241217_0010.jpg)

*And she was loved*

"The profession of a singer is no life," Christa Ludwig insisted in the course of a television interview in 1994, after she had retired from the stage. "It is a profession that requires enormous discipline because you have to renounce everything you could otherwise be doing. You're never alone. True, I cannot imagine a life without music, but I don't want to sing another note."

In her farewell concerts, Christa Ludwig invariably amused her audiences with a song by Leonard Bernstein: "I hate music, but I like to sing". In her own case, this should presumably have been: "I hate singing, but I like music." "Once you start to brood on whether the profession of a singer is worth leading this non-life for, then it's time to stop." This is an understandable, albeit extreme, standpoint. Fortunately for all opera- and concert-goers, Christa Ludwig adopted such a position only at a very late date.

Christa Ludwig was born in Berlin on 16 March 1928. Her father was both a singer and a theatre administrator, while her mother, too, was a singer. In short, she was a child of the theatre with a propensity for a life on the stage. She was only eighteen when she made her stage début as Prince Orlofsky in Die Fledermaus. Her initial engagement in Frankfurt, where she remained until 1952, was followed by others in Darmstadt and Hanover before Karl Böhm invited her to join the Vienna State Opera.

"Böhm was looking for a young Elisabeth Hongen and had evidently heard good things about me," Ludwig recalls. "But I felt that I was too young and inexperienced to survive in Vienna, which even then was regarded as a hotbed of intrigues. Also, I was engaged to a doctor and was even thinking of giving up singing in spite of this, and anyway I auditioned for Böhm and did so, moreover, no fewer than three times, in Frankfurt, Berlin and Salzburg. On the third occasion Böhm succeeded in making her feel less nervous with his now famous remark, "Dear child, why are you so worked up? You've already been taken on!" Her engagement and her wish to have children were now a thing of the past: the future had begun.

By the end of her first season in Vienna, then, Christa Ludwig was fully integrated into the company. For the next four decades the Vienna State Opera remained her home. Here she sang all her great roles, even if Vienna's opera lovers often had to manage without her while she honoured her commitments to the opera houses in Berlin, Paris and the New York Met, while increasingly finding time for concert work, especially lied. Evidence of her wide-ranging activities fills innumerable recordings, which, taken together, constitute an impressive discography of mainly complete studio recordings that preserve the beauty and individuality of her voice as well as the artistic care and authority that Christa Ludwig brought to all that she did.

The present live recordings from the Vienna State Opera are designed to complement Christa Ludwig's existing discography in several important ways. Drawn from the archives of both the Vienna State Opera and Austrian Radio, they have not been edited or touched up in any other way but document the work of a great stage artist who always wanted to do more than just sing, even though she repeatedly stresses in her reminiscences that her mother, a leading singing teacher, always insisted on vocal discipline and caution. But this made no difference, quite the opposite, in fact: it was this that persuaded Chris- ta Ludwig to go to the very limits of her potential in roles that represent-ing a supreme challenge to her acting skills and stage temperament. When Christa Ludwig made her Vi-ennese début in 1955 at the age of twenty-seven, she had already spent more than half her life in the thea-tre. As the daughter of an op-era-house administrator, she had of-ten watched from the wings, before taking up her first professional en-gagement at the age of only seven-teen. In short, she had an experi-ence of the stage that many older singers could only have envied. Not only had she sung countless small- and medium-sized roles in opera and operetta but had even essayed a number of more demanding parts, including Octavian in *Der Rosenka-valier*, Eboli in Verdi's *Don Carlo* and Bizet's *Carmen*.

She auditioned for the Vienna State Opera because the company's then director, Karl Böhm, was looking for a "young Elisabeth Hön-gen" for the rebuilt house on the Ringstraße that was to reopen in the au-tumn of 1955. This was no small chal-lenge, as Elisabeth Höngen was inter-nationally celebrated as one of the most fascinating actresses of her generation in all the major dra-matic mezzo roles from Gluck to Ri-chard Strauss, taking in Wagner, Verdi and Bizet along the way.

Böhm was, of course, astute enough to introduce the young Christa Lud-wig to Vienna's *Figaro* ensemble as Cherubino, only casting her as Octa-vian in December 1955 after she had already proved a successful Com-poser in *Ariadne auf Naxos*, which he conducted at the 1955 Salzburg Fes-tival. Even here, of course, the young singer had to contend with competi-tion from other colleagues, as these roles were regarded as the preserve of Sena Jurinac and Irmgard Seefried in Vienna. But Christa Ludwig fought shy of none of these challenges. She knew what her voice was capa-ble of. And her stage temperament and self-confidence were impres-sive. During her first season in the new house, all of which was planned by Böhm, she sang not only Dorabella but also - within the same week in February 1956 - Brangäne in *Tristan und Isolde* and Amneris in *Aida*, fol-lowing this up with Eboli in March. At the same time she was also singing a number of smaller parts ranging from the Second Lady in *Die Zauberflöte* to the Third Maid in *Elektra*.

By the end of her first season in Vien-na, then, Christa Ludwig was fully in-tegrated into the company. For the next four decades the Vienna State Opera remained her home. Here she sang all her great roles, even if Vien-na's opera lovers often had to man-age without her while she honoured her commitments to the opera hous-es in Berlin, Paris and the New York Met, while increasingly finding time for concert work, especially lied.
