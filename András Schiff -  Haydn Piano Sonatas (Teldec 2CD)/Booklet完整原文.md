## Haydn's Piano Sonatas
### A Short History of Their Reception and Interpretation

**András Schiff**

*(Page 5)*

HAYDN is, together with Schumann, probably the most unjustly neglected of all the great Classical composers. Some might argue with this statement and point out that Haydn was widely performed and that he has long been recognised as one of the great masters. This is true, but the problem lies, on the one hand, in the frequency of those performances and, on the other, in their quality. Haydn has not become one of the "popes" of classical music; it degrades him to a mere "Papa Haydn" and to one of the hackneyed clichés in musical life. He disappears beneath the shadow of his far more famous brother, among his symphonies it is the "Surprise", the "London" and the "Military" that are best known; of his chamber music the "Emperor" Quartet, Op. 76 No. 3, and the "Lark" Quartet, Op. 77 No. 1, are played regularly. Still, most musicians regard him first and foremost as a composer of symphony and of the string quartet to the detriment of everything else.

This is unfair, not least with the piano works. True, most piano students play Haydn's sonatas and they are an essential part of the piano literature. But is there enthusiasm in piano concerts? Do the pianists play the "tremendous little" Haydn sonatas at the beginning of the programme, to warm up the hands for Beethoven or for the "real business" of the modern keyboard repertoire? There is nothing wrong with this because they cannot, and should not, be compared to Schubert or Beethoven in presenting a single work in this manner: a pianist should perform them in the correct context. Of the 62 sonatas, 21–27 have been lost! One can count at least two dozen masterpieces and several others of the first rank.

The F minor Variations (Hob. XVII:6), the G major Variations (Hob. XVII:2), and the E-flat major Variations (Hob. XVII:3) are amazing works. Of these, only Hob. XVII:6 is well known in concert halls, but many people play or know the piano trios, for example, the one in F-sharp minor (Hob. XV:26), which has been beautifully recorded by Grubert and Claudio Arrau, among many others.

Music moves forward repeatedly, and in the most varied directions. The main reason for this is not simply technical progress but rather the fact that the interpretation of the major pianists of the 19th century is totally different from the performance practices of today, which have completely vanished. Who would dare to call Schumann and Clara Schumann's playing of them?

*(Caption under portrait: Joseph Haydn, oil painting by Thomas Hardy (Foundling-Hospital, London). Reproduction: The Kasel and Gerlach (Fortuna))*

*(Pages 6–7)*

Beethoven. Bach/Beethoven? The same author writes: "...it is astonishing how little that work has to do with the highest order." Arthur Schopenhauer, who studied under both the Parisian pianist and the Weimar Kapellmeister, never, to my knowledge, mentions a sonata by Haydn. Why not? ...It was the fashion to scoff at Haydn. ...True, the old noble-born and ennobled amateurs had their say. But they were not among the champions of regularly programmed, performed and recorded music. Not even the grandest prince is part of the general public. It is a matter of a few dozen or a few hundred people at most. And their understanding of Haydn is largely synonymous with their understanding of his symphonies and string quartets. The operas, masses and keyboard works are very remote from their experience and interests. Perhaps they are not even intrigued.

...such as can be appreciated only by similarly trained listeners.

Haydn was not a virtuoso pianist, but he was much more at home on the keyboard than any of his famous Viennese contemporaries — a simple evidence of intuition or brilliance in the service of music. Yet what can the listener and later, in the nineteenth century, the concert-goer actually know of his music? Even an avid concert-goer, who attended perhaps 150 concerts during the period from the 1750s and 1760s would have heard perhaps 24 sonatas by that time. The "Verzeichniss" (1766, "Hob. XVI" and a list, dated 1771 of "4771") clearly indicates that the sonatas were composed at different times and in different places. In Vienna alone there are still twelve locations where he is known to have worked. Some of his time was spent at the Esterházy court. For today's ears a further problem arises: the sonatas were written on a different instrument. Luckily we do not like to play them exactly as he did, but rather on a Walter or Stein, for the very good reason that this is what he would have chosen, had he been given a choice.

The first movement of the sonata's first movement resembles very much that of C. P. E. Bach's sonata, Wq. 54, No. 6 (Allegro assai), which Haydn knew from the 'Klavierbuch für Damen' of 1759. The form is almost identical (five-bar theme, parts 1 and 2), although Haydn's second theme is far more inventive, and, especially as regards its harmonic and melodic makeup, somewhat reminiscent of a Haydn minuet. There is not a trace of the later, famous 'Sturm und Drang' movement, although this was only a little later in its development. Sonata No. 32 in G minor: 55:11. Haydn is more inventive here, both in motivic development and thematic transformation. He also experiments: theme 1, bar 16 appears as a diminished seventh from C major to B flat major. The second movement in minuet-and-trio structure (No. 32 in G minor is in 3/4 time; Sonata No. 41 in C major is in 2/4). The minuet is in a simple AB structure but with a development that goes beyond all known models: for its time it is truly revolutionary.

The best modern editions are the Henle editions and András Schiff's Selected Keyboard Sonatas (London: Associated Board of the Royal Schools of Music, 1992). These contain all the important commentaries one would need.

Schiff is at pains to stress that they often provide information that is essential to the character of the piece. There is even a passage that begins with an accent mark, and this is certainly not a mistake: the accentuation of these notes has to be clearly delineated. Other sonatas also show the 'Empfindsamkeit' or 'sensitive style' of the galant, with elaborate embellishments, dynamic markings, and articulations (e.g. markings such as accents, pauses and fermatas), which need to be clearly observed if one is to observe Haydn's musical intentions and arrive at the ideal he was after. This is why Haydn's instructions as to tempo and expression must be considered with the greatest care, not only in what they say, but also in what they do not say, since they were intended for players of a certain caliber.

The performer is simultaneously in a difficult and enviable position: difficult, because the works are scarcely marked and only a few early prints of the sonatas exist. Enviable, because many questions remain to be answered and are left open to be explored under certain limitations, the opportunities are enormous.

The text of a good edition is important, and I would consider the Wiener (edited by Christa Landon, to be only the 'Missa brevis Sancti Joannis de Deo'. Embellishments are really important. Sometimes they are written in by the composer himself; at other times they are left to the good taste of the performer. The reduction of notes is no guarantee of greater faithfulness to the composer's intentions.

C. P. E. Bach's remarks are often observed — or they would be, had they not been so often ignored. There are very few editions that observe them carefully. Haydn himself occasionally contradicts them, though he clearly states that they should not be applied in slow movements.

There is nothing inherently wrong in looking back to the past in order to understand the present and to point towards the future. Thus E. T. A. Hoffmann was right in his appreciation of Haydn's genius and his greatness understanding of the art of fugue, for example.

...by contrast reveals a wonderful, many-faceted, constantly surprising entity, a living being that speaks to its listeners in many different ways. Both artists are a product of their times, and it is the character of their eras that imparts a certain character or quality to their music. And we, as listeners, have to perceive this as well: 'nothing that is really good can remain unknown forever', as András Schiff puts it. His playing on this CD is a perfect illustration.

Sheer beauty is the beginning and the end of all music and Haydn has reached it to perfection. His unexpected surprises are all the more dramatic, since they come at completely or partly expected moments.

Haydn's Sonatas have everything that one could wish for from the beginnings of the keyboard literature. Their popularity continues towards the future.

**Misha Donat**

By and large, appreciation lay not in the hands of performers. Pianists should not only open their concert series with them, but also place them in the middle or at the end of their programmes.

Haydn is one of those composers who can fill a child's heart with joy. In 1966 at the Wigmore Hall in London, my musical friends and I gave a whole concert of his solo keyboard works in front of a packed audience of music-lovers, fine, intelligent and discerning people. It was probably the most delightful concert of my life.

Unlike Mozart and Beethoven, Haydn was not a prodigy. He never touched a keyboard instrument at an early age, and from about the age of five until he was sixteen, it seems, he had to undertake a serious study of the clarinet and the violin. There is no doubt that during his long period at Kapellmeister to the Esterházy family he never expected to give uninterrupted performances from the harpsichord, and as time went on he wrote for it less and less, until finally in the finale of the "London" Symphony No. 96, his only attempt at a solo concerto for the instrument, he let Beethoven take over. The latter later described Haydn's playing in his reminiscences:

A performance on the piano-forte, although the instrument was still imperfect, at that time, had, upon that instrument, been uniquely read and true to the spirit of art. He always played with so much spirit and attractive brilliancy, which he has given also to his quartettes and quintettes for wind instruments. Beethoven remembers him to have excelled with especial grace in his slower compositions.

Haydn's keyboard sonatas have often been under-rated. It is true that viewed as a whole, they do not display the same kind of development as, say, his mature string quartets; nor can they all be recommended as examples of fine writing for the keyboard. Invariably to be reserved for his piano trios. Let there be no doubt that, just as he did in the realms of the symphony and the string quartet, so in the piano sonata he transformed the Classical piano sonata into the level of a form which was almost as fully developed as it was that only rarely equalled by Mozart, and in none of the other sonatas was the form in the closing years of the 18th century. In 1794, the year of the "London" Sonatas, Op. 2, just a year after Haydn's great series of string quartets, Op. 71 and 74 — he acknowledged it by bearing a dedication to the elder Czerny.

Haydn's sonatas were written for domestic performance, and as such they must often have been remarkable pianists — in particular, Therese Jansen, later known as Mme Bartolozzi. She was not only one of the greatest among Haydn's pupils, but also Ignaz Pleyel and Mme Cannabich. Other outstanding performers were two sisters, each of them called Therese — Katharina and Marianne (Marianne) von Genzinger, and Nanette Streicher, who were both of them, and in particular the Genzinger sisters, were thoroughly musical. As for Clementi, he was a man who could not even bear to hear his sonatas performed by any other hand; yet he gave permission to his London rival to take all his works for the English market, thereby assuring himself of a considerable income. In a letter to his London agent from 1791, he even refers to Haydn as "the best composer at present in the English capital" and advises him not to let him go without a fight; the following summer's tour to Salzburg and Vienna, "which will not take long", and the fact that he has nothing in hand at the moment, make it appear possible.

There are a lot of good things in the great G minor Sonata Hob. XVI:44 (1771). It is one of five Viennese sonatas. It has been compared to its C minor and G major counterparts. Everything in it, and in the later C minor Sonata, points to the composer of The Seasons: not only in the structural mastery, but also in the great depth of the expression of the slow movement, which here acquires the character of a lyrical "scena", a form that Haydn was to use in the oratorios to such wonderful and inexhaustible effect. In the first movement, Haydn reveals himself as a master of sonata form, a form he had developed at the second stage of his career. The first movement is a movement in the fullest and most varied sonata form he had yet achieved. It is the largest of the three first movements to be found in the sonatas of this period.

The sonatas in which Haydn's style were markedly different from the concerto and concerto-like pieces of his middle period were, for the most part, the ones in which he followed Italian models. The sonatas in which he followed the English school, like those of the early years of his career, are more concerned with the outer display of the keyboard; the ones which were closest to the Continental model, the model of Mozart, are the ones which are most deeply felt, most noble and most expressive.

The great C minor Sonata Hob. XVI:20 (1771) shows typical features of a great concerto movement. Everything in this sonata shows how closely connected the two instruments are in Haydn's mind. The first movement is, in its way, a very large-scale movement, with a slow introduction and a very free treatment of the material. The second movement is a relatively modest movement in D major. The finale in G minor shows Haydn at his most Italianate.

In 1794 he visited London for the first time. "At the last concert", he writes to Marianne von Genzinger, "the King was in the royal box, and he listened to it all with the greatest attention. The hall was so packed that there were seven or eight rows of people standing behind the orchestra." He went to London at precisely the right moment, when the old Italian opera, the opera "seria", was beginning to be seen as an anachronism. "The serious opera", says Burney, writing in 1789, "is going the way of all flesh; the public has lost its taste for the old forms, which now appear artificial and unnatural." A new form of musical drama was making its appearance in England, a form which foreshadowed the changes that were about to take place in the world of Italian opera. The opera "seria", with its great showpieces, its singers and its virtuosity, was giving way to the opera "buffa", with its human drama, its naturalness, its sincerity of expression and its warmth of feeling. "The English like music", wrote Haydn to Marianne von Genzinger in 1790, "but only when it makes an impression; if it goes on too long, they get bored." The London concert-goers did not care for music that made too many demands on their intellect; what they wanted was entertainment. They preferred the lighter forms of music, the pieces which were easy to understand, the pieces which were immediately appealing.

A glance at the three "London" sonatas in G major, Hob. XVI:40 (1794) and the Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI:50 (1794) shows how closely Haydn had followed the new fashion. But Haydn was not the only one who had changed. The piano itself had changed; it had become more brilliant and more sonorous; it had become a vehicle for virtuosity. The English piano-makers, in their efforts to produce a more powerful instrument, had developed a mechanism which gave the player a greater range of dynamics and a greater variety of tone colour.

Haydn anticipates a procedure Beethoven was to explore 20 years later: a coda that becomes, effectively, one of three contrasting sections. Beethoven derives this technique from Haydn, perhaps unconsciously. Since it is only a single arrangement of a theme, it is debatable whether the section constitutes a formal part, or is a modified, highly decorated variant. But the variation pattern the coda exhibits defines the individual characteristics of this entire movement.

Through most of the three periods Prince Nikolaus Haydn wrote ever more keyboard sonatas, and by the end of his last years as Kapellmeister, he had returned to Vienna, where he had first been hired 40 years before. Three have come down in apparently authentic manuscript copies, two of them signed by Josephus Haydn and dated 1786. (One shows a curious preliminary sketch of the opening, suggesting that a passage originally planned for the keyboard ending in Haydn's mind as he wrote it down; the other bears the inscription "Friedrich Starke in Wien" and a note indicating the sonata was a gift.) In keyboard style, these are Haydn's earliest sonatas from this period.

The C minor Sonata Hob. XVI:20 is among his most impressive achievements. It is far ahead of its time. Placed apart from its expressive fecundity, the opening is among Haydn's boldest ideas: a first half consisting of two five-bar phrases, the first creating a strong impression, the second answering the start of the other, leading into a section calling for great finger dexterity.

The music's asymmetrical nature extends to the second subject as well, which seems to have an affinity with Haydn's Symphony No. 44 in B minor of the previous year. The development section, which begins as the main subject appears in a remote key.

*(Page 13)*

Before the development occurs in a "free," episodic manner, giving a mysterious cast to the music, Haydn is able to cast a somewhat mysterious air before the movement closes in a more relaxed, but scarcely conventional, manner that is not the least of the surprises it contains. There is no written-out repeat of the second half.

With the return of the minor material, the form is rounded off. Towards the end, Haydn introduces an extraordinary passage for the left hand alone, leading to a restatement of the initial theme in the key of G. This is followed by what appears to be a brief transition to the slow movement, but Haydn's penchant for surprise has not deserted him, and the transition concludes here, before a brief coda leads the sonata into what appears to be its proper key, B-flat major, albeit with a different movement in an unusual tempo. Haydn appears to consider this section a kind of "second theme" in this sonata.

An important feature of the C minor Sonata is its profoundly tragic quality. Haydn's later sonatas are occasionally characterized by their wit and sparkle, and their sometimes breathtaking dramatic effects (they are not to be found in direct juxtaposition with the equally extraordinary emotional depth of the earlier Sonata in D minor, Hob. XVI:33, or the C minor). The sonata is a glacial example, and the writing is more contrapuntally complex than most of the earlier sonatas, while the sonata appears to have no equivalent in his later work.

The finale is a fully-blooded sonata movement, with the opening theme in a bold, rising flourish. It is a movement of enormous strength and character, and the second subject is a graceful, ornate theme, decorated with trills and delicate embellishments. The final movement of the C minor sonata is a masterpiece of concentrated emotional expression, and its recitative-like passages give the music an extraordinary immediacy and drama. The slow movement, a set of variations, and the finale are linked by the opening gesture, and the variation movement is also a remarkable achievement.

In the late 1780s and early 1790s Haydn's music became increasingly sophisticated in the orchestral works that the so-called Sturm und Drang period is explicitly.

XVI:52 that Haydn composed for Therese Jansen in 1786, and her husband, the celebrated English painter, there is, indeed, a strong claim to be regarded as Haydn's greatest single achievement for the piano. Probably not performed publicly during Haydn's lifetime, the sonata is known only through a set of parts copied for his London publisher, who never issued it with the dedication, which may explain why the E-flat major sonata appears to have been a late addition to the set, perhaps added when it became available, or when Haydn wished to provide a more impressive counterpart.

In 1788/90 Joseph Haydn described the E-flat major sonata as having an "agreeable, cantabile manner." He added: "The work makes no special demands on the performer," and noted its "unexpected modulations" and "its related keyboard musical ideas, the execution of which might give trouble to an inexperienced performer," adding that the E-flat sonata was "in general a bit difficult, particularly because of its constant awkwardness, which should be avoided, although it is quite well set." (Letter from Haydn to Jansen, as quoted in C.F. Pohl's biography)

Haydn's seriousness (most in the C minor sonata) is counterbalanced by the brilliance of many of his later works, and the two-key aspect of the opening movement is resolved in the recapitulation, as that point has been greatly increased in its dramatic proportions, culminating in a coda of extraordinary power. In this sonata, Haydn's development has been described as a "fantasia" movement, where ideas follow each other in rapid succession, producing a most personal style of composition, and its sometimes unconventional character. Haydn was already contemplating retirement, and it is likely that some of the composer's plans to visit London had not yet crystallized.

Whether or not the last of the three movements, however, it is the finale, and the astonishing fluency in its second-subject melody, that is the work's crowning glory. It is unique in the final of a Sonata in its class, in which every note has its place, in a movement that is unparalleled for its length and depth of expression.

The key of Haydn's third movement is not, however, altogether unconnected with the first movement. The main second subject appears not in B-flat major, but B-flat minor, and before the movement comes to a rather abrupt close, there is a mysterious passage in B-flat minor, that most unusual, remote key, which recalls to the ear the tonal equivalent of the tonic B-flat major, a key that is related to B-flat minor only on the second degree of the scale, and the last movement of the opening theme.

The dotted rhythms that fill the second movement's opening theme give rise to the middle section of the piece in

*(Page 19)*

more dramatic music, like in the minor. One might almost think one was following the theme to the second movement in Haydn's longest variation form. The music then embarks on a free development, that expects for such a design to be practised, although this is not always the case. The first movement is followed by a shorter form, and the second part is rounded off, through, though compensated, with a code of brilliant, virtuosic character.

The slow movement begins with a delicate, almost chamber-music-like texture. The repeated note idea with which it opens, leads to two contrasting sections of ideas, and the second movement comes as a considerable surprise, as it turns out to be a simple, plain theme with one, and the first idea is a minuet-like character. It is a remarkable feature, and the second section is a graceful, flowing melody, with flowing quavers, and at the same time a fitting accompaniment. For this, he has been described as a composer of great genius.

## ANDRÁS SCHIFF

*(Page 19)*

András Schiff was born in Budapest in 1953, and has studied there and in London with several distinguished teachers, including George Malcolm and Peter Frankl, among others. Prize-winner at the Tchaikovsky Competition in Moscow, the György Cziffra and Premio in Venice, and the Liszt Prize in Budapest in 1976.

Career highlights include residencies and visiting professorships at several major music institutions, and his work with the Chamber Orchestra of Europe and the Dresden Staatskapelle.

Artistic activities are unusually varied, encompassing chamber music, Lieder, and orchestral performance. He has appeared as soloist with most of the world's leading orchestras. His numerous recordings on the Decca label include the complete Mozart piano concertos with the Chamber Orchestra of Europe, which won many international prizes. He founded the "Musica Omnia" concert series and international festivals in Cartagena, Tokyo, and elsewhere.

Schiff has many recordings, a number of which have won prizes, among them recordings of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier and the Goldbergs with the Salzburg Mozarteum Camerata Academica under the baton of the young composer and conductor Andrés Orozco-Estrida.

Among the many distinctions that András Schiff has received are the Kossuth Prize in his native Hungary, the German Federal Republic's Order of Merit, the Buxtehude Prize of the City of Hamburg, and the Queen's Medal for Music (2008). He has also received numerous international awards, and was appointed to a Professorship at the Barenboim-Said Academy. András Schiff has been a professor at the Barenboim-Said Academy since March 2008. He received his native Hungary's most distinguished honor, the Kossuth Prize, in March 1997 he received the Leonie Sonning Music Prize in Copenhagen.
