## Haydn's Piano Sonatas
## A Short History of their Reception and Interpretation
### By András Schiff

Haydn is, together with Schumann, probably the most underrated and most misunderstood of the great composers. Some might argue with this statement (and, in fact, Haydn's works are frequently performed) and that he has long been recognised as the father figure of Viennese Classicism. There is, on the other hand, the problem lies: on the one hand the frequency of the performances is not supported by their quality; on the other hand "Papa Haydn" has become one of the worst clichés in classical music. It degrades one of history's most innovative composers into a father figure. Conductors prefer to programme one or two in the context of the "Great" Symphonies do get an occasional hearing, but the near complete String Quartets are seldom performed. Similarly, of the 83 string quartets only opp. 76 & 77 are played regularly. Still, most musicians are more devoted to the art of the symphony and of the string quartet.

This is sadly not the case with the piano music. True, most piano students play Haydn's sonatas and are quite often heard as a (banal) "overture" or introduction to piano recitals. But that means that the pianist plays a "harmless little" Haydn sonata.

This is a phenomenon of the admiring form and critics are unable to distinguish the true achievements from the trivialities of the so-called "Gassenhauer". It shows a complete ignorance of the classical style and an utter lack of appreciation.

Haydn like Shakespeare was not a virtuoso pianist. He was a man much at home on string quartets. Between the earliest and the latest sonatas (from 1788 to 1794) we see a remarkable progression for music of style, even an evolution of style. The sonatas of the 1750s and 1760s are written for the harpsichord, or clavichord, while the later ones are for the fortepiano. In England Haydn was inspired by English pianos. These were broader and more powerful. For today's day and age, however, it does not make much sense to play them on a different instrument. I feel they are done best when played on a modern piano.

The performer is simultaneously in a difficult situation. On the one hand the musical demands of the works are extremely modest, only a few easy runs and no huge jumps. On the other hand, an accomplished musician/musicologist is required to understand all the notations and symbols and to make the right artistic limitations. The opportunities are enormous.

Use of a good edition is essential, and I consider the Wiener Urtext, edited by Christa Landon, to be the best. Other good ones are the Henle edition and those of Gerald Gerstenberg.

Familiarity with the most important contemporary music broadens one's mind. Haydn's piano works and Quartets are unavoidable: they often provide the key to a better understanding of the Viennese classics. The three sonatas (Hob. XVI: 59-60) are among the most remarkable pieces of the period, displaying dynamics, phrasing and articulation. In every way they are equal to Mozart's finest works.

## Piano Sonatas by Haydn
### By Misha Donat

Unlike Mozart and Beethoven, Haydn was no virtuoso pianist. He had, however, begun to learn to play the keyboard instrument at an early age, and from 1749-55, while a pupil at the choir school in Vienna, he undertook a serious study of the clavichord, harpsichord and organ. There can be little doubt that during his long period as Kapellmeister at the Esterházy orchestra Haydn had made himself a noted performer on the harpsichord and as late as 1792, he included a tiny keyboard solo into the finale of the "London" Symphony No. 98. The performance was witnessed by Samuel Wesley, who later described Haydn's playing in his Reminiscences.

In the medium of the sonata and symphony, Haydn more or less single-handedly raised the Classical piano sonata to the level of a high art form. His greatest sonatas are of a stature that was only rarely equalled by Mozart, and was not approached by Beethoven, who had transformed the form in the closing years of the 18th century.

Haydn's sonatas were written for domestic performance by amateur players, the large majority of them were published during Haydn's lifetime. For them Haydn composed not only two of the grandest among the late sonatas, but also the finest, and in some ways most ambitious works. Other outstanding performers to whom Haydn dedicated sonatas were Katharina and Marianna (Martines) von Auenbrugger, whose musical talents were highly rated.

The instrument for which Haydn wrote his mature sonatas was the still relatively new fortepiano. The piano for which Haydn wrote were markedly different from the concert grand of today. Their touch was lighter, their keyboard compass smaller, their tone more rapidly decayed.

## About András Schiff

**ANDRÁS SCHIFF** was born in Budapest in 1953 and began his piano studies at the age of five. His first teacher was Elisabeth Vukits; later he studied at the Franz Liszt Academy in Budapest with Ferenc Rados, George Kurtág and Annie Fischer. In 1974 he won the Chopin Competition in London. He lives in London.

Central to András Schiff's artistic activities are his historically-informed performance on the fortepiano. His many articles and masterclasses have been influential in the revival of interest in period instruments. In recent years he has also established himself as a conductor, with orchestras such as the Philharmonia, the Chamber Orchestra of Europe, and the Vienna Philharmonic.

András Schiff has made many recordings, a number of which have won prizes. Among his releases on the Teldec label are Beethoven's piano concertos with the Dresden Staatskapelle, Schubert's piano sonatas, and Bach's Goldberg Variations.

Among the many distinctions that András Schiff has received are the 1991 Bartók Prize and the first Claudio Arrau Memorial Medal, which was awarded by the Robert Schumann Society in 1994. In March 1996 he received the Danish Sonning Music Prize in Copenhagen.
