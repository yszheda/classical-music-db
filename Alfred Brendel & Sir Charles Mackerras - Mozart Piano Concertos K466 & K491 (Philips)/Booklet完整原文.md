# Mozart's "Grand" Piano Concertos in the Minor Mode
## by Wolfgang Rehm

Within the mighty corpus of Mozart's 21 concertos for one piano and orchestra – the rich diversity of which never ceases to astonish – two works composed only a little over 13 months apart, the Concertos K.466 (dated 10 February 1785) and K.491 (24 March 1786), are exceptional in several respects. Mozart's only two concertos in a minor key (D minor and C minor, respectively), they are completely different in stylistic orientation from the preceding six piano concertos (those from K.449 to K.459). With these works, Vienna's most famous keyboard virtuoso in the years 1784 to 1786 broke entirely new ground, which in terms of musical content can be described briefly as follows: the D minor Concerto is pervaded by drama and an impending sense of drastic fate, while the C minor Concerto, perhaps the most significant of all Mozart's compositions in this genre (and about which Beethoven reportedly said to the famous pianist and composer Johann Baptist Cramer: "We shall never be able to do anything like that"), is characterised by veiled tragedy, suppressed sorrow and ominous tensions. They have another distinct feature as well: the treatment of the woodwind, especially in the C minor (and there above all in the second movement) is so striking, that the latter piece is rightly often referred to as a concerto for "solo" piano and "solo" wind.

---

## Page 2 (scans/HIPPO_20230312_0002.jpg)

Both of the minor-key works belong to the series of twelve piano concertos that Mozart, in a state of creative "frenzy", committed to paper in Vienna between the beginning of 1784 (K.449) and the end of 1786 (K.503) for performance at his own "academies" (subscription concerts). These are works that he himself refers to as "grand concertos" in a letter to his father in Salzburg. Each of them, the D minor of 1785 and the C minor dating from the Figaro year, 1786, is framed by another pair of great concertos. K.466 was preceded by the F major K.459 (December 1784), the last of the group of six already mentioned, all composed in quick succession within that single year; and it was followed immediately by the C major K.467 (dated 9 March 1785), lighter and finely integrated in its musical structure, as a deliberate contrast to the D minor. K.491 was written just after the A major Concerto K.488, with its gently elegiac Adagio middle movement in F sharp minor; and it was followed by the radiantly festive C major Concerto K.503, the last of the twelve "grand" concertos, which again (as with the pair K.466 and K.467) forms an obvious antithesis to the preceding minor-key work.

Mozart's great importance as a composer and his popularity as a performer on the fortepiano are manifested in his "frenzied" compositional activity and successes during the years 1784 to 1786, for example, when he writes to his father on 3 March 1784 that he was certain to be giving 22 academies, or when, at the beginning of what was perhaps his most brilliant season of all, 1785, he is already set to appear in six Lenten or Friday subscription concerts. In the first of the latter group, on 11 February at the municipal casino "Zur Mehlgrube" on Vienna's Neuer Markt, the D minor Concerto received its first performance. Leopold Mozart arrived in Vienna on the day of the concert from Munich, where he had been staying since the end of January, and reported on the première in a letter of 16 February to his daughter Maria Anna (Nannerl) in Salzburg: "On the same Friday evening at six o'clock we drove to his first subscription concert, at which a great many members of the aristocracy were present... The concert was incomparable and the orchestra excellent. In addition to the symphonies a singer of the Italian theatre sang two arias. Then there was a new and very fine concerto by Wolfgang, which the copyist was still copying when we arrived, and the rondo of which your brother did not even have time to play through, as he had to supervise [correct] the copying" – K.466 was only completed on the day before the concert (10 February).

---

## Page 3 (scans/HIPPO_20230312_0003.jpg)

The Romantics had a preference for the D minor Piano Concerto and held it in particularly high esteem (Beethoven and Brahms both wrote cadenzas for it), and it seems to have been the only one by Mozart to find a place in the concert repertoire during the 19th century. An indication of why this was so may perhaps be seen in the character of the principal theme, marked by tense and uncanny syncopated upbeat triplets in the low strings, as well as in the passionately seething G minor middle section of the second movement, headed "Romance", which begins quietly and lyrically in B flat major and, following a transitional passage, concludes in the same manner. In the rondo – which in Mozart's autograph has neither movement heading nor tempo marking – the dramatic, fateful character of the opening movement is taken up again in the urgently ascending principal motif, but this extended finale ends light-heartedly in conciliatory D major, reached after the cadenza (here as in the opening movement none by Mozart himself survives, and Alfred Brendel plays his own cadenzas) through a brief transitional passage based on the main motif.

The Piano Concerto in C minor, K.491 was, along with the Concertos in E flat major, K.482, and A major, K.488, intended for a cycle of three academies, which Mozart had hoped to put on between January and April 1786. In the first concert the E flat work was given – the soloist-conductor was, according to his father Leopold, called upon to "repeat the Andante"; the A major presumably was heard on the second evening; but whether or not the C minor Concerto enjoyed its first performance at a further (third?) academy, which according to a rather vaguely worded report in the Wiener Zeitung took place on 7 April 1786, cannot be determined with certainty from the available documentary evidence. Nor is it possible to verify the assertion, found in the older Mozart literature, that he had to "repeat the Andante" of this concerto (as he had done in K.482).

---

## Page 4 (scans/HIPPO_20230312_0004.jpg)

In his original fair copy of the Concerto, Mozart did not prescribe a tempo for any of the three movements (the entire manuscript bears signs of being hastily prepared, as though he had wanted to leave himself scope for improvising). The indications "Allegro", "Larghetto" (a five-part rondo form in the brighter key of E flat major) and "Allegretto" (march theme with variations) have been entered in the autograph in another hand. Mozart himself, however, noted the tempo marking "Allegro" for the expansive, dark and menacing first movement (523 bars) in the catalogue of his own works that he kept from 1784, suggesting that the other two tempo indications also originated with the composer and were later entered in the manuscript in accordance with his wishes. For the C minor Concerto – which, incidentally, betrays its proximity to the G minor Symphony, K.550, of 1788 – there are, once again, no cadenzas by Mozart; he would have improvised them in the event of a performance and then "kept" them for his own subsequent use. Here Alfred Brendel plays his own cadenzas.

Wolfgang Hildesheimer, in his Mozart biography of 1977 [English translation by Marion Faber, 1982], deals in a few wise, pithy and pointed generalised remarks with the subject of Mozart's minor-key works: they occur rarely within the composer's output (yet are invariably special works of uncommon weight), so "that when we do suddenly come upon them we prick up our ears and search for a particular motivation: why here, precisely? Let it be understood that we seek, not an occasion, not an external cause, but the determining factor within the sequence of his works. Of course, we seek in vain. Is it really a decision for 'the tragic'? Since we have no definition for a musical equivalent of what we call in words 'the tragic,' this question [even for the two "grand" piano concertos in minor] cannot be answered."

Translation: Richard Evison

# Alfred Brendel

Alfred Brendel was born in 1931 in Wiesenberg, Czechoslovakia (now Moravska Trebova, Czech Republic), and grew up in Zagreb, before moving to Graz at the age of fourteen. He studied with Edwin Fischer, Eduard Steuermann and Paul Ba*m*gartner, and won first prize at the Bolzano Competition at the age of sixteen. He has lived in London since 1972 and was knighted in 1989. He retired from the concert platform in 2008.

One of the most distinguished musicians of our time, Alfred Brendel is universally recognised for his penetrating intellect, his quiet authority and his deep affection for the music he plays. A noted writer and lecturer on music, his numerous awards include honorary doctorates from universities around the world, the Ernst von Siemens Music Prize, and the Praemium Imperiale. He is an Honorary Member of the Royal Philharmonic Society, and in 1998 was awarded the Queen's Gold Medal for Music.

Mozart has always occupied a central place in Alfred Brendel's affections and in his concert programmes. This is his seventh recording of Mozart for Philips Classics, and follows his highly acclaimed complete cycle of Mozart's piano sonatas.

# Sir Charles Mackerras

Born in 1925 in New York State to Australian parents, Sir Charles Mackerras began his musical career as an oboist with the Sydney Symphony Orchestra before moving to London to study conducting at the Royal Academy of Music. He subsequently studied with Václav Talich in Prague. He began his conducting career in 1948, and quickly became known for his championship of Czech music – he is widely regarded as the leading interpreter of Janáček in our time, and has also made many groundbreaking recordings of Mozart and Beethoven, including a complete cycle of Mozart's symphonies with the Scottish Chamber Orchestra.

He was principal conductor of the BBC Symphony Orchestra (1987-1990), and music director of the Welsh National Opera (1987-1992), the Orchestra of the Age of Enlightenment (1993-2006), and the Royal Liverpool Philharmonic Orchestra (1995-1998). He was knighted in 1979. He received many major awards during his career, including the Royal Philharmonic Society Gold Medal in 2005. He died in 2010.

# Scottish Chamber Orchestra

The Scottish Chamber Orchestra was formed in 1974 and quickly established itself as one of the finest chamber orchestras in Europe. It performs an annual season in Glasgow, Edinburgh, Aberdeen, Dundee, Perth and Inverness, and tours regularly throughout the United Kingdom and abroad. The Orchestra enjoys an international reputation for its vibrant and stylish performances, and its award-winning discography of over 100 recordings includes recordings of Mozart symphonies conducted by Sir Charles Mackerras which have won widespread critical acclaim.
