## LINER NOTES

### HONEGGER AND MILHAUD

Arthur Honegger and Darius Milhaud were both born in 1892. Honegger's birth took place in Le Havre, but his parents were in fact Swiss. When he was old enough he was sent for a two-year period of study at the Zurich Conservatoire, which gave him a good general grounding in the German musical tradition. When he completed his studies at the Paris Conservatoire, his immersion in French culture provided a second dimension to an unusually broad musical outlook. Milhaud's early influences were the folk music of Provence, where he was born and brought up, and the Jewish faith. He also studied at the Paris Conservatoire.

In 1920 a newspaper article named a group of six young composers and christened them *Les Six*. This loose artistic group, which included Honegger and Milhaud, survived as a matter of convenience for a short period, but neither Honegger nor Milhaud was strongly committed to the Satie-influenced, anti-romantic fashion of post-war Paris. Both composers achieved success in their twenties and travelled widely as performers: during the inter-war years, Milhaud lived to a ripe old age, but when he died in 1974 he had been plagued by rheumatoid arthritis for half his lifetime. He was still able to conduct, however, and made numerous recordings in later years. Honegger died at the age of 63, having been handicapped by a heart complaint since his mid-fifties. This condition was made worse following the strain of conducting an early LP recording of his cantata *Le Roi David*.

Honegger's *Pacific 231* (the work had the name of the engine which then drew express trains in Europe) was written in 1923, and its strong but easily assimilated dissonances brought the composer immediate fame, even notoriety. At the time of its first performance he wrote a fairly detailed programme of the railway journey depicted by the short tone poem, but later he insisted that the piece should be regarded merely as absolute music. *Pacific 231* had the formal title of *Mouvement symphonique No.1*.

*Mouvement symphonique No.2* emerged in 1928, and was subtitled *Rugby* (Honegger was a keen sportsman in his youth). He at first revealed that the piece had been inspired by a match at Paris's Colombes Stadium, but not long afterwards he insisted that its vigorous, thrusting rhythms should be regarded merely as conveying in a general sense the vitality and colour of a Rugby game. (When the lesser-known *Mouvement symphonique No.3* was completed in 1933 Honegger this time avoided the use of a secondary descriptive title). *Pastorale d'été*, written for small orchestra in 1920, was later to be used as the basis of his one-act opera *L'Aiglon* (the overture to Shakespeare's play *The Tempest* was written in 1923 as part of incidental music for a French version of Shakespeare's play).

Honegger had studied composition with Vincent d'Indy, and his highly expert, immediately strong readings have that peculiarly direct quality which can only occur when a composer realises his written score in performance. It seems likely that the very good anonymous orchestra was a major Paris ensemble which could not be named for contractual reasons. Milhaud was also a very experienced conductor by the time he first started to make records. Usually of course he conducted his own music, but he had directed the Philadelphia Orchestra in a 1922 programme which included works by C.P.E. Bach, Mendelssohn and Stravinsky, and at about the same time he took part in a strange Viennese double bill, wherein Schoenberg conducted his *Pierrot Lunaire* in its usual German setting, and then Milhaud conducted the same work in a French translation.

Milhaud's *Concertino de printemps*, cast in a single movement, was written in 1934 for the violinist Yvonne Astruc, "whose style of playing, both technical and interpretative", wrote the composer, "seems to me what a work of this sort surely inspired by Spring". Astruc was not a well-known artist, but to judge from her performance she was quite a formidable technician, and Milhaud was delighted with her "beautiful playing" on the record. The *Concertino* is scored for a 12-strong ensemble of single winds (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon), trumpet and timpani, though it is sometimes played with multiple strings. Nearly 20 years were to elapse before he once again became part of a four-movement suite entitled *Les quatre saisons*, which also includes mini-concertos for viola, two pianos and trombone.

Milhaud's Piano Concerto of 1934 (his first essay in this form), was written for the eminent pianist Marguerite Long, artist admired by Fauré, Debussy and Ravel. Ravel, indeed, had written his Piano Concerto in G for Long in 1932, and recorded the work very soon afterwards with her. The three short movements of Milhaud's busy, bustling Concerto each fit neatly on to one 78rpm side: could the composer have anticipated an early recording of his work with Long, perhaps?

Milhaud's ballet *La création du monde*, based on an African tribal legend which purported to explain the origin of our planet, had discovered jazz for the first time in 1920, on his visit to the USA in 1922 he made every effort to visit Harlem and hear as many good jazz groups as he could. Their music made a great impact on him. It was "completely different", he wrote later, "the melodic lines, set off by the percussion, overlapping contrapuntally in a throbbing mixture of broken, twisted rhythms". This influence came to fruition in a controversially famous ballet which attempted to fuse traditional form with jazz elements. Milhaud scored the work for 18 instruments, including a saxophone.

*Les songes*, written in 1933 for a group called *Les Ballets 1933*, and with choreography by George Balanchine, is a much more obscure work, and has failed to gain a niche in the standard repertoire. Though the record labels do not admit to such a thing, Milhaud's recording contains less than half of the original set of two ten inch records is very scarce, and I hope that listeners will overlook a few ticks at the beginning of one 78rpm side.

**ALAN SANDERS**
