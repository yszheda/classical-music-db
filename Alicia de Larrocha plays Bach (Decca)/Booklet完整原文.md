Bach's Italian Concerto BWV 971 was published in 1735 under its formal title 'Concerto nach Italienischem Gusto and Concerto im Geschmacke' (Concerto in the Italian Style) together with the Ouverture in the French Style (BWV 831) as the first half of Clavier-Übung (Keyboard Practice), which was also the title of his first published work, the Six Partitas, BWV 825-30. It consists in fact of a single movement – an Aria – between a Prelude and Fugue, and Busoni's transcription of the Goldberg Variations, BWV 988 was intended to contrast the popular Italian and French styles of keyboard music, with two of the most original works of the period – the Italian concerto and Ouverture – whose sound-spectrums are, respectively, transferred to the keyboard.

Bach's only work for solo keyboard which also specifies the type of instrument to be used (in this case a two-manual harpsichord) for the Ouverture and Goldberg Variations, it is perhaps best regarded as a composition in which the forces in an instrumental concerto are contrasted by a single performer on the piano manual of a harpsichord. The effect, the music is far too good to be regarded as an experimental novelty. There are several inspired performances that made all the difference to the slow movements and a finale that is not rushed, as so many are.

No less genial is Larrocha's handling of the last of Bach's French Suites, No. 6 in E major, BWV 817 and the final set in the Clavier-Übung, published in 1801: all six consist of what had become in the early eighteenth-century a standard sequence of movements – allemande, courante, sarabande with various additional movements (gavotte, polonaise, menuet, bourrée and gigue) after the sarabande. (The menuet is placed after the gigue in some editions, but not in the first.) No. 5's air of complex polyphonic strands gives way to an utterly charming finale; Larrocha's enthusiasm for this style galant.

To conclude this volume we have Busoni's masterly transcriptions of Bach's mighty Chaconne from Partita No. 2 in D minor for solo violin, BWV 1004. Both are magnificent works of art, very different in their own ways, contributing towards the musical heritage of our time. The Busoni version is the most original, juxtaposing French and Italian styles in an effortless sequence of variations over a repeated ground bass of D-D-C-sharp-D-B-flat-G-A-D, a movement that is longer in duration than any of the four preceding movements. Over and above this, from a reading of the most conspicuous devices and extra-musical gestures of the score, it emerges that this extraordinary movement is in fact a profound tribute to the memory of Bach's first wife Maria Barbara.

Busoni's transcription begins with a faithful enough reproduction of Bach as it does for the Chaconne, now on two pianos, and while Brahms continues in this vein, he gradually transforms the texture of the work into a grand Romantic free arrangement using all the sonorities of the modern grand without distorting Bach's harmonic structures and melodic lines.

This is the first (1973) of two recordings of the Chaconne that Larrocha made; the second was made in 1988. ALICIA DE LARROCHA

'Everything about me', said Alicia de Larrocha, 'is small.' And it was true – almost: her stature (a diminutive 4'9"), her hands (which barely stretched a tenth) and, as she admitted, her concerto repertoire. The sound she produced was another matter altogether, as was the emotional warmth of her playing, her spirit and her stage charisma: all were huge.

Born in Barcelona in 1923 to a Catalan father and a mother of Navarraise ancestry, and with Andalusian and Basque blood in her veins, Larrocha began studies at the age of three with Granados's pupil Frank Marshall. She was proud of her Iberian roots, but she disliked the 'Spanish specialist' tag, and thought herself 'a universalist, not a specialist'. Yet though she particularly excelled in Bach and Mozart, it was nonetheless in the Spanish repertoire that she was (and remains) unrivalled.

Herbert Breslin, the renowned artists' manager who masterminded her recording career with Decca, said of Larrocha in 1978, 'There are two kinds of repertory Alicia plays: things she plays extremely well, and things she plays better than anyone else. But what I think makes her a phenomenon is that she doesn't give the impression of being a great personality. She's cool as a cucumber. Onstage, she doesn't even like to look at the audience. So what the public is responding to is something in the music.'

Larrocha made her first recording aged nine, in 1932. Exactly 70 years later, she retired following a final concert at Carnegie Hall. She died in the city of her birth on 25 September 2009. Jeremy Nicholas
