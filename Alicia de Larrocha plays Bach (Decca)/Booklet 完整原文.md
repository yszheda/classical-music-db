## ALICIA DE LARROCHA

'Everything about me', said **Alicia de Larrocha**, 'is small.' And it was true – almost: her stature (a diminutive 4'9"), her hands (which barely stretched a tenth) and, as she admitted, her concerto repertoire. The sound she produced was another matter altogether, as was the emotional warmth of her playing, her spirit and her stage charisma: all were huge.

Born in Barcelona in 1923 to a Catalan father and a mother of Navarraise ancestry, and with Andalusian and Basque blood in her veins, Larrocha began studies at the age of three with Granados's pupil Frank Marshall. She was proud of her Iberian roots, but she disliked the 'Spanish specialist' tag, and thought herself 'a universalist, not a specialist'. Yet though she particularly excelled in Bach and Mozart, it was nonetheless in the Spanish repertoire that she was (and remains) unrivalled.

Herbert Breslin, the renowned artists' manager who masterminded her recording career with Decca, said of Larrocha in 1978, 'There are two kinds of repertory Alicia plays: things she plays extremely well, and things she plays better than anyone else. But what I think makes her a phenomenon is that she doesn't give the impression of being a great personality. She's cool as a cucumber. Onstage, she doesn't even like to look at the audience. So what the public is responding to is something in the music.'

Larrocha made her first recording aged nine, in 1932. Exactly 70 years later, she retired following a final concert at Carnegie Hall. She died in the city of her birth on 25 September 2009.

**Jeremy Nicholas**

## Notes on the Music

**Bach's Italian Concerto BWV 971** was published in 1735 under its formal title *Concerto nach Italienischem Gusto* (Concerto after the Italian taste) together with the Overture in the French Style BWV 831. The two works from Part II of what eventually became the four books of the *Clavier-Übung* ('Keyboard Practice') which Bach produced between 1726 and 1741. In Part II he uses the Six Partitas, BWV 825-30, for Part I consists mainly of liturgical organ music, while Part II is of a profoundly secular nature. In Part I Bach wanted to contrast the popular Italian and French styles of the day, and here the chief orchestral genres of the two nations – the Italian solo concerto and the French overture-suite – are transferred to the keyboard.

Bach's only original concerto for solo keyboard is also one of the few works which specifies the use of a two-manual harpsichord (the French Overture and Goldberg Variations are two others). The aim was to reproduce the difference between an instrumental concerto with the contrasting forte and piano manuals of the harpsichord. Though this instrument convey the same effect, the music is far too good to be solely the preserve of the harpsichord: the precepts of the most severe purist could object to it as the present-day, especially in such a life-affirming performance made all the more delightful by the sensitive phrasing of the slow movement and a finale that is not rushed, as so many are.

No less genial is Larrocha's handling of the last of Bach's French Suites, No. 6 in E major, BWV 817 completed in about 1722 but not published until 1801. All six consist of what had become by the early eighteenth-century a standard sequence of dance movements. Each opens with a stately allemande and sarabande with various additional movements following – in the case of BWV 817 a polonaise, menuet, bourrée and gigue (the menuet is placed after the gigue in some manuscripts). Most noticeable in the Suite here is: still's the lack of complete polyphonic strands – unlike the English Suites with their galant and chivalrous enthusiasm for the style galant.

Larrocha follows this with a purity account of the Fantasia in C minor, BWV 906 (with an incomplete fugue; both are in the Suite (with an sometimes is, as with BWV 921)), written sometime between 1726 and 1731 and not published till 1803.

Bach's six English Suites, BWV 806-11 differ from the French counterparts in several respects: they are both opening preludes (as well as the first of these is an extended virtuosic concerto); they are larger in scale (as are Bach's concertos); their dance movements are generally more developed with a greater use of counterpoint; and the final gigue is almost invariably, ironically, closer to those of Couperin and other French composers of the time than those found in the French Suites. Exactly how they came to be called 'English' Suites is unclear. Completed sometime before 1720 (perhaps as early as 1714), none were published until 1871. The prelude of Suite No.2 in A minor is followed by an allemande, courante, sarabande (with two beautifully nuanced gaies here from Larrocha), two bourrées and a gigue.

In 1967, after these Decca Studio recordings, Alicia de Larrocha recorded in Kingsway Hall, London, two Bach transcriptions by Harriet Cohen (1895-1967): the chorale *Liebster Jesu, wir sind hier* (Beloved Jesu, we are stand), BWV 731 and *Ertöt uns durch dein Güte* (Sanctify us by thy goodness), BWV 22, the final chorus of the cantata *Jesus nahm zu sich die Zwölfe*. A leading British pianist in the first half of the twentieth century, Cohen had a close relationship with Arnold Bax, Vaughan Williams wrote his piano concerto for her and Bartók dedicated the last six pieces in his Mikrokosmos to her.

To conclude this volume we have Busoni's masterly transcription of Bach's mighty Chaconne from the first movement of the Partita No.2 for solo violin, BWV 1004. Both are, in their own ways, towering contributions to the literature of their respective genres. Bach's original juxtaposes French and Italian styles in an effortless sequence of variations over a repeated ground-bass of D–D–C♯–D–B♭–A–G–D–A, a movement that is longer in duration than the preceding four movements combined. Over and above this, from a melding of the many compositional devices and extra-musical constructs, it has been suggested that this extraordinary movement is in fact an elegiac gesture in memory of Bach's (first) wife Maria Barbara.

Busoni's transcription begins with a faithful, reverberant reproduction of Bach's original (as does Brahms's ingenious version for the left hand), but while Brahms continues in this way, Busoni (like Alexander Siloti) expands the scale of the work into a grandly Romantic piece of work, using all the sonorities of the modern grand piano without losing sight of Bach's structure and harmonies.

This is the first (1973) of two recordings of the Chaconne that Larrocha made for Decca; the second was made in 1996. Early in her career Larrocha was criticised for a heavy-handed style with Bach (Busoni) The Times noted her 'relentless metallic brilliance'. Nothing of that here: in this transcendent performance the slow opening is given a spacious breadth and a gravitas which builds inexorably to a fine, full-throated finale.

**Jeremy Nicholas**
