### Producer's Note

From the gramophone's earliest days, the flautist has always been a favourite recorder, both as soloist, and accompanist to the likes of Dame Nellie Melba and Amelita Galli-Curci. In acoustic recording days, the instrument recorded surprisingly well, probably because its range suited the process and the sound was easily directed into the recording horn.

The recordings on this CD were all made by the electrical process (with a microphone and electronic amplifiers) with its greatly improved fidelity. That being said, there are occasions when the engineers simply did not record enough level onto the disc, thus making the surface noise louder in relation to the music. Several of the later London recorded Columbias suffer from this to a certain extent. I had wanted to use a 1931 Decca recording of the trio from *L'Enfance du Christ* by Berlioz, but the recording level was so low and with the Decca surface as bad as usual, even with modern noise reduction techniques, the music was swamped by the noise.

### Marcel Moyse - Flute Fantastique
**by Julian Haylock**

Arguably the finest and certainly the most influential flute player-teacher of the 20th century, Marcel Moyse's priceless series of recordings provides an exemplar that very few handbooks could ever have surpassed. Whether in the seamless, lyrical phrasing of Saint-Saëns' *Le Cygne* [2], technical wizardry and faultless intonation of the Sechers [5], Reichart [6] and Doppler [11] showpieces, or the noble simplicity of Ravel's *Menuet* [12], and Tchaikovsky Andante cantabile [16], Moyse seemingly had it all.

Marcel Joseph Moyse was born in St. Amour, Jura, on 17 May 1889, shortly after the completion of the duo concertante RDI that became his legendary Paris Exhibition. His earliest claim to fame was having played flute in an orchestra conducted by Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov when he was just fourteen. His tutors at the Paris Conservatoire included such legendary figures as Paul Taffanel, Adolphe Hennebains, Philippe Gaubert and Lucien Capet, and in 1906 Moyse was awarded the Premier Prix. His European career might be said to have started when he was appointed solo flautist with the Padeloup orchestra in 1910, by which time he already had a burgeoning reputation as a brilliant soloist.

Three years later Moyse was appointed principal flute with the Opéra-Comique (a post he held until 1938), and toured the United States with the great Australian diva, Dame Nellie Melba. In 1918 he took up a principal flute with the Paris Conservatoire Orchestra, and the following year he joined a similar position with the Concerts Sevihan. Also during this period he played with the Ballets Russes Orchestra. By the time he was appointed Professor of Flute at the Geneva Conservatoire in 1939, and the Paris Conservatoire in 1952, (in succession to his ex-teacher, Gaubert), he was already seasoned recording artist, as testified by the Odeon and HMV takes in this collection.

In 1930 he received the first of several Grand Prix du Disque for his recording of Mozart's D major Flute Concerto, K314 (285d, on Pearl GEMM CD 9118), coupled with the *Grand Polonaise* in E flat, Op. 32 by Glière. He also recorded the Debussy Flute, Viola & Harp Sonata, and Pearl GEMM 0267), listening and these stunning performances are to be reminded of one of his often-quoted lines: 'When I play I try to convey the impression of laughing; of someone talking through the medium of my instrument in a manner almost as much as that expressed by the human voice.'

In the meantime Moyse's orchestral career continued to flourish. In 1930s he appears under the most internationally renowned conductors of the age, including Otto Klemperer, Arturo Toscanini, Bruno Walter, Willem Mengelberg, Richard Strauss, Serge Koussevitzky, and with Stravinsky and Prokofiev. Albert Roussel dedicated the first solo movement of his Op. 37 *Joueurs de flûte* (1925) to Moyse, and Jacques Ibert wrote several flute works especially for him.

The previous year, Marcel had joined the Moyse Trio with his daughter-in-law violinist, Blanche Honegger Moyse, and his son Louis as pianist (see tracks 11, 15, & 16). The latter had (like his father before him) recently won the Premier Prix for flute in 1932, and was essentially filling in time playing in bars and restaurants, as well as working as his father's teaching assistant at the Conservatoire. Such was the impact of the new ensemble that only five years after its formation it was awarded the Grand Prix du Disque for a recording of Bach's Triosonatas, and went on to make regular tours of Europe, South America and the USA up until 1950. Meanwhile, in 1934 Marcel had been made a member of the Légion d'honneur.

In 1930, Marcel received a telegram from the great conductor Arturo Toscanini inviting him to join the New York Philharmonic as principal flute. Moyse declined the offer, preferring to remain in Europe. However, this telegram demonstrates the high regard in which Moyse was held by the greatest conductors of his day.

### Later Career and Legacy

Following the Second World War, in 1949 Marcel emigrated to America, and made such a profound impact that just a year later he joined with his son Louis (who had moved to the US in 1948), Rudolf Serkin, and Adolf and Hermann Busch to establish the Marlboro Music Festival. This was to become the hub of Marcel's teaching activities for the next fifty years, and attracted some of the world's finest musicians and teachers, including Pablo Casals, Alexander Schneider, Felix Galimir, Misha Schneider, and Mieczysław Horszowski. Moyse also became a regular visitor to Switzerland, where he also gave masterclasses. In 1973 he gave a series of legendary classes in Japan, and at the grand old age of 92 conducted a concert of Mozart and Dvořák in New York. He died three years later on 1 November 1984.

Among Moyse's many students were some of the leading flautists of our time, including James Galway, Michel Debost, Trevor Wye, William Bennett, Gary Wincenc, Bernard Goldberg, Robert Aitken, Julia Bogorad and Paula Robison. Fortunately, some of Moyse's teaching has survived on videotape for the illumination of future generations. "Those of you who studied with Marcel Moyse", enthuses Paula Robison, "will always carry with us the memory of his incandescent eyes, his gesturing hands, his wonderful laugh – which he used, just as much as he used his keen intellect, as teaching tools – drawing out everything that was good and possible in a player." Each time I quote Mr. Moyse to my students I wish that they could have seen him in action. I wish that they could be inspired by him directly! Now, with the help of these historic films, that direct inspiration will at last be possible."

Moyse was the author of innumerable teaching publications for the flute, including two notable classics, *De la Sonorité* and *Tone Development through Interpretation*, which are still widely in use today. In addition, he composed a vast series of pedagogical flute studies, including 20 exercises et études, 24 petites études mélodiques, 101 études faciles et progressives, 25 études de virtuosité (after Czerny), 12 études de grande virtuosité (after Chopin) and 50 Variations on the Allemande from Bach's A minor flute sonata.

The Marcel Moyse Society has announced that there is to be an Archive housed at the Lincoln Center Library for the Performing Arts in New York City, and will be catalogued as part of the Music Division Collection. The collection will include rare photographs, letters to Moyse from well-known musicians such as Gaubert, Ibert and Rampal, manuscripts of unpublished study books, and audio and videotapes of lessons and master Classes. As Moyse himself so memorably put it: 'I long for the day when I can hear the beauty of the sound comes from the generocity [sic] of the soul.'

**— JULIAN HAYLOCK**
