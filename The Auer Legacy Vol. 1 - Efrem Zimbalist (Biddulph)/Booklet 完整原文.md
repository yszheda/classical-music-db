## 内页说明文字 / Liner Notes

**Efrem Zimbalist** was one of the very first Auer ambassadors. He made a vital and influential contribution to the violin world in America for many decades, largely through his pedagogical and administrative activities; but also—particularly during the first decades of the 20th-century—by means of his strikingly individual playing.

Zimbalist was born on Rostov-on-Don. The chronology of his birth remains uncertain: most reference books quote 9 April 1889 as the date of his birth though Zimbalist himself eventually settled upon 1890. He received his early training from his father, a violinist and opera conductor, who discovered his son had perfect pitch, a phenomenal memory and a natural dexterity on the violin. The boy's departure for St. Petersburg was the inevitable next step. Auer not only awarded him a special scholarship—one reserved for pupils with quite extraordinary talents—but also went to great lengths to secure a special permit to enable Zimbalist's family to live in St Petersburg, legally out of bounds to all Jews, while the boy completed his studies. (With the imminent influx of Jewish pupils such machinations were to become almost routine.)

Although Auer considered Zimbalist to be perhaps his most intelligent student, the relationship between mentor and pupil was frequently stormy; never more so than when Zimbalist became involved in the revolutionary upheavals of 1905. In his autobiography Auer maintains that Zimbalist even went as far as to threaten his fellow students with a beating should they attend class—*the very antithesis of the civilized, cultured figure Zimbalist was to become*. Despite his subversive activities, Zimbalist graduated later with highest honours, in the process earning the unstinting praise of Glazunov, the Conservatory's Director. In his report Glazunov wrote: "*A colossal talent. Phenomenal musical feeling. His interpretation was inspired, full of mood... A shattering impression, beyond comparison*." Auer concurred though he was forced to observe, somewhat wryly, that during his last two years at the Conservatory Zimbalist had rarely attended his classes which might have contributed to his progress!

In that same year (1907), Zimbalist made his debut in both Berlin and London. After a prolonged European tour he arrived in America. His Boston debut in 1911 signaled the start of a remarkable success, including an instant recording contract with RCA Victor. It was a foregone conclusion that he would settle there and ultimately assume American citizenship. For sometime his career was divided between teaching and the concert platform though gradually his professorial duties began to dominate, particularly after his appointment as head of the violin department at the Curtis Institute in 1928. He was appointed Director of the Institute in 1941, a position he held until 1968. Although he officially retired from the concert platform in 1949, he continued to make guest appearances, most memorably to give the premiere of Gian Carlo Menotti's Violin Concerto in Philadelphia in December 1952.

Zimbalist was a very different violinist from the majority of Auer's pupils. Significantly, he did not come from the deprived areas of the Pale of Settlement, those regions where the Jewish community was forcibly confined and which proved to be the bedrock of a wave of stupendously gifted prodigies. Zimbalist's more privileged background unquestionably influenced his style, which was less overtly emotional and more intellectually based than the majority of violinists from the Auer stable. Also of note is the fact that Zimbalist entered the public arena not as a prodigy but as an unusually mature young man in his late teens. But it was Zimbalist, or rather the recognition he earned first in Europe and then in America, along with the success enjoyed by Auer's prodigy phenomenon, Mischa Elman, which opened the floodgates. Soon students of all ages were beating a path to Auer's door.

Zimbalist's recordings confirm the claim that Auer encouraged his pupils to retain their own individuality. In many respects they reveal the introspective face of the Auer legacy: his sound is hauntingly chased and pure, his playing defined by a rare delicacy and sophistication. The technique is rock solid though, as for example his legendary bow arm that is capable of spinning effortless and endless phrases of seamless legato, and never is there a hint of virtuosity for virtuosity's sake – not even in his effervescent transcription of Chopin's "Minute" Waltz!
