## 内页 - 关于指挥与乐团

**Stanislaw Skrowaczewski**, music director of the Minnesota Orchestra from 1960 to 1979 and now its conductor emeritus, has maintained his current reputation — that of a conductor of brilliance, power, and distinctive personality — from the earliest years of his career. He was already a well-known conductor and composer in his native Poland and throughout Europe before he made his U.S. debut in 1958, conducting the Cleveland Orchestra at the invitation of George Szell. Now in great international demand, he regularly leads major ensembles in Vienna, Berlin, Paris, London, Boston, Munich, and other European cities, and makes frequent appearances with the most prestigious American orchestras, including those of Boston, Chicago, and Philadelphia. In 1968 Skrowaczewski made his Salzburg Festival debut with the Vienna Philharmonic; that same year, at the request of the late United Nations Secretary General U Thant, he led the Minnesota Orchestra in a special concert in the United Nations Security Council Hall, commemorating the 20th anniversary of Human Rights Day. His Metropolitan Opera debut followed two years later, when he conducted performances of Mozart's *The Magic Flute* both at Lincoln Center and on a national tour. Among his acclaimed compositions is the Concerto for Clarinet and Orchestra, premiered by the Minnesota Orchestra under Skrowaczewski's own baton in April 1981.

The Minnesota Orchestra has long enjoyed prominence as one of America's leading symphony orchestras. Founded in 1903 as the Minneapolis Symphony Orchestra, the ensemble has established a tradition of musical eminence through a succession of great conductors, from Emil Oberhoffer (1903-22), Henri Verbrugghen (1923-31), Eugene Ormandy (1931-36), Dimitri Mitropoulos (1937-49), Antal Doráti (1949-60), Stanislaw Skrowaczewski (1960-79), Neville Marriner (1979-1986) to Edo de Waart, its present music director.

The 96-member Minnesota Orchestra continues to tour throughout the region and the country as it has since 1907, and to record as it has since 1914, when it became the second American orchestra to do so. Its 52-week season includes symphony series in Minneapolis, St. Paul, Rochester and St. Joseph, Minnesota, and annual performances at Carnegie Hall. Weekly home concerts are broadcast live nationally from the world-famous Orchestra Hall in Minneapolis, known for its outstanding acoustics.

Bartók's *Concerto for Orchestra*, composed on Koussevitzky's commission between August 15 and October 8, 1943, is one of the most popular pieces in today's orchestral repertoire and, paradoxically, a highly conventional piece of music — electronic and concrete music notwithstanding.

The main point of contention is the celebrated passage in the fourth movement (*Intermezzo interrotto*), where a theme of Shostakovich's "Leningrad" Symphony (No. 7) is said to be parodied (here are people who are not absolutely convinced of this in spite of the supporting statement of Peter Bartók, the composer's son). The second bone of contention is the cryptic theme from Lehar's *The Merry Widow* — "Da geh' ich zu Maxim's" — with the distinction of having served as a model.

That the movement represents an autobiographical allusion is inferred from the composer's cantankerously impersonal statement in the notes he supplied to the program book of the first performance:

The general mood of the work represents, apart from the jolting second movement, a gradual transition from the sternness of the first movement and the prevailing lugubrious death-song of the third, to the life-assertion of the last one.

In the grand design of the work we discover the typically Bartókian principle of symmetric architecture: the outermost and last movements are related to each other not only through individual modifications. Both the second and fourth movements show a schema-form, of which the third seems to be the simple pivot. But this symmetry is not merely a structural necessity, it is an expression of the composer's philosophical outlook. The third movement, the factual and intellectual axis of the *Concerto*, is conceived in Bartók's favorite bridge pattern in which two paragraphs are recapitulated, after the interposition of a contrast, in reversed order.

In the mysterious introduction to the first movement (reminiscent, among others, of the opening of Bartók's opera *Bluebeard's Castle*), we soon hear the outlines of a theme of unmistakably Hungarian stamp. It will be heard later in the work, especially in the first movement. The passionate theme that now bursts upon us on the strings reinforces this nostalgically national feeling. The principal theme of the main *Allegro* is anticipated by a stubborn repetition of its opening motif. The theme is particular in that it is strictly rhythmic. In contrast to its fiery attack, the second theme is quiet, undulating, and of a narrow compass. Between the two a third theme is presented but it is not allowed to make a full statement. The opening fragment of the first theme is sprung up so rudely from the violins, indicating the beginning of the development section, in which the strings deserve particular notice: a new theme of quietly melodious character introduced by the oboe; and the fugal and canonic elaboration of the brochure theme (the first proposal between the two basic themes) is neither. The principal theme's surprise is indicated, in unison. A full by the full orchestra after a brief and incomplete reference to the principal theme.

Main ideas are now remembered in reverse order of occurrence, with the trombone theme preceding the others.

In the second movement after a strictly functional introduction of the side-drum, which is to reappear as a bridge between the formal sections of the movement, the main idea is presented by a couple of bassoons in comic-opera style. A contrasting melody of good-humored themes is now repeated by a pair of oboes in thirds, by the clarinets in duet at the seventh, and by the flutes at the fifth, but with brilliant additional embellishments. The formal occurrence of the woodwind instruments concludes the development.

The contrasting middle section of the movement consists of a chorale announced by the serious and solemn tones of the brass. After the clarinets have announced their theme, the clear formal outlines is the essentially unaltered reprise of the first. It is concluded by a vanishing chord of the dominant seventh, whose vibration is extended by the side-drum's tattoo.

The second theme is a continuation of the first. The side-drum interrupts the development, to hear the mysterious opening characterized by fourths, and the theme of Hungarian flavor. These are substantially enlarged and amplified partly with a characteristically Bartókian reminiscence of this open-ended, a continuation of the first, as though it were to lead to a new theme, but of an essentially colored recollection. The music fades away with the last flicker of memory's vanishing image.

The prime subject of the fourth movement is exotic in melodic contour and rhythmic organization: its reiteration is combined with its own inverted figure. Refrain-like, it is repeated twice more: first, in the form of a brief recollection, and at the end, with extensions and a cadenza that pass from *pianissimo* to *fortissimo*. 1939 — (a) Completion of the *Mikrokosmos* for piano.
(b) *Divertimento for String Orchestra*, composed at Szanen, August 2-17; premiere in Basel, June 11, 1940.
(c) String Quartet No. 4, completed in November and dedicated to the Kolisch Quartet, which had given the premiere of No. 5 in Washington in 1935 and introduced this work in New York on January 20, 1941, in Bartók's presence.

Because Bartók knew exactly how to realize his musical ideas, he was able to compose and work quickly and with great concentration. Even so complex and substantial a score as *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta* was completed just ten weeks after the commission was accepted; from start to finish Bartók had been wholly absorbed in his work and plans, and as the title implies, he was concerned with sonorities as well as content. He specified in diagrams how the instruments were to be placed in performing this music: the two parts of the double string orchestra occupy opposite sides of the stage, separated by the other instruments — timpani, bass drum, cymbals, suspended cymbal, side-drums (with and without snare), tam-tam, xylophone, harp, celesta, and piano — whose respective positions are also carefully indicated. Spatial effect is heightened by dynamic nuances and contrasts between the individual instruments, form an essential part of the concept, as do the unusual effects Bartók devised. Among these are drum glissandos and what has come to be known in standard repertoire now as the Bartók pizzicato, in which plucked strings is made to strike the fingerboard. What is most striking about this numerous devices, though, is not their novelty; it is how the new sounds, rather than an effect, not of precisely contrived order, but of sweeping spontaneity and emotional impact.

In a sense this music might be said to represent an extension of principles and techniques Bartók had used in the String Quartets Nos. 3 and 4. As in those works, "unified diversity" is achieved here through an organizational scheme in which the thematic content of the entire work is developed from that of the first movement. That movement in this case is a restless yet mystical-sonding fugue, in which successive entries are governed by a tonal scheme, with frequent changes of meter, a climax is reached, and with the entry of the celesta, the music falls back to its opening mood — with an inversion of the theme.

In contrast to the introspective nature of the first movement, the second is aggressively energetic.

In 1923 Bartók and Kodály were commissioned by the Hungarian Government to compose orchestral pieces for the celebrations of the union of Buda, Pest, and Óbuda. Kodály wrote his famous *Psalmus Hungaricus*, and Bartók's contribution was the lively *Dance Suite*. Dohnányi, also a contributor to the event, conducted the first performances.

Within the context of this score, *Dance Suite* displays a transitional style in the sense that it stands midway between his more recognizable pre-1920s style and yet looks ahead to his mature post-1930s works. A brilliant concert piece, it portrays the rhythmic vitality so prevalent in all of Bartók's music throughout his career, and at the same time displays Stravinsky's influence on him at this time. There are five sections in the piece, and the themes are entirely in folk nature, though not actual quotations of folksongs. There is use of dance motives, and even the scoring suggests certain folk instruments.

The first movement (or dance) displays a rigorous theme taken by the bassoon that, after being joined by the other instruments, reaches a climax and then gives way to a tranquil theme announced by the oboe. This rather meditative episode ends with a solo clarinet passage.

The second movement is characterized by energetic trombone glissandi, and ends peacefully, as does the first movement.

The third movement is folklike in nature, reminiscent of Bartók's earlier Romanian folk dances. It ends brilliantly (*Vivacissimo*).

The fourth dance opens with an interesting parallel fourths, and is long and sectional. It utilizes all of the previous themes and several new ones, bringing the work together in an orderly, cohesive fashion.

Two years after the completion of his ballet *The Wooden Prince*, Bartók composed a suite (or more exactly a mime-play) called *The Miraculous Mandarin*. He obtained the libretto from Melcher Lengyel rather than Béla Balázs, whose advanced political ideas had, he thought, provoked the attacks of the *Bluebeard's Castle* production, and which Bartók deeply abhorred. (At the time, it was the "immorality of the scene" that was in question. The first performance at Cologne in 1926 raised a storm of protest; after a second attempt, at Prague in 1927, Bartók had withdrawn the work, and it was not until after his death that it was performed again, first in Budapest in 1945 and then in New York in 1951. The American choreographer Todd Bolender (who had created *Mother Goose* in 1942), foresaw that the public would react adversely to the depiction of the prostitution, the beating and the strangling of the same mandarin in *Jeune Homme et la Mort*. But he boldly disregarded this, all the more since the work contains a passionate and dramatic story-line libretto, in which he has also reflected the disruption of his own existence at this troubled time.)

As with *The Wooden Prince*, Bartók wished to make an orchestral suite of *The Miraculous Mandarin*, and to avoid the difficulties of the mime play, he made a short concert suite. The work premiered at Cologne in 1926, then in Budapest in 1928 (under the direction of Ernő Dohnányi), met, from then on, with complete acceptance.

In the opening part of a street in a large city, three tramps force a girl to waylay passers-by so that they may rob them. After two uninteresting victims, there appears an astonishing Mandarin; his behavior, now impassive, now excited, disconcerts the girl, who tries to escape, but he hotly pursues her. The tramps rob him and try to suffocate him by holding his nose and then by strangling him, then by running him through with a sword, and finally by hanging him. But he survives all their attempts. It is only when the girl shows pity for him that his wounds bleed and he dies at last.

It is likely, too, that in that tense summer of 1939 (the German invasion of Poland ignited World War II exactly two weeks after Bartók completed this score) Bartók, who was already planning his departure from Europe, under such a work to let his own spirits rise. In any case, he was off from his usual pattern as possible: he worked on this composition not in Budapest but in the tranquil setting of Saanen, a fairy-tale Swiss town in which Sacher had provided him with a villa. He did not look at a newspaper; instead that entire period was devoted entirely to music. He wrote to his wife: "... Luckily I can banish ... anxiety-provoked thoughts. ... While I am at work my doesn't disturb me."

The *Divertimento* for work bespeaks its character: outgoing, hearty, extremely good-humored in its outer movements, and yet by no means frivolous in any section. The opening has an especially early feeling, as of a foursquare peasant dance in which the melody simply grows out of the rhythms itself. Numerous imitative devices are employed: canon, inversion, and rhythmic and harmonies; a three-note figure from the end of the first theme reappears now and then as a kind of ritornello throughout the first movement. The strings are often playing each other or alternate with the full ensemble in some passages; the mood passes from merrily introspective to boldly declamatory, the latter predominating in the recapitulation, which "winds up" the music in a frenzied, whirling dance.

The slow movement is somewhat more inward and profound than the two outer ones. It is another example of the composer's "night music," though less exotic than many of its predecessors. In this respect, it resembles the slow movement of the *Concerto for Orchestra*; it is predominantly ruminative; the music then becomes more animated, with piercing little jabs and a profoundly Hungarian flavor. A somewhat anguished climax is reached, and then contentment returns. The various instrumental groups alternate in almost chamber music fashion, as solos from the ensemble until at last the emotional storm passes and the movement ends calmly. It is hardly far-fetched to find the seeds of the central *Elegia* of the *Concerto for Orchestra* in this poignant piece.

## 作品说明 - 第 8 页

**Princess has eyes only for this wooden prince, and it is with it that she dances. Then the Fairy reverses the roles; the Princess desires the wooden doll and not the doll, but he spurns her attention. The trees come to life and bar her way, so then casts off her crown and cloak, cuts her own hair, and humbles herself before him. Finally the Prince and Princess are reunited, the surrounding objects resume their normal appearance and everything returns to normal. Through this charming tale, Béla Balázs wished to show that people are always satisfied with appearances; they only penetrate to the true nature of things if the fairies, forcing them to make necessary sacrifices, oblige them to open their eyes.**

The orchestral suite, which opens pianissimo with the basses and timpani, shows us the Princess at play. Then follows the "Dance of the Princess," which is accompanied by a giocoso recitative on the clarinet, counterpointed by the flutes over the pizzicato of the strings. The "Dance of the Trees" is introduced by rapid runs on the muted strings and the woodwind; the violence of the dance on the drums rapidly brings the orchestra to its full volume, but the violence of the flood gradually dies away to a slow and gentle movement on the harps. The episode of the Prince and his wooden doll is rendered by a passage in a most original melodic vein that is picturesquely written (ornamentation, grace notes, pizzicati, col legno bowing, etc.). Next, "Dance of the Waves" is introduced by the trills on the woodwind and harp, filled out by the celesta, played by four hands above sustained notes on the strings; the mighty forces of the waters is seen rumbling in an immense gelatin from the whole orchestra. Then follows the "Dance of the Princess" with the wooden doll, in a dazzling variety of tone-colors and percussion (xylophone, cymbals, castanets, side drum, bass drum, etc.). Finally, the "Postlude" indicates that the fairy is going to put everything right again. A peaceful air is sustained notes on the woodwind and strings, punctuated by airy arpeggios on the harps.

**Colin Mason**
