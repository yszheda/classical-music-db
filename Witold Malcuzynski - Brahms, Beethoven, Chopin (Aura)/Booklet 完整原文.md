## HONOR THE ARTISTS
*by Piero Rattalino*

Today, a CD must not last more than eighty minutes. Therefore, the number of times I was compelled to eliminate something from recitals that exceeded eighty minutes, even if only by a small margin, and I was often embarrassed about having to make these editorial decisions.

Concerning this recital in Locarno by Witold Malczynski, I was neither hesitant nor doubtful: I eliminated the initial excerpt, Bach's Chromatic Fantasy and fugue. The reason: because Malczynski often displayed nervousness in this excerpt and defended himself only by a professionalism. It was to be expected by a performer of his calibre, essentially Chopinian pianist who did not arrive in terms of counterpoint, it was important for him to perform in this way, at the beginning of a concert, was stressed more than ever and made mistakes more easily. I dwelled upon this detail because I wanted the listener to notice the structure of Malczynski's program, quite singular for a pianist born in 1914: Bach at the beginning, then Brahms, then Beethoven.

Anyone who attends concerts knows that in fact the most powerful order of the critics, and order under some respects, is the chronological one and that, in any case, the classic Composers must not be mixed with the Romantic composers. Usually Malczynski respected this canonical rule: during his debut recital at the Carnegie Hall in New York on April 20, 1942, he performed Bach's Chromatic fantasy and fugue followed by Beethoven's Appassionata, then passing to Chopin (Nocturne op. 48 n. 1, Sonata op. 35), and then inserted the Czaczewski Sonatina op. 10 n. 2, Variations on 25 in Poland Ignaz Jan Paderewski, who had died in New York less than a year before, and lastly he returned to his favorite, Chopin (Mazurka op. 56 n. 2, Scherzo op. 31). And also on 29 November 1959 at the New York Philharmonic Hall, he played Chopin (Mazurka op. 10 n. 4, Ballata op. 47, Appassionata for the first part, began the second part with Brahms (Intermezzo op. 118 n. 6, Rhapsody op. 79 n. 2) and continued with Chopin (Nocturne op. 48 n. 1, Ballade op. 47, Three Mazurkas, Waltz, Scherzo op. 39). However there must have been something that did not satisfy him in the Bach-Beethoven succession: if in Ascona in 1963, in Ponam in 1964, and probably also in other cities, he fits Brahms between Bach and Beethoven.

Generally, this is what the pianists belonging to two generations before him would do, as they frequently inserted one or two "small" compositions between two "big" ones. And in truth Malczynski was somewhat an old-fashioned pianist who placed the moment's inspiration before all else and who did not pay much attention to precision or, another likely supposition, did not allow his impulses constraining for the better of him.

When he enters in the Rhapsody op. 79 n. 2 of Brahms he will hear, right in the first beat, two wrong notes struck with fingers touching not only the right keys but ones that are keys. Those two notes are played by the left hand, which crosses the right hand, passing under a thumb. It is not considered a very easy position but it is not difficult either. It is simply a dangerous or uncomfortable, particularly for an international concert musician who was certainly not lacking in experience. Malczynski stroked those two notes wrong because he was bored, stirred by an impetuous and excitable music, makes a gesture that is too vivacious, too quick; and the musical significance is correct, two notes are wrong. Then, once into the game, he continues on this road, but the mistakes (at least not always), but when he is exposed, when the beginning is proposed again, when the end is approached, the hand loses its ease, he makes the same rash gesture and makes the same mistake he made the first time. Neither is the rest of the program impeccable. At the contrary, the moment's inspiration is there; the clear sky is darkened by passing clouds is not infrequent, and the end of the program, when he is most tired, is when he reaches his best. A hail of a storm, which fortunately is quickly dispersed. The pianists of Malczynski's generation were less subject to the storm's winds than the contemporary pianists of today's; almost always for no reason or control. But Malczynski was an artist and experienced the storm as it is experienced today in a world in which many others surpassed him both from a technical and a virtuoso point of view. He ranked fifth ex-aequo in 1936 at the Vienna Contest (won by Yakov Zak).

## ONORARE GLI ARTISTI
*di Piero Rattalino*

Il CD ha oggi una durata che non deve superare gli ottanta minuti, e a mano a mano che gli anni passano la tendenza e quella di aumentare i percorsi di non buona resa del micro. Mi è perciò capitato più volte di dover tagliare qualcosa di recitals che superavano anche di poco i sessanta minuti, e spesso di questa operazione di forbici, mi sono sentito imbarazzato. Nel caso di questo recital di Accore di Witold Malczynski non ho avuto né esitazioni né dubbi: ho eliminato il brano iniziale, la Fantasia cromatica e fuga di Bach, perché Malczynski mostrava in essa panie-re tracce di disagio e si salvava col mestiere. C'era da aspettarselo, da un pianista come lui, da un pianista essenzialmente chopiniano che era approdato tardivamente, almeno in rapporto alla struttura del programma di Malczynski, molto singolare in un pianista di idee da artista. La nostra epoca è stata ed è ricca di pianisti. Non altrettanto è stata ed è ricca di artisti: non dimentichiamoci di dar loro il posto che a loro spetta.

## Biographical Information

During the Chopin Contest he met the French pianist Colette Gaveau (who ranked seventh). Together they made plans to go to France. He escaped when France went to war; first he went to Portugal, then Argentina (where he was extremely successful, then the United States; he made his debut in New York, April 20, 1942 at the Carnegie Hall where he returned on the 19th of October of that same year. He appeared in the main American, Latin American and European concert halls. He arrived in 1945 made his debut in London, picked up his concert activity in Europe and played on a regular basis for thirty years around the world. His last appearance at the Carnegie Hall took place on May 18, 1971 (an all-Chopin program: Polonaise op. 26 n. 2, Fantasy op. 49, Nocturne op. 48 n. 1, Scherzo op. 39, Polonaise op. 44, Four Mazurkas, Nocturne op. 15 n. 1, Scherzo op. 31).
