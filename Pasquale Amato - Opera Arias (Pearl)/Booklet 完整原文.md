## Back Cover

**PASQUALE AMATO**

One of the incredible pantheon of Italian baritones, Amato's talents were early noticed by Gatti-Casazza of the Met in New York. Amato spent 12 seasons there, creating Jack Rance in *La Fanciulla* and was acclaimed for his bell-like plangency and precise focus, his diction, trenchancy and smooth legato, as well as for the very wide range of parts he sang. This excitement more than deserves to be heard again today.

## Liner Notes by Charles Haynes (Pages 4-6)

**PASQUALE AMATO**

Of all the great Italian baritones, the story of Pasquale Amato is one of the most sad. Blessed with every virtue for which a singer/actor could wish, he over-used his voice and so unwisely destroyed it and his career, was in effect, over in a short time he had reached his early forties.

Amato's young life seems to have presented few problems, for he was early enrolled at the Naples Conservatory in his native city (he had been born in Naples on 21 March 1876) to study with signorina La Manna. He studied first under Carelli, and then Lombardi, another of whose pupils was de Lucia. His début took place at the Teatro Bellini, also in Naples, when in September 1900 he was heard as Germonet père. Amato then broadened his experience with a wide variety of additional engagements. He went to Milan, Genoa and Germany then, in the summer of 1904, made a tour of South America. Later in the same year he visited Covent Garden to compete with Sammarco, but he does not seem to have made any great impression then, for he never returned.

Through the influence of Toscanini, Amato was engaged at La Scala for the 1907/8 season where he impressed in a number of standard rôles, and was Golaud in Debussy's *Pelléas et Mélisande* when that opera was heard for the first time at La Scala. It was during this period that Gatti-Casazza, recently appointed Artistic Director of the Metropolitan Opera, New York, heard Amato and determined to take the talented young baritone with him. Thus it came to pass that, on 20 November 1908, in his first house, he repeated his original début as Germonet, in a cast which also included Sembrich and Caruso. He was immediately acclaimed, and remained with the company for twelve seasons.

It is said that Amato sang 446 performances embracing thirty-four rôles, an astonishing number for a man with a big voice who used it as such. Little wonder then that some deterioration was soon to set in, and that after only some five years a prominent critic was able to write: "his voice has acquired a harshness and his intonation has become less sure."

During Amato's lengthy period with the Metropolitan his repertoire was seen to be expanding at an amazing variety of parts. Apart from 'standard fare', it included appearances in Puccini's *La Fille de la West*, Gluck's *Armide*, Franchetti's *Germania*, Mascagni's *Lodoletta*, Montemezzi's *L'amore de tre Re*, Zandonai's *Francesca da Rimini*, Massenet's *Thaïs*, Bernadin Pons's *Roi d'Harape* most remarkably, he created the part of Jack Rance in Puccini's *La Fanciulla del West*. This taxing variety of parts, and the frequency with which he sang them, inevitably led to a major vocal disaster.

And so it happened that by 1921 he was no longer required at the Metropolitan – this at the age of only 45. The last years were patchy. He sang elsewhere in America, sometimes with other regional troupes, but failed his morale sufficiently to give a huge concert at the New York Hippodrome in celebration of the twenty-fifth anniversary of his Metropolitan début. The final years were spent teaching in New York, and as a Professor at Louisiana University. Amato died in New York on 12 August 1942.

As to the quality of the artistry we can offer – apart of course, the opinions which listen on – can speak for themselves – various comments. The "Metropolitan Opera Encyclopedia" (whose views must be well-informed) states that he had a voice of wide compass, with a brilliant top register, his diction, tone production and phrasing were considered classic, and his dramatic talent ranged from the humorous to the ferocious. Thompson's *Cyclopædia* mentions his power, range and brilliance.

For a fully comprehensive assessment, however, one must – as so often – to the most knowledgeable Michael Scott writing in *The Record of Singing* (Duckworth, 1977, repts), "Amato's was a splendidly firm and ringing voice in his best days and up to high A-flat, with a great impression. On records it is almost impossible to confuse it with any other baritone, for there is a bell-like and plangent quality this is wholly individual, a rapid but narrow vibrato... the scale is always correctly developed, and the tone always precisely focused... Amato's style resisted the excesses of *verismo*, exposure to Mascagni, Leoncavallo, etc. did not prevent him from making a proper effort for Verdi's music. *Eri tu* and *Cortigiani* (both included here) are justly famous interpretations in which the unusual pure vowels, which have never covered even on the highest notes, make each word clear and telling, alike in trenchant declamation and smooth legato. The Doge's monologue singing, the high C coming out just as Verdi ordered *di dolce*."

In the compilation Amato is joined by some very distinguished colleagues; and a few words about each seems in order, exception being being Caruso, who might call himself 'obvious'.

The French bass Marcel Journet (1867-1933) enjoyed a lengthy global career, singing a wide variety of parts. His Metropolitan début took place late in 1900, and so his connection with Amato would have dated from some eight years later. Journet was notably particular as the interpreter of French music. Margarete Matzenauer (1881-1963), of Hungarian birth, came from what is now Romania. From various international appearances in Europe, she joined the Metropolitan in 1911 – thirty years after Amato – where she remained for nineteen seasons, tackling a wide variety of parts including all the Wagner rôles written by Verdi and Wagner – which activities eventually encouraged the company to engage the soprano Johanna Gadski (1872-1932) made her Metropolitan début as early as 1895; with the touring Damrosch Company, where she sang many Wagner. After working at Bayreuth and Covent Garden, she settled with the main Metropolitan company, where she sang until 1917. Gadski was equally at home in Wagner and Verdi (encouraged by Gatti-Casazza) and was noted for her fluency, pure tone and power.

All the 'HMV' records included here were actually American Victor pressings for the Victor company for British issue. Except when identity is specified, orchestras may be assumed to be the 'house' one of Victor's. William Rogers was for many years the principal conductor. Lapitino the harp obbligato. Much of the material to be heard here is familiar enough and hardly in need of introduction, though there is just one exception: *Votre Santé* (from *La Favorita* – Argentina 1914), is a tragedy set in Venice in 1457. It revolves around a conspiracy to murder the Doge Foscari to keep the public of the opinion that his son is guilty of a crime which he never committed. The old man eventually dies of a broken heart. We hear his *romanza* from Act One.

**CHARLES HAYNES**
