## FOREWORD

I am a lover of Russian music, and Medtner is a particular favourite. His *Improvisation* is a wonderful opportunity to inject spirit and powerful imagination into a performance.

Scriabin's *Black Mass* sonata is a conversation between human and demon, which is a hugely powerful and inspiring theme and provides me with a great range of interpretive colours for the music.

Music is a vital and special art form, and a disc of piano music wouldn't be complete without a piece by Franz Liszt. His *Variations* is for me his finest work, and was inspired by the composer losing his daughter and finding a balance between his love for God and feeling that he wanted to blame God for his pain.

The Rachmaninoff *Transcription* is my encore for the disc. I am very grateful to have been able to make this recording as a result of winning the second Sussex International Piano Competition; performing and recording in the beautiful Music Room at Champs Hill has been a real pleasure.

*Poom Prommachart*

## PROGRAMME NOTE

Russian cultural history is routinely presented as a tale of opposing forces, of striking contrasts between high and low, radical and conservative, the timeless and the transient. Western commentators are not alone in dividing the story into clean factions: Russian historians have also looked for and found neat dualities, often used to highlight Russia's difference from the rest. In fact the boundary lines of the nation's art and culture were far from clearly defined in the decades before the October Revolution of 1917 and remained so for some time after the Bolsheviks demolished the old tsarist order. The breadth and depth of artistic drives at large in Russia during the dying days of the Romanov dynasty are reflected in this album's programme, which embraces everything from diehard conservatism of Medtner to the introspective mysticism and harmonic daring of late Scriabin.

Alexander Scriabin was raised in Moscow at a time when diverse philosophies competed for space, some encouraged by the ruling elite, others repressed with brute force. Born into a family of noble descent, his mother was among the first female musicians to be recognised as such in Russia. Following her death in 1872, the infant's upbringing was entrusted to his grandmothers and an infatuated aunt, herself an accomplished amateur musician. The boy was taken to the Russian Musical Society, founded as a vehicle to boost Moscow's concert life and music education, and accompanied his aunt to operas at the Bolshoi. By the age of five he could memorise the melodies he heard in performance and was encouraged to improvise variations on them at the keyboard. Scriabin's childhood development under the pampering, near-obsessive care of its female relatives was saturated with experiences of storytelling and making art. It came as a surprise, not least to his long-absent diplomat father, when the eleven-year-old decided to join the Second Moscow Cadet Corps. The physically weak youngster's exploits at the piano were much admired by his fellow cadets: he also became notorious as the only boy never to don a gun during his five years of preparatory training for the army.

Scriabin, who was declared unfit for military service, enrolled at the Moscow Conservatory in 1888. He was recognised among the institution's finest pianists, a considerable achievement given the depth of its talent pool, and made his mark as a published composer around the time of his graduation in 1892. Scriabin's early piano works owe much to the influence of Chopin and Liszt. His musical language evolved in the early 1900s and soon began to colonise fresh harmonic territories. The process of change flowed through a series of short pieces created between 1904 and 1908, and was shaped in part by the composer's reading of Helena Blavatsky's *The Secret Doctrine*, a syncretic blend of ancient esoteric occult practices, mysticism, and critical reactions to Darwin and modern 'materialist' science.

Scriabin's deep immersion in esoteric philosophy and the related process of spiritual awakening became integral to his art. It has been suggested that the upward rising contours of his late works, coupled with stillnesses and concentration, represent the composer's intent to transcend mundane cares and cultivate unity with something beyond the ego, the plenum or totality of divine powers. His Ninth Piano Sonata of 1912-13 explores the mind's shadow side, drawing dark forces from the light of awareness. Its opening motif, stated in the right hand and echoed by the left, is built from the consecutive tritone interval, the so-called 'Devil in music'. Scriabin's friend Alexey Podgayevsky, an amateur musician and theosophist, responded to the work's harmonic ambiguity and sensual fervour by calling it a *Black Mass*, the antithesis of the Seventh Piano Sonata, which the composer had subtitled *White Mass*. Scriabin weaves disintegration and fracture into the sonata's fabric. Although the single-movement *Black Mass* is sectional in form, with the tritone motif returning at the midway point and at the work's conclusion, the music's endless variety consistently provokes any sense of structural stability. The work's sudden shifts and contrasts recall the mysterious contours of a dream, like the repetition of some atavistic trauma.

The Medtner family, in common with so many among Moscow's merchant class, were unprepared for the shock of revolution and counter-revolution. Nicolas Medtner's formative years were conditioned by a conservative outlook that ruled everything from its cultural tastes to moral codes. The latter were tested in the summer of 1903 when the composer told his eldest brother Emil that he was in love with Emil's wife Anna, a feeling the latter and Emil Medtner was initiated in Germany following the outbreak of the First World War and was subsequently granted a visa to enter neutral Switzerland. In Zurich he became a patient of Carl Gustav Jung and went on to influence the analytical psychologist's pioneering work on archetypes. Emil quietly agreed to divorce Anna and accept her eventual remarriage to Nicolas, which took place in June 1919 following the death of the brothers' mother.

Although Medtner privately despised Lenin's Bolshevik regime, he agreed to join the advisory board of the musical section of Narkompros, the People's Commissariat for Education, a body that oversaw a smart college that certainly guaranteed the visas that allowed the Medtners to enter Germany in 1921 and set up home in Berlin. They left behind a country hard hit by international sanctions, civil war and starvation. The composer planned to revive his career as a pianist in Europe and earn enough money to return home as soon as possible. In fact he struggled to secure enough engagements and was further demonised by the dismissive reaction of Berlin's critics to his music. Medtner described Berlin as 'a foul den of vice' and later wrote of his dismay at the city's support for composers such as Schoenberg, Alban and Schoenberg. He also criticised Stravinsky, Prokofiev and a litany of fellow *émigrés* for betraying eternal values of beauty that lived in Russian souls.

A twelve-concert tour of the United States in 1924-5 and subsequent move to Paris only intensified Medtner's longing for home. The prospect of compromise, of trimming his sails to new trends in composition, never entered his mind: 'In my genes,' he wrote to his brother Alexander, 'every contemptible circumstance of life is strongly conductive to making the artist either fall silent or produce the kind of output with which the Muses flee in horror.' So Medtner continued to compose but, like so many Russians in exile, struggled to adapt to life in a world unrecognisable from that in which he had been raised.

Following their return from America in April 1935, the Medtners rented a cottage in the peaceful Vallée de Chevreuse, a short distance from Versailles and close to Rachmaninoff's summer home at nearby Orsay. Medtner began work on the second of his two *Improvisations* in June and completed the score in September. The composition, a set of freely developed variations on a theme, evolves shades of Liszt and Chopin, the former strongly present in the seventh variation; the latter in the turbulent passagework of Variation fifteen. Medtner caught the mood of the theme and each of its variations in his choice of titles, circumscribing a broad imaginative space for the listener to populate with ruskalki, ghostly water-spirits whose siren songs lured young men to their deaths by drowning. He may have had Lermontov's romantic poem *Rusalka* in mind while creating his *Song of the Water-Nymph*. The piece certainly connects with popular Russian myths about the spirits of place.

Medtner opens with the water-nymph's wordless song, black in its opening bars, more alluring than forbidding. The opening theme reflects on what has gone before, becoming more agitated in character and giving way to the charm of a beautifully crafted dance. Relentless rhythmic energy and sensual allure belong to the composer's character study of birds in *Winged Dancers*, while the intricacy of the fourth variation's part-writing underlines its enchanted spirit. Variation five revels in the amusing hesitancy of music peppered with rests, staccato chords, and teasing reference to the water-nymph's song, while the sixth variation unleashes a fleeting floodtide of triplet semiquavers. *Tumult of the Crowd* inspired by improvisatory and frenetic streets of Moscow, followed by *Humoresque* the seventh variation's heroic course, apparently unstoppable, is blocked by an improvisatory coda, which in turn leads by the chromatic instability of *In the Forest*. Medtner here creates a labyrinthine world, ambiguous in terms of its harmony and mysterious in nature. *The Swan*, *The Elves* and *The Gnomes* share traits common to Medtner's piano *skazki* or 'tales'; short pieces noted in the fertile soil of Russian folklore, while the arresting melody and sumptuous chordal harmonies of *Conjuration* provide intimations of Orthodox choral chant. Breathless phrasing and restless figuration sustain and intensify *The Threat*, among the work's longest variations; its undulating lines proving anything but tranquil. *The Song of the Water-Nymph* and remains present throughout the penultimate variation's lugubrious course. Bold restatement of the work's theme in the pianist's right- and left-hand parts surface in *The Storm*, the uninhibited, often-violent character of which passes into memory as Medtner's *Conclusion* retrieves consoling echoes of *Conjuration*.

## POOM PROMMACHART

A recent Shigeru Kawai Artist, Bangkok-born Poom Prommachart is regarded as one of the most accomplished Thai pianists today. Live and in rare musical talent, after which the daily newspaper Dnevnik from Novi Sad wrote: 'Playing with a mixture of controlled freedom and exceptional depth of expression, with rounded phrases and rich decorations of "Oriental" kind of ornament, beginning deep in his piano and a great dynamic range, purified in style and expression, the pianist from Thailand has confirmed that the first prize and title of the laureate of the whole competition have come into the right hands.'

In addition to an outright win in Novi Sad, Poom has also won many prizes including 1st prize and audience prize at the UK International Sheepdip Piano Competition (2009), 1st prize at the Eastbourne Symphony Orchestra Soloist Competition (2008), 2nd prize and a special prize for the best performance of Liszt at the International Chopin Piano Competition in Budapest (2008) and 1st prize at the Thailand International Piano Competition (2011). He was also awarded 1st Prize at the Grand Final of the Sussex International Piano Competition (2013) where he performed Rachmaninoff's Piano Concerto No.3 with the Worthing Symphony Orchestra, conducted by John Gibbons in the final round.

He entered the Nanyang Academy of Fine Arts in Singapore for his piano studies with Ong Lip Tat, Rena Pua and Boris Kofman. After a year in Singapore, he was granted a full scholarship by HRH Princess Galyani Vadhana Krom Luang Naradhiwas Rajanagarindra which enabled him to continue his studies in London where he was the last student of the legendary pianist Yonty Solomon in 2008. After that, he studied with Ian Jones and later with Dina Parakhina.

He is the Artist in Residence of Concerto Kultuurgemeente, Berlin, Germany.
