### Page 4-5: TORTELIER AND THE ELGAR CONCERTO
**by Tully Potter, 1993**

No cellist, not even Jacqueline du Pré, had a closer connection with the Elgar Cello Concerto than Paul Tortelier. He played the first two movements in his graduation concert at the Paris Conservatoire, when he was 16, and the work was always central to his repertoire. He recorded it with three musical knights, Malcolm Sargent, Adrian Boult and Charles Groves. His live performances with Boult were memorable: but their studio collaboration was quite so inspired and this first effort with Sargent is to be preferred. Tortelier's interpretation, lithe and inward-looking, stood in direct contrast to the extrovert, heart-on-sleeve reading of du Pré—who incidentally studied with him for a time. His way with the work appealed to the classicists, while du Pré's was more to the romanticists. Either view was equally valid, since the arch-romanticist Elgar showed himself at his most classical in this, his last major orchestral work.

Even as a teenager, Tortelier was developing his unique intellectual interpretation of the Elgar. He was a devoted admirer of Pablo Casals; indeed, of all the cellists who fell under Casals' spell, he came closest to the spirit of the Catalan master's music-making—the two men's different total profiles, with Tortelier's more sinewy sound reflecting his sparer frame, did not obscure their basic affinity. Yet the younger cellist was always able to diverge from Casals' interpretations and strike out on his own path. When it came to the Elgar Concerto, the older generation—represented on record by Casals, W. H. Squire and Beatrice Harrison—played the 9/8 Moderato theme of the first movement with much *glissando* or *portamento*, and Jacqueline du Pré unabashedly followed their example. Tortelier wished to avoid this effect, feeling that the theme should be executed with the greatest purity. He therefore, with the help of the principal cellist at a Parisian theatre where he was then moonlighting, worked out his own fingering, different from that of his teacher at the Conservatoire, Gérard Hekking. 'Am blessed with a large hand,' he told David Blum in their 1984 book of conversations, 'I was able to devise a special fingering which enabled me, by the use of extensions, to avoid all slides.' The result, as Blum saw it, was that whereas Casals played the Moderato as 'a series of sighs, with a rise and fall of nuance and expressively judged glissandi', Tortelier created 'an entirely different atmosphere ... calm and ethereal'. For the Scherzo, a technically challenging piece demanding supreme control of the *sautillé* to a rapid, springy bow stroke in which the hair hardly leaves the string. Tortelier contrived to minimise his left-hand movements so that he could concentrate on the bowing.

When he added the last two movements to his repertoire and played the Cello concerto complete, Tortelier liked to use the supple flexibility of steel, so he concentrated on sustaining a singing line of limpid freshness and inventiveness. He never 'pulled about' in the romantic style. For the Finale, he again scaled new heights of virtuosity, bringing his bow off the strings to lighten the speed required for the contemplative passages. The result was a performance of alternating fire and sensibility, which was always in perfect taste and attentive to the many markings with which Elgar spelt out exactly how his springing bowing should be done.

Sargent caught this interpretation at a key point in its development—when Tortelier had already considerably refined it, but had not lost none of his Concertgebouw spontaneity. For it is possible, when considering the work, to overplay the autumnal mood and fall too much into the disillusionment which Elgar undoubtedly felt in the aftermath of the First World War. In this reading, the Cello Concerto has another, tragic side: but it is also a work of brilliance and concision, in which Elgar summed up his formal mastery and so passionately accentuated its content. It brings a perfect poise between the Late Romantic excess of Mahler and the advanced Classical developments of Brahms. It is the crowning work of a great composer and in Tortelier, it found a peerless interpreter.

Paul Tortelier was born in Montmartre, Paris on 21 March 1914 into quite a poor family: my father was a cabinet-maker and it was his mother who decided his future—the heard Francis Touché play in a café and determined that when she had a son, they should be a cellist. From the age of six, Paul was cajoled by her to practice two hours every day. His teacher Beatrice Blum gave him a good start and at nine he went to Louis Feuillard, his mother taking in boarders to pay for the lessons. At 12 he entered Feuillard's class at the Paris Conservatoire and at 14 transferred to Hekking; in 1930 he took a first prize with the Elgar performance. Even as a boy he had played in a cinema at weekends, and now he earned his living in cafés and restaurants and began to freelance in the best ensembles, playing within Toscanini on one occasion. His solo debut was made at the Concerts Lamoureux with the Lalo concerto but, egged on by his mother, he returned to the Conservatoire for three years to study harmony with Jean Gallon and counterpoint with Noël Gallon. Sharing the first prize in 1935 with Henri Dutilleux. That year he was made first-principal in Monte Carlo under Toscanini, Walter and Mitropoulos and in 1937 performed Don Quixote under Strauss's baton. He then joined the Boston Symphony's cello bench until, in 1940, back in German-occupied Paris he married, but that relationship, which produced one daughter, ended in divorce. After the Liberation he became principal of the Orchestre de la Société des Concerts du Conservatoire, deputising Charles Munch, and taught the cello; Maud Martin, who in 1946 became his second wife - of their three musical children, the violinist and conductor Yan Pascal Tortelier is the most famous. Tortelier's career began in earnest with Beecham performed Jean Hubeau's Concerto in Amsterdam in 1946 - this led to his 1947 London performance of Don Quixote, in the composer's presence. The cellist became more popular in Britain than in France and was a constant visitor until his death. In 1950 he led the cellos in the first Pades Festival and he returned for several years to those summer events, forging ever closer links with Elgar's music. The apex of Tortelier's career came in the 1960s and fell abuzz in the mid-1950s. The extrovert side came out in the TV masterclasses which made him a British institution. He taught at the Paris Conservatoire from 1956 to 1969 and at the Salzburg Mozarteum from 1969 to 1975. He also visited Peking to give classes. He was teaching at Manor de Villarsceaux that he died suddenly on 18 December 1990.

### Page 6-7: THE DREAM OF GERONTIUS—A UNIQUE RECORDING
**by Jerrold Northrop Moore, 1975**

This is the first complete recording of *The Dream of Gerontius* ever made, and it came into being at a strange moment. It was the Spring of 1945. The European War was nearing its end—the war which had changed irrevocably so much of what had stood for the inherited values, the old ideals. The ebb of the war was beginning to reveal dimensions of psychological damage which had been done by more than half a decade of general organised violence. It was a time for finding and picking up whatever the storm might have left, for trying to use anything that could be used toward whatever life might now be made. "His Master's Voice" and the British Council combined resources to preserve Elgar's *Dream of Gerontius* in a performance that would call upon the skills and inspirations of the national musical life at this moment eleven years after the composer's death.

They could not have chosen better. *Gerontius*, that unique hymn to Death and Change, must memorably have gathered and shaped the mood of such an hour. Written in 1899 and 1900, it was, of course, a fragment from the Old World whose renaissance lay all about in the Spring of 1945. Yet *The Dream of Gerontius* was itself the quintessential expression of a man lacerated by the winds of change blowing through his own time. J. B. Priestley, who knew Elgar the man as well as the time in which he lived, caught this very well:

> Elgar is essentially Edwardian, not because of his pomps and circumstances and his increasing enjoyment—though perhaps, better, his enjoyment of his wife's enjoyment—of the society of important personages and a fine style of living; but because there is in him and his music all the rich confusion of this age, the deepening doubt, the melancholy whispers from the unconscious, as well as all that hope and glory.
> (p. 138, *The Edwardians*, Heinemann, 1970)

But rich confusion seeks artistic expression to resolve as well as to express itself. And Elgar's very choice of subject took him some way toward both expression and resolution. The composer, with an open commitment to write a major work for the Birmingham Festival in 1900, had been drawn to two subjects, both of which had been simmering in his mind for years. One was something about the Apostles who followed Christ and who were left to live out their lives after the Crucifixion. The other was Cardinal Newman's poem of experience of dying and death, *The Dream of Gerontius*. Death and Change dominate both these subjects; but the point of view is entirely different. A work about the Apostles would be an affirmative statement to the older world to contemplate empty-handed a vital essence gone out of it. With *Gerontius* Newman saw that life began at death; it was God who was the source of life. *Gerontius* before Pope Innocent shows how right Priestley is in seeing the strength of the poem in its appeal to an artist creatively on the make. Evolving attempts could be said at last to have been enough to meet the demands.

In making his abridgment of Newman's poem, Elgar strongly emphasised the seventh. Newman said so much in Part Two that Elgar thought the work should be seen dealing with his post-mortal experiences. The composer cut very little from the opening, but he ruthlessly telescoped the remaining six divisions into something like more than ever before. The emphasis might seem to be on the life that had to be abandoned—was raised enormously in relative importance. And with it, the twin processes of Death and Change became very much clearer. But then the very notion of setting such a thing to such music, with solos for the separate characters, must bring up enormously the human element of drama, confrontation, relationship. That's the special music of just those elements of Gerontius has survived.

This then was the world that met with its first complete recording at Huddersfield on 8-12 April, 1945. There gathered on 8-12 April the Huddersfield Chorus with their chorus-master Herbert Bardgett (1884-1962); closed up their depleted ranks and set to work with the Liverpool Philharmonic Orchestra under its great conductor Sir Malcolm Sargent (1895-1967), acknowledged everywhere as the finest choral director of his day and an orchestral musician of superb poise. He brought with him the orchestra he regularly trained and conducted, the Liverpool Philharmonic.

Sargent and many of his players and singers could reconstitute in their memories performances under the direction of Elgar himself. So could the soloists. Norman Walker (1907-1963) was too young actually to have sung in festivals or concerts conducted by the great man, but Elgar was the leader as well as the figurehead of the great English musical profession during the Walker's youth and student days. The same was true for the other singers. Dennis Noble (1899-1966) and Gladys Ripley (1908-1955) had both appeared early in their careers with Elgar conducting their first. Elgar's own favourite Heddle Nash (1894-1961) preserved the closest connection. The idea that Nash should sing Gerontius was Elgar's own. He wrote to his wife on 26 May, 1933 to Nash about the forthcoming song recital, but Elgar ended his letter: "I hope to hear you sing Gerontius some day."

Elgar's hope was realised when Heddle Nash sang for the first time Gerontius at Croydon in 1931. When in 1945 the decision was made to record Elgar's inscrutable singer's copy: "Many thanks to Heddle Nash" Edward Elgar, Croydon, 10th November 1931. 'Gerontius' had been heard many times on the radio, on record and at the war's end, is to realise how greatly we have changed by events since 1945, since 1931, since 1900—and how little.

**© JERROLD NORTHROP MOORE, 1975**

Just before the end of the war when many souls, like that of Gerontius, were feeling pretty exhausted, HMV and the British Council refreshed music lovers with the first complete recording of *The Dream of Gerontius*. For those fortunate to have listened in the school library, everyone eager to hear what was then a pioneering effort, the first twentieth-century oratorio to be recorded complete. Transferred to LP in 1977, it still made a more vivid impression without the need for concentration-breaking pauses or 78rpm sides. Now, on CD, the sound is further improved in the sense that one hears not only what the performers did but what Elgar intended as both producer and his team.

What distinguished this set from all its successors is the fervour shown on all sides from the conductor, soloists, chorus and orchestra alike. Perhaps the wartime community spirit entered into the hearts of the Huddersfield singers; whatever the reason they sing with tremendous vigour allied to sensitivity, no doubt inspired by Sargent, then the leading conductor of chorus of his day and not often surpassed since. He knew and understood the meaning and feeling of Elgar's oratorio. He was brought up under the composer's influence, and his interpretation comes as close as anyone's to the composer's own view of the work, one that eschews the self-indulgence that can so easily lead to sentimentality. This is next best to having a complete recording from the composer's own baton. With him Sargent had his own orchestra, the Liverpool, always considered one of the country's finest bands, if not the finest, lucky to have in it many of the country's best instrumentalists who were not serving in the Forces. The string section is particularly eloquent.

As Jerrold Northrop Moore explains above, Elgar himself persuaded Heddle Nash, whose centenary is celebrated in 1994, to undertake the title role. Experienced in opera and oratorio alike he was ideal, eloquent, uninhibited and impassioned in expression, individual in his accents, above all wholly committed to the role, and having, as the contemporary critic, Richard Capell, commented, a peculiarly minted-like tone—perfect, for instance, for Elgar's *pianissimo* injection at "Mary, pray for me" and for the inwardness of "Novissima hora est". At the same time, Nash was quite free of both oratorio stuffiness evident in the work of the earlier interpreters and brings to both "Sanctus fortis" and "Take me away" an uninhibited thrust that matches that of the music. Above all, as many of Nash's contemporaries averred, he conveys the agony and ecstasy of Gerontius.

Nash was also a master of verbal values as you can hear at "Oh, what a heart-subsiding melody" as he first encounters the Angel. That role is here taken by Gladys Ripley whose mezzo is miles away from the contralto hoot then all too prevalent. Her full, flexible voice, and her treatment of the text is natural, clear, unforced. Sargent—I suspect perhaps Legge—insisted on having two soloists for the roles of Priest and Angel of the Agony, a valid and rewarding decision as Dennis Noble's dramatic baritone and dramatic experience are ideally suited to the Priest's opening words, higher lying than the Angel of the Agony's music that a warm, soft-grained yet urgent interpreter in Norman Walker, then the country's leading bass. Like their colleagues, they make the most of their words.

**© ALAN BLYTH, 1993**

### The Elgar Society

[Photograph caption]
3rd August, 1900: Elgar finishing the score of *The Dream of Gerontius* in the study at Birchwood Lodge, Malvern

**The Elgar Society**

The Elgar Society has, for many years, assisted in the production of new recordings and in the re-issue of historically valuable material. The opportunity to ensure that the first complete recording of *The Dream of Gerontius* was once again made available to a new generation of listeners was therefore one we accepted with enthusiasm. The inclusion of Paul Tortelier's first recording of Elgar's Cello Concerto reminds us of the reasons why this great Frenchman became so closely associated with this work.

Testament have ensured that some wonderful sounds have been extracted from the original source of these recordings. We look forward to working with them again in the future.

**Andrew Neill**
Chairman, 1993
