## Liner Notes by Lawrence F. Holdridge

### GUSTAVE CHARPENTIER

Gustave Charpentier, the composer whose opera *Louise* captured the essence of Parisian life in the 1890s, was born in Dieuze, Lorraine, on 25 June 1860. After the Franco-Prussian War and the imposition of German government, the family moved across the border to the French setting in Tourcoing. It turned out to be a fortunate decision for Gustave began musical studies as a youth with his father, a baker and amateur musician, from whom he learned the clarinet and violin. When he was sixteen he worked in a weaving factory where he was drawn from his mechanical labour to account for his wages. Gustave impressed his employer, Albert Lorithois, a local musical talent. Lorithois took the boy under his wing and helped establish an orchestral society in Tourcoing with the young Charpentier as conductor.

The youth's progress was phenomenal so that his benefactor sent him to the Conservatory at Lille, where he won several prizes. He then moved to Paris on a stipend of 1200 francs a year by the town of Tourcoing for study at the Paris Conservatory. Charpentier soon developed a taste for the Bohemian lifestyle in Paris, which was to become his home for the remainder of his long life. He also tended to rebel against his staid music teachers at the Conservatory—violin teacher Massart, with whom he came to blows, and César Franck, for a period, returning to study harmony with Pessard, and later departing, this time for military service. Upon his final return to the Conservatory, in 1885, he became a student in Massenet's composition class. Here he progressed rapidly and won his First Prize de Rome in 1887 for his cantata *Didon*.

One result of his Prix de Rome was to travel to Italy, as his hero celebrated orchestral work, *Impressions d'Italie* (1887-1889), on Italian folk music. This colourful work, very much in the musical language of the time, gained recognition on both sides of the Atlantic and was for decades heard in symphony programmes as a matter of course. In preparing the text for his symphony-drama *La Vie du Poète*, the composer used a poem to overshadow all his other creative efforts. He based the Parisian poet and his life. All this other creative effort was also begun, the opera *Louise* while a student in Massenet's composition class. Here he progressed rapidly and won his First Prize de Rome in 1887 for his cantata *Didon*.

One result of his Prix de Rome was to travel to Italy as his hero affair prior to his Rome departure with a young girl whom he loved. "I loved Louise dearly, but what became of her has always remained a mystery. We lived happily together. We were very poor. My jealousies began to make us uncomfortable," he later related as they parted. Soon afterward I went to Rome to study. I looked for her when I came back and never succeeded. Louise had been the love of his life. He never married.

Upon his return to France Charpentier settled in the hill of Montmartre quarter of Paris, where he became very active in the Bohemian life, in dress and philosophy. His *La Vie du Poète* was subtitled *Souvenirs de Montmartre*, a colourful depiction of a fête at Montmartre. During this period Charpentier often led a bohemian existence and won sporadic successes surrounded by tremendous contrasts of fortune. *Louise* was completed in 1896 but had to wait another four years before receiving its première at the Paris Opéra-Comique on February 5, 1900, to phenomenal success.

### The Music

The five songs date from the period 1890 - 1896. The music for the first, *À Mules* (1890, text by Jules Méry), will be recognized from the same title music in *Impressions d'Italie*. A muleteer expresses his sadness in having been forsaken by the one he loves, whose motto seems to be 'out of sight, out of mind'. Alternating are the voices of women at the village fountain. 1893 was the year of composition of the following three songs. The most outwardly colourful of the group is *Les chevaux de bois*, a setting of a poem about the cries of the oppressed, with their poor, worn shoes and humiliating conditions. The final song from 1893, *Sérénade à Watteau*, is a setting of Verlaine's *Clair de Lune*, a poem that subsequently attracted several other composers including Fauré, Debussy and Satie. Charpentier's version was written for the unveiling of a monument in the Luxembourg Gardens to the glory of the poet Watteau, the text being appropriate to Watteau's masques and revelers ('masques et bergamasques') playing the flute and dancing. The works presented here were recorded originally for three different companies: French Gramophone Company and Pathé. All have the distinction and great historical importance of being directed by the composer.

In most of his future creative output, the opera *Julien* would serve as witness to his disenchantment with love and life, much as *Louise* had done previously. The opera was not successful at its premiere, but its music found new life in this symphonic-dramatic form.

### La Vie du Poète

Charpentier's *La Vie du Poète* (Symphonie-Drame) was written between 1888-1892 and is music that was later incorporated into the opera *Julien, ou La vie du poète* (1913). The work is a colourful depiction of a fête at Montmartre, a semi-autobiographical account of the composer's own experiences. The symphony-drama is divided into four acts: Enthousiasme, Doute, Désespérance, and Ivresse, tracing the emotional journey of a poet through enthusiasm, doubt, despair, and finally intoxication.

The work was originally planned with the title of *La Mort du Poète* (The Death of the Poet), but as completion the composer changed the title to reflect a more positive outlook on life. The opera was first addressed at the Paris Opéra-Comique on May 23, 1913.
