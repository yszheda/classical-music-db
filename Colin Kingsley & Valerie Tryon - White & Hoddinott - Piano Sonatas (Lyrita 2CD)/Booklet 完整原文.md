## Liner Notes

### JOHN WHITE

JOHN WHITE was born in Berlin on 9 April 1936. At the Royal College of Music, he studied composition with Bernard Stevens and piano with Arthur Alexander and Eric Thorman; he also mastered the trombone, tuba and organ. Academic institutions in which he has held posts include the University of Reading, the Royal Academy of Music, the Yehudi Menuhin School and the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art; he is currently Head of Music at the Dulwich College Preparatory School. A versatile musician, he performed as bass trombonist with the Royal Ballet Touring Orchestra and tuba player with the Grimethorpe Colliery Band, as well as a piano accompanist in many theatres. The Promenade Theatre Orchestra and Garden Furniture Music are two of the many ensembles he has founded.

To date, he has written 166 piano sonatas, 3 operas, 26 symphonies, music for the stage, thirteen concertos for many unusual instruments, Camperdown and the National Theatre, as well as countless scores for vocal and chamber ensembles, electronics and percussion. *A cheerful, bluff Englishman, he is quite without artistic pretensions*; among them fellow pianists in his repertoire as a recitalist, such as Satie, Alkan, Gottschalk, Ives, Busoni, Milhaud, Poulenc and Messiaen. *His own works*, 108 in number, *are present in spirit only - there are no direct quotations or attempts at pastiche*. A certain eccentricity is evident in many works; the first movement of Sonata No. 7 (1961) takes about one and a half hours to perform and *Maddox* (1968) is the longest piece ever written for cello and piano (four hours). White is also the author of fifteen novels, fifty-three short stories and numerous plays, as well as several hundred drawings, notably *Harlequin's Face* - for chamber ensemble, and the title of a book of drawings.

### Piano Sonata No.1

John White started composing in 1956, at the age of nineteen, as an immediate reaction to experiencing the British premiere of *Landon of Menzies*. Paragonable, Symphony powerful influences detectable in the sketch; visionary qualities of this act acknowledged by the composer, Pierre Boulez. In the first two decades of his life, he sought to get it up, resulting in performances for the Society for the Promotion of New Music and the BBC. The music displays a characteristic clarity and simplicity of style, with the initial insistent fanfares of fifths and fourths, and a gentle, more flowing theme. In the development section, both themes are developed sequentially and at greater length. The central section is in rondo, a favourite form of the composer. An opening harp-like arpeggiel chord is the main subject; a contrasting staccato idea in its octaves pre-echoes the finale's main theme. This begins flirtatiously in the bass, two octaves apart. In the ensuing variations, the theme becomes the basis of a passacaglia, later incorporates the fanfares of the first movement and is then made the subject of a canon. A brilliant coda recalling all the preceding themes concludes this remarkably assured Opus 1.

### Piano Sonata No.5

(1959) is a predominantly dark and reflective, with transparent, often contrapuntal textures which allow the individual elements of Mompou's music contrasting episodes, freely assembled in rondo form. The Sonata begins with a gently rocking, but fluent and broad theme, presented in the tenor register and then, after decorated by a harp-like accompaniment. *Allegro presto* whose main theme is presented by both hands two octaves apart. A reminiscence of the first theme's octave preludes a pianissimo recitative-like cadenza for the left hand. Next, insistent, pulsating octaves in the bass appear; over which intermittently spaced chords introduce sparse, muted horn-like calls. A bright fanfare-like idea is suggested in its Gallic-sounding innocence of Fauré or Poulenc. Amorphous chords give rise to a wistful theme in the bass. Rich, trilled chords in the bass lead to a varied reprise of the opening theme. A harmonic sequence, marked *dolcissimo* introduces the final march-like section. The drumbeat of the bass is joined by a brilliant toccata. At its conclusion, the bass solo one of the composer's most enigmatic works draws to a resonance with the work of Harvesbury and Bax. The finale is a passacaglia, the concluding recurring material and introduction creation of a convincing whole from disparate elements.

### Piano Sonata No.4

Dedicated, like the First Sonata, to Colin Kingsley, the Fourth (1959) employs a more austere harmonic style than White's Opus 1. It is divided into six sections, the first featuring a gently throbbing barcarolle-like main theme. There then follows a section, marked *legato sostenuto e sempre la stessa*, a measure-textured *Andantino*, a lyrical section with a gently rocking arpeggiel theme, the middle section of which is marked *poco a poco più mosso*, a scherzando intermezzo heralds the *dolente cantabile* slow movement, with an extremely economy of means. The central section introduces material first heard before a varied reprise of the main theme. After another 'Distinct Pause', the final section is in rondo form, with a brilliant toccata, the concluding recurring material and introduction creation of a convincing whole from disparate elements. The finale is a passacaglia, the concluding recurring material and introduction creation of a convincing whole from disparate elements.

### Piano Sonata No.9

Looking ahead to such concentrated, epigrammatic one-movement piano sonatas as Nos. 6, 7½ and 8*, the Ninth (1968) is expansive and closely argued. In cohesive structure involves clear inter-relationships between texts in six sections. The opening Andante mesto section presents a delicate fall-and-lift theme gradually fleshed out by chordal accretions and pseudo-descants. An energetic Allegro follows; its material subsequently developed melodically. The original theme returns in varied form; before a third subject appears, an episodic-sounding passage that assumes increasing rhythmic vitality. A brief opening theme precedes a return of the double-dotted figure. There is a brief coda in the form of a falling Press note motif rather than the traditional cadential gestures; material; quickly establishes its own gracefully ethereal character; before melting away with a most un-Sibelius like figure into the hushed final chord. The Ninth Sonata is dedicated to Peter Norris.

### ALUN HODDINOTT

One of the most versatile and prolific composers of his generation, ALUN HODDINOTT was born in Bargoed, Glamorgan on 11 August 1929. After graduating from University College, Cardiff, he studied with the Australian composer Arthur Benjamin. Appointed lecturer in music at his old college in 1956, and promoted reader in 1961, he served University College, Cardiff as lecturer (1959-1963), reader (1963-1967) and Professor and Head of Department of Music (1967-87). Hoddinott is now Professor Cardiff Festival of Twentieth Century Music's artistic director.

His substantial body of compositions includes operas, seven operas and a wealth of chamber, instrumental, vocal and choral music. He was particularly renowned for his concerts, working with many first-rate artists, both singers and players, orchestral, trumpets, horns, trombone and euphonium. The genre occupied a significant place in his output for six decades: one of the very earliest works, the Concerto for Clarinet and Strings (1950), premiered by Gervase de Peyer and the Hallé Orchestra under Sir John Barbirolli at the 1954 Cheltenham Festival. Another important work, composed in 1968 for the 2004 Proms. He died in Swansea on Wednesday 11 March 2008.

Hoddinott's interest in the sonata form began in his teenage series of works for solo instruments. His commitment to the genre bespeaks both a constant fascination with writing for the piano and an ongoing search for new patterns of musical thought. Though he wrote consistently and idiomatically for the piano, he was not a pianist (his lean instruments were cornet and viola). He cultivated the sonatas for a succession of virtuoso performers such as Valerie Tryon, John McCabe, John Ogdon, Raimonds Garle and Martin Jones, who all inspired his creativity.

Perhaps more than any other genre, the piano sonatas chart Hoddinott's developing style and preoccupations: the first two contain a palindromic, a favourite device; and the Seventh both an arch-like structure, another characteristic form. The Third is in two interlinked complementary movements; indistinguishable in mood and thematic content, while the Sixth and Eleventh (1993) are fine examples of the composer's many compact one-movement sonatas. The Fourth and Fifth highlight his passion for baroque forms.

### Piano Sonata No.1 Op.17

Written in June 1959, the First Sonata establishes Hoddinott's individual style: in writing for the piano with incisive and pithy subjects in all four movements. It begins with an expressive, contemplative Andante in the form of a weightless nocturne based on a lilting melody; a second theme more intense for repeated chord patterns. Steadily accruing texturation, the material builds inexorably towards a central climax, at the beginning of which the opening theme returns, now in more dramatic guise before further argument. The second movement is a relentless, grisly toccata in the form of a palindromic which the opening theme returns to generate chord patterns. Steadily accruing texturation is restored in the form of a serene Adagio, prefaced by a lyrical theme which is subsequently extended, eventually rising in a finely meshed with two main themes, suggesting affinities with sonata form: the first theme is lyrical and cantabile, the second more rhythmically incisive. As the movement. After a cadenza-like passage, the movement gathers sufficient momentum to launch a sparkling, brilliant conclusion in full flow. The First Sonata was commissioned by the Cheltenham Festival of Music and premiered on 12 July 1959 at Cheltenham Town Hall by Margaret Kitchin, who also gave the first London performance at the Wigmore Hall on 31 January 1966.

### Nocturne No.2 Op.16 No.1

Following the Nocturne for Orchestra Op.5 (1952), Hoddinott's gift for creating music of the night was confirmed by his two Nocturnes for piano. These atmospheric, darkly coloured and finely crafted pieces have an improvisatory freedom allied to keyboard writing. The Second Nocturne is notable for its elegant, crystalline textures and a mood that hovers between the meditative and the melancholic. Like the First Sonata, the work was first performed in London on 31 March 1959.

### Piano Sonata No.2 Op.27

Though recognized by the same composer as its predecessor, the Second Sonata of 1962 is noticeably more succinct and concentrated. The intense opening *Moderato* quietly but obsessively reiterates a series of restricted melodic intervals and rhythmic motives, creating an air of claustrophobia and latent energy. At mid-point, the movement turns back on itself in a palindromic return to a sense of withdrawn introspection.

In contrast, the central *Allegro* is a widening-out nocturne whose muted, meditative lyricism is abruptly shattered by a climactically violent central eruption analogous to that in the *Andante* of Schubert's Piano Sonata No.20 in A major D959. The spirited *Allegro finale* contains an example of the composer's characteristically sardonic, satirical march-like figures, reminiscent of Prokofiev and Hindemith. Another theme is markedly different: dance-like, airy and fleeting. A bravura *giocoso* coda seals the work's tonal ambiguities with a fact-link resolution on D. The Macnaghten Concerts commissioned the Second Sonata, which was premiered by Colin Kingsley on 26 February 1962 at the Arts Council Drawing Room, St. James Square, London.

### Nocturne No.1 Op.9

Marked *Espressivo e rubato*, the First Nocturne is launched from the shadows, exploring the piano's sonorous deep registers before extending out to the full range of the keyboard. Gradually the music gathers momentum, blossoming into a rhapsodic outpouring in a sequence of rich clusters of chords. Eventually, an air of stillness is retrieved and the piano subsides back into a meditative calm. Robin North gave the first performance in University College, Newcastle on 22 February 1956.

### Elegy Op.18 No.3

Dated 1959, the *Elegy* became part of a four-movement Suite for clavierbird or piano, completed four years later and dedicated to Thorsten Dare. The *Elegy* takes the form of a brief, spacious meditation whose thirty-six bars develop from a single motive in the opening phrase. After a central weighty climax, the initial delicate, restrained mood returns to establish the meditative character of the close. *Presto* finale follows without a pause, though the lack of resolution in the *Elegy's* final bars defies the *Presto's* quixotic, insistent outbursts.
