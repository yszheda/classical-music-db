## 内页 3（英文说明文章第 1 页）

This is the essence of what sparked the *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses*. This collection of ten pieces of varying length (from around five minutes for the *Ave Maria* to the twenty of *Bénédiction de Dieu dans la solitude*) was composed between the end of the 1840s and beginning of the 1850s (with the single exception of the fourth piece, *Pensée des morts*, the first version of which dates from 1834 and was published in 1853, one year before the Sonata in B minor. The title is borrowed from Alphonse Lamartine's collection of poetry of 1830; several pieces of the ten pieces make explicit reference to titles in Lamartine's poems (*Invocation*, *Bénédiction de Dieu dans la solitude*, *Pensée des morts* and *Hymne de l'enfant à son réveil*). It is clear that the composer's intention is to create a cycle in which music and poetry meet (at each other. In the same mood of a mystical melancholy that the poet describes in the preface to his collection: "There are meditative souls that solitude and contemplation elevate towards ideas of the infinite, that is, towards religion; all their thoughts become absorption and prayer, their whole existence is a silent hymn to divinity and hope. These souls seek in themselves and the world around them poetic and musical harmonies to God, expressions and images that can reveal Him to them, to able in turn to reveal themselves to Him, to see and to find one tool! There are hearts crushed by sorrow, rejected by the world; that take refuge in the world of silence, in the solitude of their souls, to weep, to pray: they can be visited by Muse in solitary abasement, familiar themselves at one with its harmonies and despair, when they listen, 'We pray with your words, we weep with your tears, we pray with your songs!'")

The first piece in the cycle, *Invocation*, is characterised by a strong sense of drama, accentuated by the use of octaves and double octaves, while the *Ave Maria* that follows (a mystic transcription of a vocal piece for mixed voices and organ) evokes some vaguely archaic mood with its fivefold theme (the sustaining pedal is lowered almost throughout) and its dramatic quality. Despite the outward calmness of pieces like the *Sonata* and *Totentanz* polyphony, Liszt's religiosity is more faith than mystical or dogmatic. It is anchored to the earth, to the desires and sufferings of humanity. This is revealed in the third piece, *Bénédiction de Dieu dans la solitude* (a F sharp major) where the mood alternates between a delicate rhapsody of the left hand, with before moving to the upper line and finally, reinforced by octaves and chords, leading to a heroic statement. Between these two sections, there are two delicate interludes in D major and B flat major. After the second statement the main theme reappears in the left hand, while the right hand indulges in gentle arabesques until the coda, when the theme of the first interlude reemerges, like an echoed reminiscence.

The next piece is the complex and enigmatic *Pensée des morts*. It opens with a chord that contains a tritone, leading to a descending figure that is immediately repeated inverted, ascending: this is a Gregorian-sounding passage. The *Hymne de l'enfant à son réveil* is also a transcription (of a piece for women's voices and organ in which piano takes the place of the diminished fourths and harp), and is characterised by a sentimental melody in the ethereal key of A flat major, in which the composer encroaches on the refined sound world of the short piano cycle *Consolations*, written at the same time as the *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses*. In this recording we can hear, like an encore, the third of the *Consolations*, in D flat major, a little jewel of enchanting simplicity, with a gentle melody above continuous arpeggio figures.

*Funérailles*, on the other hand, opens in a mood of grandeur with the tolling of a death-knell (the opening chords in the backdrop of right-hand chords which then emerge with all their force until they come to overwhelm the tolling bell). This is the most spectacular and visual of the collection, whose virtuosity is mostly restricted to the many octave passages (there is an explicit reference to Chopin's op.53 *Polonaise*) and whose funereal character is that of a heroic lament. The central section of *Funérailles* is built on a bass motif that descends chromatically, creating a feeling of increasing anguish.

## 内页 4（英文说明文章第 2 页）

an aspiration towards a form of total art (embracing every single artistic expression (music, poetry, theatre and figurative art) with a corresponding look at Beethoven's thought in Europe, and in Germany in particular. In music it reached its apogee with the conception of the *Gesamtkunstwerk* (total work of art) that Wagner developed around 1850, but Berlioz and Schumann had already undertaken the same direction. Berlioz's orchestral works occupy a new area, somewhere between instrumental music, novel and theatre, both *Symphonie fantastique* (1830) and *Harold en Italie* (1834) taking the shape, by way of their references to a narrative, of a kind of novel in music. On the other hand, the piano cycles that Schumann composed in the same period, from *Papillons* to *Carnaval* to *Kreisleriana*, put forward an alternative formal structure to the old classical sonata form, since they derived their coherence from extra-musical elements.

Music seems to have been ideal for the development of this new conception of art, one that was revolutionary in both an aesthetics and spirit. A preverbial art, it paradoxically carries more significance than poetry, in that it is not restricted by words, which can only suggest music from a perceivably reality towards a spiritual one. To borrow the expression of the poet Heinrich Heine, music is "a pure" art par excellence, with the only apparent contradiction that its spiritual and emotional mission is not free from subjection to words, it can then re-absorb them, but from a dominant position. Goethe had already shown the way with the famous entry of the baritone solo in the Ninth Symphony: "Friends, not these tones! Let us rather strike up more pleasant and joyful!" It is no coincidence that Beethoven was the ideal that inspired Wagner.

This preamble shows the reason why the nineteenth-century wizard of the piano keyboard, the diabolic Franz Liszt (Raiding, 1811 - Bayreuth, 1886) talked to Beethoven with such love - a love that he felt right from his days of Liszt, when he travelled across Europe as a virtuoso performer. At this time he roused both admiration for his keyboard skill and scandal for his private life, in particular his relationship with Marie d'Agoult, the Comtesse d'Agoult, whom he met in 1833 when he was 22 years old, and with whom he would have three children. Berlioz invented the recital as we know it today, as the sole protagonist on the concert platform, an opportunity to reflect on music's past. Yet between the travelling virtuoso of the 1830s and the Liszt who produced an endless series of operatic paraphrases and studies of astonishing difficulty (the two versions of the *Transcendental Études* of 1837 and *Études d'exécution transcendante* of 1838 are almost unplayable as written) and the composer who in the calm of Weimar in the 1850s wrote the symphonic poems and the Sonata in B minor, there is a strong sense of continuity.

This is clear from Liszt's attitude to religion and Catholicism. A rapid glance at the events of his life gives an impression of a successful virtuoso attached to worldly glory who suddenly gives up his career as court Kapellmeister. He then retreats to Rome where, in 1865, he takes minor orders, and completes a sort of a Franciscan air of renunciation of earthly vanities. In actual fact, sensuality and religion have always been hand in hand in Liszt's life (at nineteen he was already considering entering a seminary), the intermingling of heaven and earth, flesh and spirit, sin and redemption (a pairing that, unsurprisingly, recurs in Wagner) becomes a non-manifesto for his all-embracing vital force, that was the kind of religiosity that was Catholic in form but non-confessional in its substance. In a famous letter to the Princess Jeanne Elisabeth Carolyne von Sayn-Wittgenstein, his partner from the 1850s, Liszt writes a sentence that reveals his genuine outlook: "My life has been merely wondering at length between the good and evil." And it is the Romantic "yearning": love is the impulse towards the infinite, able to annihilate the finite embrace both human emotions and surrender to God.

We return to a mystical mood with the *Miserere d'après Palestrina*, a plain, unadorned piece that gradually becomes more elaborate as accompanying figures increase in complexity. The last pieces in the cycle, *Andante lagrimoso* and *Cantique d'amour* tend towards the salon manner of *Hymne de l'enfant à son réveil*, although the *Cantique* has the same process of thematic transformation (complete with rapid ascending scales in the final statement) found previously in *Bénédiction de Dieu dans la solitude*, which is from the salon directly to the concert hall.

With the *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses* Liszt undoubtedly goes beyond sonata form, just as Schumann and Berlioz had done, and as he himself was in the process of doing in his symphonic poems. It opens with a prelude, then *Les Préludes* of 1854, was inspired by Lamartine's poetry. The unity of the cycle, while the aesthetic quality of the individual pieces may not be all on one level, is provided above all by an abstract and spiritual principle from a purely formal one. Liszt's contemporaries, however, were not ready for this. Clara Schumann wrote in her diary in 1851, after Liszt had visited the Schumann house in Düsseldorf and had played some of the *Harmonies*: "As always, he played with diabolic bravura, dominating the pianoforte like a demon (it is impossible to find adjectives to express it)... But his compositions are truly terrible". Clara Schumann fell into the same misunderstanding as other listeners musicologically: from the years when faced by Liszt's compositions, of considering them extravagant, showpieces displaying technical prowess instead of concentrating on the musical substance. Liszt's music, as the great Lisztian Humphrey Searle wrote in an essay in 2011, acquires significance only when it finds a performer aware of its poetic and aesthetic complexity and, I would add, a listener prepared to put aside their prejudices.

In any case, it is clear that the cycle as a whole has a poetic intent rather than a virtuoso one, bearing in mind that in Liszt own the most blazing virtuosity can acquire a poetic and mystical connotation (it is there, for example, in the very explicit "transcendental" given in the Études); from this point of view the virtuosity is also one of sound. And even the *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses* ultimately appear as virtuoso works, given that the performer is often asked to play at the extremes of the keyboard, and especially in the most interesting and innovative piece in the cycle, *Bénédiction de Dieu dans la solitude*, in the key of F sharp major, with its delicate harp effects.
