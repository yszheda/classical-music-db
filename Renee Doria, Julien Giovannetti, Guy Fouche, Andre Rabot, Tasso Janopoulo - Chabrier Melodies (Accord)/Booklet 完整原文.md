## Liner Notes by Claude Rostand

### Emmanuel Chabrier

Chabrier wrote relatively few songs, and they are all in this recording. Their place in the history of French music nevertheless goes far beyond the composer's original intention, and would no doubt have greatly surprised him. Verlaine said that Chabrier was "as gay as a chaffinch and as melodious as a nightingale" and the austere D'Indy called him "the jay of French music, who flies about and would have his revenge." One can have difficulty in taking himself seriously as one of those illustrious figures who quietly change the face of musical history.

To a however considerable extent, what he did, particularly in the realm of song, had the French song before him: 17th-18th century air, which had gradually declined, and made its return, much later, through the back door, in the form of the romance, and then of the mélodie. This latter genre having been adopted by Germans — Beethoven, Weber, Schubert, Schumann, Mendelssohn, Brahms — and Frenchmen alike, Chabrier, like Gounod and Saint-Saëns took it further, with varying success, and Franck, Lalo and Bizet added features from different French and foreign sources. And then Chabrier's melodies had to compete with the piano accompaniment, which was becoming more and more refined and almost self-stand. At first sight Chabrier's songs might seem old-fashioned compared with their immediate predecessors, a return to the style of the romance of the popular song. But these simple pieces, which often do not take themselves seriously, so to speak, at themselves, bring a host of innovations.

First of all there is the incredibly bold harmonic invention of a master who had every appearance of the most serene, of the least "revolutionary" of musicians — elegant, casual solutions to the most formidable problems of prosody. Here Chabrier is exemplary in his concern, in common, rare in his predecessors, to follow "the subtle music of the words," to be the slave of the prosody and the meaning of a text, and to translate exactly, in a short, image or idea in the text. In this Chabrier could be called the first musical impressionist.

Such examples were, they did not escape notice. Later generations took Chabrier as a model, and many of the masterpieces of Debussy, Satie, Ravel, Milhaud, Auric, Poulenc and others might not have been what they are if their composers had not followed the path he pointed out. In fact they proudly proclaimed themselves his successors.

Some of the songs in this recording are marked by their time and in somewhat facile fashions. Moreover the words are not always of the highest literary or poetic quality. But they often have an old-fashioned charm and, more importantly, they have lasted because of the details and analytical skill that were to be found everywhere in music, indelible, ever-living signature.

**Credo d'Amour** is one of the earliest Chabrier songs of which the date is known — 18 February 1867. It is a somewhat declamatory style, by Armand Silvestre, and the composer had followed his somewhat declamatory style.

**Duo de l'Ouvreuse de l'Opéra-Comique et de l'Employé du Bon Marché** is a sketch dating from 1892 for a projected work for the Comique (in one act), and in which there was a comic piece combining the café-concert, Troyan and opéra-comique styles, in which an usherette ("dodéfal and rather silly") and a department-store clerk ("very stupid") sing a duet in which they declare their love for respectively to Cavallo and Roussiant, whose names admirably admirably in the refrain.

**Tes Yeux Bleus**, to a poem by Maurice Rollinat, dates from 1 February 1885. Wagner's influence can be heard in places, and Chabrier goes into great detail in a conversation about which French song moved away from the Germanic style when it was tending to follow in the late 19th century.

**Chanson pour Jeanne**, composed from 23 September 1886, is a setting of a poem by Catulle Mendès, whose period of tics are effortlessly hidden by the music. It is one of the most characteristic examples of Chabrier's profound lyricism, very personal and free of all mannerisms.

Three songs published posthumously were probably written at about this time. The first, dedicated to "my friend Lucien Fugère, of the Opéra-Comique" and entitled *Sommation Irrespectueuse*, setting a text by Victor Hugo, is a sort of "Hymn of Hate" of a very special kind. The second, *A quoi bon entendre*, is also to a poem by Hugo, and is full of lively invention, particularly in the delicate piano accompaniment, which is typical of Chabrier; the first impression of the third is an undated autograph with no title. *Tes Yeux Bleus*, written a few years earlier, in 1870, by Duparc. It is extremely interesting to compare the two versions. Chabrier is somewhat uncharacteristic, as is certain passages (in Gwondoline, where a bassoon is added to the piano accompaniment).

**Lied**, such a poem by Catulle Mendès, is probably one of the last Chabrier wrote. The style is that of a romance, but in spite of the rather silly words, it is one of Chabrier's most delicate pieces. Its melodic, harmonic and rhythmic invention are unequalled. Chabrier of the finest vintage, transcending the mediocrity of the poem.

**Romande de l'Etoile** was published separately after *L'Étoile*, the 1877 in the Bouffes-Parisiens in 1877. It was published separately after the 1877.

**Toutes les Fleurs**, equally from 1890 (2 April), is composed sur un poème d'Edmond Rostand. Plus que la pièce zoologique précitée elle méritait le qualificatif de « baveux » imaginé par Istar. C'est une réussite charmante dans ce genre cocasse et malicieux où Chabrier se moque du ton des salons musicaux de l'époque, et où, évitant de tomber dans la caricature facile et grossière, il en donne une évocation exquise tout en témoignant d'une originalité et d'une désinvolture qui n'appartiennent qu'à lui.

**Lied**, sur un poème de Catulle Mendès, est probablement une des dernières mélodies de Chabrier. Elle conserve le caractère de la romance, mais malgré le côté un peu coucou du texte c'est une des plus finement ciselées que nous propose cette intégrale. Elle est d'une invention mélodique, harmonique et rythmique inouïe. Chabrier pur jus, en dépit de la pâleur du prétexte littéraire.

**La Romande de l'Etoile** ne fut publiée ainsi séparément qu'à titre posthume. Elle est extraite du premier opéra-comique de Chabrier, *L'Étoile*, qui avait été représenté aux Bouffes-Parisiens en 1877.

— Claude Rostand
