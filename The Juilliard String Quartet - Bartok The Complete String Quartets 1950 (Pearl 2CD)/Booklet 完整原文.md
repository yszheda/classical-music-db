## 内页说明文章

**BÉLA BARTÓK**
**STRING QUARTETS**
**Juilliard Quartet**

When Béla Bartók died on 26 September 1945 in New York City, at the age of 64, his music was, at last, beginning to achieve a wider attention from music-lovers in general rather than mainly from modern music specialists. In some ways, the wider appeal of Bartók's music arose from a gradual change in his musical language from the modernism of most of his works written between, say, 1919 and 1934, towards the more lyrical expression which characterised the music of his final decade. There are exceptions. In particular, the Sonata for Solo Violin of 1944 is as musically and intellectually demanding as any of his works from that earlier period - a period which, in turn, had marked an evolution from that which Bartók wrote between 1904 and 1919, music perhaps more frequently folk-based than at any other time.

Bartók wrote much chamber music in his 50 years as a creative musician; an early unrepresentative quartet, an un-numbered string quartet, in F major dates from 1898, and at the time of his death he left sketches for a seventh. With Bartók's six mature string quartets (1908-1939) the genre is the only one (apart from his piano music) which spans his entire career as a composer. In so far as their reputation at the time of his death was concerned, Bartók's quartets were in some ways the best-known part of his output - again, with the possible exception of the piano works.

During his lifetime, all six of his quartets had been recorded - two of them more than once. Excepting No. 1, the sixth, which was first heard in New York in 1941, had been recorded three times on 78rpm discs by the time the first integral, long-playing, set was made by the Juilliard String Quartet for American Columbia in 1950. These recordings - released here on CD for the first time - were also issued in the USA on 38rpm sets in 1951.

This relatively young ensemble had been formed in 1946, the year the Juilliard School of Music came into being, as a result of the amalgamation of the New York Institute of Musical Art and the Juilliard Graduate School. The Juilliard Graduate School was the brain-child of William Schuman, who had been appointed president of the Juilliard School some months before. The ensemble's founder members were Robert Mann and Robert Koff, violins, Raphael Hillyer, viola, and Arthur Winograd, cello. These four musicians were members of the faculty of the Juilliard School, and were to give on average around fifty concerts a year under the sponsorship of leading American colleges, universities and other educational institutions, alongside musical and established concert-giving organisations.

After Bartók's death, it was William Schuman's determination that ultimately led to these recordings being made. He himself, George Krouse, who had commissioned and premiered one of Bartók's last works, the Concerto for Orchestra, and who had founded the Tanglewood Summer School of Music in upstate Massachusetts, and persuaded him to permit the Juilliard Quartet to record the School in 1947. This was the ensemble's debut, and the following year they returned to play all six Bartók quartets.

In preparing these works, they were greatly helped by Jenő Kenei, who had been a member of the Kolisch Quartet, had taken part in the premieres of No. 5 and No. 6 by Bartók well, and had led his own distinguished Quartet for many years. Lisner died in New York City in November 1948 at the age of 54. The Third Quartet was the first the Juilliard Quartet learned, and the Fifth was the last. Although every one of the Bartók Quartets poses problems for the players, they found the First and Sixth among the easiest with which to come to terms. The finale of No. 5, however, proved the most difficult movement for the musicians at first.

It is impossible to over-estimate the importance of these performances in the dissemination and acceptance of the Bartók quartets. The impact was such that the Juilliard Quartet gave the first-ever complete public cycle of Bartók Quartets, at Times Hall in New York City, on 28 February and 28 March 1949. Among the packed audience was Dimitri Shostakovich, then on his first visit as part of a Peace Delegation. This was a painfully tense period in international relations; on his return to Russia, Shostakovich shared his impressions of the works - it appears the quartet to which he took the strongest exception was No. 4, which he criticised for relying too much on technical effect. But there can be no doubt that Shostakovich was much impressed by what he had heard.

Appreciated by Schuman and others, also in the New York audience was Goddard Lieberson, the British-born head of American Columbia Records (later CBS, now Sony), who had persuaded Bartók, in his final years, to record a representative selection of his piano music, as well as the historic recording made at the work with the original performers - Joseph Szigeti and Benny Goodman (see GEM 0057), alongside the composer himself. The invention of the long-playing disc by American Columbia, and the subsequent launch in the USA in 1948 enabled Lieberson to bring to fruition his long-wished-for plan to make an integral recording of Bartók's string quartets. For Lieberson, there was only one ensemble to undertake such a task - the Juilliard String Quartet - but there was a problem. Since Columbia, for the label had recently placed under exclusive contract the much-admired Budapest String Quartet, who had already played (and recorded, but not for release) all six of the Bartók quartets. By reserving the Hungarians for standard classical repertoire, and leaving the Juilliard Quartet for modern music (in addition to the Bartók quartets, they went on to record for Lieberson the first Schoenberg Quartets and Berg's Lyric Suite, among many other works), this problem was satisfied all round.

Robert Mann, the founding leader of the Juilliard Quartet, had been an enthusiast for Bartók's music since his student days at Juilliard (during the years 1938-42). Mann had been present at the world premieres of both Contrasts and the Sixth Quartet, but he never met Bartók personally, regarding this omission as one of the great regrets of his life. Mann was also a conductor (he conducted the first recording of Bartók's First Piano Concerto with the pianist Leon Fleisher) as soloist with the Boston Symphony Orchestra - called, owing to contractual restrictions, the 'Tanglewood Sinfonietta'). As violinist, Mann also recorded Bartók's Contrasts with Samuel Dushkin and Hambro, as well as the First Sonata for Violin and Piano (with Hambro) and the Solo Violin Sonata - these were all for Bartók Records, the label founded by the composer's son, Peter. The second violinist, Robert Koff, stayed with the Quartet until 1989. Raphael Hillyer remained until 1969; the original cellist, Arthur Winograd, was the first to leave the Quartet, in 1955. Winograd became thereafter a noted conductor, making records in that capacity for M-G-M and Audio Fidelity.

The recordings took place at Columbia Studios on 30th Street. The producer was Harold Scott. Contrary to what has been claimed, the recordings were not made on tape but on large acetate discs. Therefore, apart from the side-joins in various movements, the performances we hear are unedited. As well as Schuman and Lieberson, and the composer's son, Peter, several famous musicians asked to be present at one or other of these sessions, once news got around that they were taking place. On one occasion, while recording the side Raphael Hillyer broke an A String but did not have a replacement; Walter Trampler rushed to supply one, and on another occasion Jascha Heifetz set in during the recording of the Fourth Quartet, making some very perceptive remarks concerning the tempi and relationships in the work.

With the release of these recordings in the USA, the reputation of the Juilliard String Quartet was assured. In the early 1960s, with the arrival of stereo recording, the Juilliard Quartet remade the Bartók quartet cycle for American Columbia, a set produced by Paul Myers; by then, only Mann and Hillyer remained of the founder players, but there can be little doubt that it was the ensemble's original, and musically revelatory, 1950 recordings that played such an important role in leading to the international acceptance of these masterpieces as part of the quartet repertoire.

**ROBERT MATTHEW-WALKER**
