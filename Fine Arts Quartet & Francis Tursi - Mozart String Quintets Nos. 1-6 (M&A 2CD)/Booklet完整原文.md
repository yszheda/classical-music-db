## Liner Notes (pages 4-5)

### Mozart's Viola Quintets

For players and listeners alike, the viola quintets of Mozart stand among the highest examples of chamber music. There are many who would not hesitate to name the most often heard of them (the C major and G minor) as representing some of the finest music in our heritage, in any category.

Mozart's interest in the viola quintet spanned a long period in his life, extending from a time almost as early as his first youthful string quartet to the last year of his tragically short life. There are six quintets for two violins, two violas, and cello from Mozart's pen:

- K. 174, in B flat, Salzburg, completed in December, 1773
- K. 406 [516b], in C minor, Vienna, early 1787
- K. 515, C major, Vienna, April, 1787
- K. 516, G minor, Vienna, May, 1787
- K. 593, D major, Vienna, December, 1790
- K. 614, E flat, Vienna, April, 1791

Of these, K. 406 [516b] is a "second generation" quintet, for it was reworked by the composer himself from the Serenade for Winds (wind octet), in C minor, K. 388 [K. 384a], Vienna, 1782/83.

### Viola Quintet in B flat, K. 174

In 1773, not long after his 17th birthday, Mozart wrote this, the first of his viola quintets. As the late musicologist Alfred Einstein points out, it was inspired by models composed by the Italian, Luigi Boccherini, and by the Notturno in C, also from early 1773, by Michael Haydn, younger brother of the eminent Joseph. In December of that year, Mozart changed his mind about the closing portions of the work and wrote a new trio section for the minuet, as well as a new finale. Elements of the original version were incorporated in the replacement section and movement.

### Viola Quintet in C minor, K. 406 [K. 516b]

Musical transcription can be a tricky business. Max Reger, for example, made an affectingly simple keyboard theme of Mozart the take-off point for a grotesquely huge set of symphonic variations that seem to leave the tune cowering for mercy at composition's end. On the other hand, transcription has also served more fruitfully, notably in Bach's re-working of music by Vivaldi and others. Case in point: Bach's resetting of the Vivaldi four-violin concerto into one for four keyboards. Here, Bach not only studies the music of an esteemed contemporary but also illuminates it with the strong light of his own instinct.

Then, of course, there is that most legitimate type of transcription, one carried out by the author of the original work. The quintet here at hand was first written by Mozart in Salzburg in 1782, as an octet for 2 oboes, 2 clarinets, 2 horns, and 2 bassoons, intended to serve as a serenade—that is, an entertainment piece for social function. Contrary to the normal form of such works, this composition has only the typical fast-slow-minuet-fast grouping we are accustomed to find in the classic chamber music piece, without the added dance- and slow-movements usually present in the serenades of the time. In this respect, this octet is similar to the next and last serenade that Mozart composed, the familiar *Eine kleine Nachtmusik*.

In 1787, Mozart was at work in Vienna on a projected set of six string quintets. He had already written the towering masterpieces, the C major and G minor quintets. To provide the third in this series, he rearranged the wind serenade of 1782 for the string grouping of two violins, two violas, and cello, the quintet now known as K. 516b. There is of course a complete change in sound-color in the

process of shifting from the wind to the string setting. However, even with the loss of the pungency and bite of the wind timbres, there is no loss of that inimitable mixture of delicacy and bluff heartiness, of poignancy and jest, of learned invention and limpid melodiousness that is the Mozartean way.

Compared to the monumental viola quintets of Mozart's later years, this youthful example may seem slighter and more inclined to the entertaining than the deeply moving. Considered in its own right, however, the work evokes keen appreciation of its invention, its lyricism, its high spirits. The genius of the 17-year-old is already enough to kindle delight, wonder, and envy on the part of the hearer, old or young.

## Liner Notes (pages 6-7)

### Viola Quintet in C, K. 515

Mozart wrote this work in April of 1787. One month later, he completed the G minor quintet. When hearing the latter piece, we are conscious of the fatalistic, somber tone of the music. Our summation of the C major quintet, on the other hand, must be in terms of serenity, quiet strength, confidence.

How can these extremes be evident in so closely spaced works by one and the same man? Let us turn to words from a letter written by Mozart to his father, just two weeks before the completion of the C major quintet. The son knows that his parent is dying in Salzburg; yet we may assume that the words are meant not only to console the older man, but in honest reflection of Wolfgang's own conviction:

*"I never lie down at night without reflecting that – young as I am – I may not live to see another day. Yet no one of all my acquaintance could say that I am morose or disgruntled. For this blessing I daily thank my Creator and wish with all my heart that each one of my fellow creatures could enjoy it."*
(Excerpted from the translation by Emily Anderson, *The Letters of Mozart and His Family*, London, 1938.)

We can call upon the music of the C Major quintet to witness to the written sentiment. In the entire work, with its spaciousness of melody and setting, there is not a note of discontent. True, the middle section of the first movement is colored in rather sober shade; but even here, the sobriety is one of ineffable sweetness – that of the profoundly perceptive, not the heedless soul.

The quiet tenderness of the Menuetto is almost painful in its muted intensity. And the lyricism of the Andante is of so exalted an order that the two cadenzas-like passages for first violin and first viola are free of any sense of virtuosity. Rather, they are the welling out of a noble and generous spirit.

### Viola Quintet in G minor, K. 516

This composition makes clear that the added viola does much more than provide another "filler" for the musical texture; it enlarges the palette of sound, freeing the first viola and the cello for a prominence that at times exceeds even that in Mozart's own quartet writing.

The temper of this work is dominated by the fact of its key, for G minor was always an especially evocative and soul-searching one for Mozart. In the opening Allegro, a pulsing eighth-note undercurrent stirs from beginning to end. The sighing first melody is heard in the coloration of varied duets: violin/viola; two violins; violin/cello. The latter pair present the still more yearning and pensive secondary theme.

Development begins immediately, moving into the dramatic interplay of the voices in the middle section of the movement and continuing even into the recapitulation, as though Mozart cannot or will not resolve the tensions of the music. The ending seems to extend the agony, breaking off at last with the three closing chords.

Menuetto is surely a misnomer, for the second movement is a dance of the tormented, marked by sharp chordal outbursts. The trio section, though more placid, is shadowy in its calmness. In the Adagio the brooding is suffused with, and somewhat relieved by, a moonlit quality.

This is not the case with a second Adagio, one that Mozart felt compelled to write as the introduction to the finale. This prelude, indeed, seems to be the center of gravity of the entire quintet, distilling the prevailingly somber mood of the work. To this listener, this solo for the first violin, abetted by the chorus of the other four instruments, summons up memories of similar episodes in the Passions of J.S. Bach. Coming on the heels of this opening, the rollicking tone of the concluding rondo can seem a shocking contrast. But this is the only possible ending to the work—a necessary release from the temper of the earlier movements, yet bearing within itself an occasional flash of that same disquiet. It is this characteristic juxtaposition of light and dark, in fact, that makes Mozart's music so fascinating and challenging to perform.

## Liner Notes (pages 8-9)

### Viola Quintet in D, K. 593

Written in December 1790, this quintet was possibly commissioned by the Hungarian-born wholesale merchant, Johann Tost, earlier the dedicatee of Haydn string quartets, Opp. 54, 55, and 64.

Mozart's opening Larghetto, nobly elegant, in its discourse between the cello and the other four instruments, serves as introduction to the entire first movement and is integrated with it melodically. The Allegro continues the conversational aspect of the Larghetto, in nonchalant, friendly manner. This discussion takes after a break, in a kind of second developmental episode.

Here Mozart demonstrates his mastery of dramatic counterpoint. So fascinated is he by the interplay of voices and melodic fragments that he sweeps into his recapitulation almost unknown. The repartee is cast now in a higher register than at the beginning of the Allegro. Moreover, the contrapuntal richness of the development section infects the dialogue of the closing of the movement. A last easy burst of melody takes us to the double bar, but not before a curious return of the Larghetto has left us with the feeling that Mozart has indeed achieved what J. Quantz, virtuoso flutist, composer, music-master of the court of Frederick the Great, would have praised as a "good mixture of pleasing and brilliant ideas."

The Adagio, again, is an intriguing mixture: two passionate, wide-arching outpourings of melody from the first violin mark the dual climaxes of this music. These landmarks are set in a verdant background of part-writing that combines the five instruments in every conceivable grouping and ordering. This movement takes on the five instruments in every conceivable grouping and ordering. This movement is characteristic enough, that Mozart achieves what seems an easier more earnest tone in the development, and the animation continues.

The Menuetto, one continuous garland of sound, again takes delight in the easy interweaving of the quintet strands. In the trio, we are suddenly transported to a magic woodland where bird-calls of supernatural clarity and brilliance echo hauntingly. As for the Finale: in Mozart's *Don Giovanni* there is the boisterous merriment of Zerlina, Masetto and the chorus of revelers, "Giovinette, che fate all'amore," which pierces for a moment the rage and cynicism of Act I. Here, at the close of the D major Quintet, the composer again captures this sense of rustic exuberance, now more easy-going, less frenzied, but nonetheless a rousing and nimble-footed jig. (And, to judge by the intricacy of the writing, a jig danced by a conclave of bucolic mathematicians!)

### Viola Quintet in E flat, K. 614

In a letter to his father Leopold in 1777, Mozart facetiously disclaims sensitivities and capacities that were underrated would suffice to show the full range of Mozart's perceptiveness, from the uttermost depth of pensive and resigned melancholy to ebullient gaiety. It is this last that fills his final quintet, K. 614.

The Allegro di molto overflows with irrepressible high spirits. A virtuoso 1st violin disports himself throughout; trill-spiked rhythms pulse from the opening measures. Rollicking dialogues break through from moment to moment. Returns of old thoughts are introduced slyly, provocatively; and the entire movement ends with a coda that is utterly bravura, with raucous outcries in the violins and thematic tumblings in the lower voices.

Mozart is his most gracious, lyric self in the Andante. One cannot improve on Alfred Einstein's description of this chapter: "Brilliance, workmanship, repose and joy in creation, all together."

In the Menuetto, Mozart's other side: the courtly man of the 18th century, but of the late 18th—spunky, swaggering, in the face of impending change. The trio section with its rolling melody, its oom-pah fillers and its drone bass, offers a glimpse of rustic life that is rather more sympathetic and real than Marie Antoinette affected in her Versailles years.

The finale is carried off with such ease and calm that its almost insane virtuosity is made to seem normal: the normalcy of a five-man round-dance performed on a high tight-rope.

This quintet, incidentally, was the last chamber-work composed by Mozart, dating from April 1791 (except for the *Adagio and Rondo* for water-harmonica, flute, oboe, viola and cello, K. 617, in May of that year). *The Magic Flute* was written in July; *La Clemenza di Tito*, in September; the *Clarinet Concerto* in October; the *Requiem* was still in progress when Mozart died on December 5. He was a month short of his thirty-sixth birthday, at the height of his vast powers.

## The Fine Arts Quartet (pages 10-11)

This distinguished American ensemble traces its beginnings to the early 1940s in Chicago. Military service in World War II intervened, however, and it was not until 1946 that the definitive career of the quartet began, with members Leonard Sorkin and Joseph Stepansky, violins; Shephard Lehnhoff, viola; and George Sopkin, cello. For eight years, the quartet was the staff ensemble at the ABC studios in Chicago, presenting weekly network broadcasts of chamber music, both from the string quartet and mixed ensemble repertoire. Irving Ilmer succeeded Lehnhoff as violist in 1952.

In 1954 (at the start of the relentless divestment of live music by the radio and television stations of America), the FAQ was dismissed from its radio post and embarked on a decade of freelance activity. It was at this point that Stepansky left the group, to be replaced by Abram Loft as second violin.

With the lineup of Sorkin, Loft, Ilmer, and Sopkin, the FAQ established itself as one of the country's foremost concert ensembles, touring throughout America and Canada and (starting in 1958) in Europe, Australia, New Zealand, and the Far East—by the late 70s, the tour list encompassed 270 cities in 28 countries, with repeat appearances in many locales. The quartet was featured a number of times on the *Today Show*, performed on a Chicago production of the Ed Sullivan show, produced 20 hour-length programs on the quartet repertoire for National Educational Television (predecessor of PBS); and, performed many broadcasts on Chicago's good music station, WFMT, as well as taping archival programs for radio stations in England, Germany, Holland, France and elsewhere. The foursome began producing a library of chamber music records that, by the late 70s, comprised 60 LP discs, with works by composers ranging from

J.S. Bach to Charles Wuorinen. LP labels included Concert-Tapes, Concert-Disc, Everest, Musicaphon, Composers Recordings, Inc., Columbia, Vanguard, and Gasparo Records. CD labels include Pantheon, Omega, Boston Skyline, and Music and Arts Programs of America. Over the years, the ensemble also added the Ravinia, Aspen and Edinburgh festivals to an early-career appearance at Tanglewood.

From the 1960-61 season, the FAQ was featured in its own concert series in Chicago and in the city's North Shore suburbs, offering never less than seven pairs of concerts each year. Violist Irving Ilmer left the quartet in 1963, to be succeeded by the Canadian musician Gerald Stanick. That same year, the Fine Arts Quartet was appointed quartet in residence at the University of Wisconsin – Milwaukee. The demands of academic life were now added to the quartet's accustomed activities. Among other items, a campus concert series that paralleled the Chicago/North Shore pair was now on the docket.

In 1968, Stanick resigned, to be replaced by violist Bernard Zaslav. And in 1979, George Sopkin retired and Abram Loft resigned, after 33 years and 25 years of membership, respectively. These four musicians, as well as Leonard Sorkin, went on to further important service in the sphere of chamber music (see bios of the individual members). For the Fine Arts Quartet, however, a distinctive and decades-long period of activity had ended. The quartet of those years, however, lives on in the esteem of many of today's established chamber musicians, as well as of young entrants into the profession, who grew up listening to the recorded performances of the ensemble.
