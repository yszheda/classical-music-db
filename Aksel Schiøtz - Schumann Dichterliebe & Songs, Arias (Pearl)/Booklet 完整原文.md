## AKSEL SCHIØTZ

Modesty, style, fastidiousness, care and deep musicality were the essence of Aksel Schiøtz. His recordings of song cycles, songs and arias are classics of the tenor art and his *Dichterliebe* a quite wonderful memorial.

No singer can have incurred the disapproval of fate to the extent suffered by the Danish tenor Aksel Schiøtz. World war broke out shortly before his operatic debut, and his homeland was invaded by the Nazis. Thus he was robbed of the first six years of what was already a belated professional career. Then, hardly a year after the cessation of hostilities, a brain tumour seriously affected his hearing and vision. The resultant debilitation led to paralysis of the right side of his body. Ultimately, came a second career as a baritone, he was to die, at the age of only 68, of leukaemia.

He was born in 1906 at Roskilde, near Copenhagen. After graduating from the University of Copenhagen, he embarked on a career as a schoolteacher. He was successful vocally while at university and his job at a school in Hillerød not far from Copenhagen permitted him to continue musical ventures. He conducted and acted as soloist with the school choir. In addition to the baroque and classical choral repertoire he developed an active interest in jazz, composing some so-called 'Jazz Oratorios' himself and singing in popular music ensembles.

Schiøtz's contact with the conductor Mogens Wöldike opened the doorway of a professional singing career. Wöldike engaged him as soloist in choral performances at the Christiansborg Castle Church. It was a historic performance of Bach's *Weihnachtsoratorium* which brought about the much wider attention. The Gramophone Company was to offer him a trial recording of Mozart arias.

Surprisingly for a singer who was later to be admired for his numerous recordings of Mozart roles and sacred music, some of his first recordings for Danish HMV were pop songs accompanied by Wurlitzer organ, in which he posed as "The Masked Tenor".

Schiøtz made his operatic debut as Fernando in *Così fan tutte* in 1939. He gave up his teaching career but the war intervened just as this new life was about to take off. He resolved to make the best of the duration, giving concerts and making records, and determined to do nothing to assist the Nazi tyranny.

When he was able to resume his career, he was invited to Glyndebourne in 1946 to share the role of Male Chorus in the world premières of Britten's *The Rape of Lucretia* and so found himself a person of international celebrity as a recitalist, including an acclaimed tour of North America in 1950. In 1955 he took up a professorship at St. Paul University, Minnesota and taught elsewhere in the USA, Canada and Copenhagen. He died in 1975.

The voice is not one of markedly varied colours. It is fundamentally a sweet, delicate, fully adjusted, but smooth-skinned instrument. It is surprisingly strong in the lower register, where there is a baritonal quality, usefully employed when describing the monumental flow of the Rhine or the reprobate low C# in the last song of *Dichterliebe*. The plaintive quality of the voice is particularly suited to the melancholy of the Dowland songs in this collection.

He has always been highly esteemed for his Mozart singing. Tamino's portrait aria finds him a little harsh for my taste but he takes both tenor roles in *Die Entführung aus dem Serail* without technical stress: he puts just the right amount of pathos into Belmonte's aching, upward-seeking held note of 'und bringe mich...'. In Belmonte's first aria from him, simply acts out the narrative of Pedrillo's signal to the escapees, the bottom of the voice taking on an adequate weight here.

The selected selections here are a souvenir of Schiøtz's solo beginnings. His 'Comfort ye my people' receives a most individual interpretation. The recitative is full of thought-provoking rubatos and unusually close tying together of the phrases, while he eschews extraversion in the aria; the English text is impeccably enunciated.

Gerald Moore clearly felt a particular affection for Schiøtz. In his book "Am I Too Loud?" Moore records the singular impact Schiøtz's personality made on him when they met for the recording sessions for *Dichterliebe* in London. "I believe that Aksel Schiøtz was one of the few modest tenors that I have ever met. He was not always talking about himself; he did not tell me where he had been or where he was going, he did not tell me about his successes here and where they were clamouring for him elsewhere. He was, in a word, a gentleman."

The tenor-bass rapport between the two performing artists is clearly evident. Moore adopts a feathery touch in the gentle gentos and keeps in step with his restrained partner where he might have been tempted to make a statement of his own. The endlessly circling semiquavers of the working days are heard by a disturbed cave-dweller, or a deranged dancer, the off-beat accents of 'Ein Jüngling liebt ein Mädchen' are not hammered out.

## DICHTERLIEBE

Robert Schumann left home in 1828 to study law at Leipzig University. That discipline was sadly neglected in favour of piano lessons with Friedrich Wieck, into whose house Schumann eventually moved. The story of his awakening relationship with Friedrich's daughter Clara, nine years his junior, and of her father's opposition, is well known. They were engaged in 1837 but had to endure Friedrich's intense opposition.

A prolonged legal battle was drawing to its climax in 1840 and there was the prospect of Clara at last being free to marry on attaining her majority. Schumann had been writing coded references to Clara, his beloved, in his instrumental compositions for some years. The extremely overblown outburst of Lied writing of 1840 (150 songs in 11 months) was palpably stimulated by the improving prospects of their union and *Dichterliebe* (written in one week in May, at a time when Schumann had been reunited with Clara after a short estrangement) reflects also the stormy side of their relationship. Heine figured strongly in this anxious mirroring of Liebe within.

The Opus 24 Cycle consists entirely of settings of his poetry and from 'Myrthen' and other songs written between February and April, some of the composer's most celebrated is set after Heine.

Heine's mature poems are rarely transparent. He may have had a double life as the stock-in-trade of German romanticism (seven of the sixteen songs of *Dichterliebe* mention flowers of various kinds), but this is a poet who also had a mordantly cynical view of life. He ended up in exile in Paris, lying paralysed by venereal disease. The wounds of his suffering were deep and he rises steadily to the surface even in the earlier *Buch der Lieder*, from which the *Dichterliebe* texts are selected.

Yet there is a third dimension, that of self-mockery. This is evident in the overstretched hyperbole of the last poem used by Schumann: the image of a huge coffin that dominates it is turned into a joke by its size and volume, and the personnel required to handle it. Schumann's music straddles these contradictions and gives perfect form to the choice of text: his mate approaches to the cycle. Schumann engages in the mockery. He enacts the charm and simple faith inherent in the poems — those elements which cannot be treated as miniatures, with an average length of little more than a minute and a half. What could be more conventional than the words of 'Im wunderschönen Monat Mai', with their uncomplicated association of the emergency of natural phenomena in spring with awakening love? But Schumann has left the door open for unease in opening the song in an unrelentingly tonality and ending in an unresolved dominant seventh chord.

Most of these recordings were originally only available in Denmark during the war. When they first became issued at large after the war, they caused a stir amongst a young generation of record collectors, starved of recent recordings. Sadly the supply dried up in 1946 with Schiøtz's illness and for some reason his purely Danish material was not issued in the UK. Luckily some of the best is available on Pearl GEMM CD 9194 and *Die Schöne Müllerin* will be issued on Pearl, but there is much more, which, hopefully, will one day see the light of day.
