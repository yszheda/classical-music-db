## 艺术家介绍：Artur Pizarro
Renowned for his lyrical poeticism and outstanding virtuosity, Artur Pizarro won the Leeds International Piano Competition in 1990. Artur performs regularly throughout the world with the leading orchestras and the world's most distinguished conductors including Esa-Pekka Salonen, Sir Andrew Davis, Charles Dutoit, Franz Welser-Möst and Sir Simon Rattle.

Artur regularly appears as a recitalist and chamber musician. As a recitalist Artur has performed at many of the world's most prestigious venues and festivals including Washington's Kennedy Centre, The Wigmore Hall, The Aldeburgh Festival, Neues Gewandhaus, Frankfurt Alte Oper, Theatre du Chatelet, Musée D'Orsay, Zurich Tonhalle, the BBC Proms, and the NHK and Orchard Halls in Japan. Artur's regular chamber music appearances have partnered him with Raphael Oleg, Christian Altenburger, Truls Mork, Toby Hoffmann, the St Lawrence and the Muir Quartets. In November 2005 "The Artur Pizarro Piano Trio" with violinist Raphael Oleg and Cellist Josephine Knight will make their debut at London's Wigmore Hall performing works by Rachmaninov and Frank Bridge.

Artur Pizarro has recorded extensively for Collins Classics, Hyperion Records, Harmonia Mundi, Naxos and Linn Records to great critical acclaim. In February 2003 Artur Pizarro released a CD of Beethoven Piano Sonatas for Linn records to outstanding critical acclaim: 'Let no one say there is no room for another set of established masterpieces when the pianist is possessed with this sort of recreative energy and exuberance' Gramophone - Beethoven piano sonatas Linn CKD 244. Throughout 2003-04 Artur Pizarro performed the complete cycle of Beethoven Piano Sonatas at St John's Smith Square in London. All 8 concerts were broadcast on BBC Radio 3's 'PERFORMANCE ON 3' and the cycle earned Artur a Royal Philharmonic Society Award Nomination for 'Best Series'.

On February 1st 2005 Artur Pizarro launched a CD for Linn Records 'REMINISCENCES', CKD248, a collection of Chopin's best loved works for solo piano. The launch was marked by a special concert at St John's Smith Square on February 1st 2005.

Artur's recording of Volume 1 of the complete piano works of Rodrigo for Naxos (Naxos 8.557272) which was released in January 05, was chosen as Editors Choice in this April's Gramophone Magazine. 'Perceptive, Sympathetic, Brilliant' - Ivan March GRAMOPHONE APRIL 2005.

Throughout 2005-06 Artur Pizarro will perform the complete solo piano works of Ravel and Debussy in a special concert series for BBC Radio 3 from St John's Smith Square in London.

## 作品介绍：FRANZ LISZT (1811-1886) HUNGARIAN RHAPSODIES NOS 1-19
Franz Liszt couldn't live without the Lords of the Earth. 'This evening I shall be going to the German theatre [...] Afterwards I shall hasten to the gypsies'. Their 'sort of music is, for me, a kind of opium, of which I am sometimes in great need'. He was exalted by the matter of their art; bewitched by the manner of their artistry. An unfettered people transforming the music of strangers and places into their own, slave to no printed page, their memory their fortune. Players of soul and mood. Improvisers beyond dream. The gypsies, he wrote in his book Des Bohémiens et leur musique en Hongrie (1859), 'do not recognise in music, any more than in ordinary life, the force of any principle, law, rule or discipline whatsoever. Everything is good, and everything permitted, provided that it pleases them - provided that their inner feeling coincides, or even goes beyond [...] For the gypsy, art is not a science that one may learn [...] it is a mystic song - a language which, though sublimated, is clear to the initiated [...] The entire possessions of the gypsy [consist] of a language and a scale - these together forming their palladium'.

A compote of mainly unacknowledged rural legacy and urban popular music ('the use and fruit [mattering more than] the property'), the Hungarian Rhapsodies - or 'Hungarian gypsy-rhapsodies' as Rahel Varnhagen von Ense called them in her Diary - 'might fairly be regarded,' their creator asserted, 'as a National Epic - a Bohemian Epic - and the strange tongue in which its strains would be delivered would be no stranger than everything else done by the people from whom it emanated'. For him they stood as 'a kind of patriotic Anthology' born of 'childhood memories of Bihari and other gypsy celebrities, and [...] my excursions across country amidst bands of gypsy musicians from Sopron, Pozsony, Pest, etc', the whole fabled narration fashioned out of 'themes, traits, and characteristic features', 'communicated [...] in generous profusion, both at the piano and in musical notation' by aristocrats and musicians sundry, 'retained and reproduced in my own manner' (letter to Heinrich Ehrlich, Rome, 30 March 1864).

'Perfect creations of their own kind', Bartók claimed, the Rhapsodies impress for their embodiment of, and attempt to write down, a largely lost performance/musical legacy. Visually, there's nothing like them: here is music, Hungarian precedents aside, of an appearance, character, expectation and descriptive wording quite unlike anything in Western classical tradition. The rhythms (free/slow, metric/quick) are complex, the ornamentation and cadenzas intricate and random, the scales foreign. Time stretched (a capriccio, fermata, tempo rubato), time strict, time accelerated, is the spirit of their world. Idiomatic 'Hungarian' phrase endings, idiosyncratic 'Liszt' coda finishes (in particular Nos 1, 5, 7, 9, 13, 15, 19), is their credential signature. The declamatory lassú and blood-quickening friss of 18th century verbunkos - recruiting dances used to enlist Hungarians into the Austrian Imperial army - is their first structural reference; interlaced variation, paraphrase and fantasy their most obvious construction method; an inclination towards non-routine key schemes, even 'progressive tonality', their frequent dynamic. Sonically, they stand without compeer, the piano turned into a transcendental camp-fire resonance of violin, clarinet, drum and un-damped cimbalom - what Walker happily calls 'the "sonic surface" of the gypsy band'.

The origins of Nos 1-15 lie partially in Liszt's notes and sketchbooks from his two visits to Hungary in 1839-40 and 1846-47. And in a series of 'detached pieces' or 'transcription processes' (Liszt's description) published at intervals between 1840 and 1847, comprising eleven Magyar Dalok - Ungarische Nationalmelodien [MD] and six Magyar Rhapsodiák - Rhapsodies hongroise [MR]. A further three (including MR 20, the so-called Rumanian Rhapsody), remained in manuscript. A fourth (MR 22), the first version of No 9, was printed in 1848. Three Ungarische Nationalmelodien, drawing on material in MD 5, 4 and 11, appeared c 1840. These various efforts ranged from the simple to the massive: comparing the Fifth Rhapsody with its arresting 1847 prototype is to encounter two radically different approaches to the same material. Nos 10, 11 and 13 likewise. Generally, as with the remodelling of the Paganini and Transcendental Studies, the finalised Weimar versions are more concise and pruned of excess ornament, the pianistic difficulties made more palatable. The first two Hungarian Rhapsodies were published in 1851 (Leipzig), Nos 3-15 in 1853 (Vienna, Mainz), Nos 16-19 between 1882 and 1886 (Budapest). The dedicatees were all influential Hungarians or Hungariaphiles - supporters of Liszt in his quest to become the rhapsode of his land.

### 逐首作品解析
#### No 1 C sharp minor–E major
Sources: Gáspár Bernát, Ferenc Erkel, Károly Thern; Consolations series 1/3 C sharp minor (c 1845); Rêves et fantaisies
Composed 1846 (-47?), dedicated to "son ami" the pianist E[de] Zerdahély [Szerdahelyi] - imprisoned subsequently for his part in the 1848-49 Hungarian Uprising, later a guest of Liszt's at the Altenburg, Weimar: 'proven haven for fugitives on the run' (Alan Walker). The lassú opens with a variant of the long-short, short-long rhythm introducing the Fourteenth Rhapsody (in the same bass register but initially monodic, quasi recitativo, not chordal). This permeates the first half, informing both the idea of the E major Andante (quasi moderato), associated with Erkel (1844), and that of the ensuing D flat Andantino to a Bernát melody (published 1847). The friss is based on a theme by Thern (1842).

#### No 2 C sharp minor–F sharp major
Source: Heinrich Ehrlich
Composed 1847, dedicated to Count Ladislas Teleky. Ehrlich's 'very strong claim' to the substance and success of this cheval de bataille is chronicled in his autobiography (1893). As a young man in 'very straitened circumstances', he'd given Liszt some Hungarian Fantasies to play on a tour of Hungary, 'In 1852, on my way to Paris, I visited Liszt in Weimar [...] he played me his latest Rhapsody [No 2]. "But that is the Hungarian Fantasy [Konzertstück in ungarischen Weisen, Op 1] I gave you in [Vienna, summer] 1846 for you to play in Pest!" [...] "What?" he replied, "I thought they were themes you had arranged!" - "Yes, but the themes were my own." - "Well, in that case I shall write on the title-page d'après des motifs de M Ehrlich" [he didn't ...] there [is] a vast difference between the use made of national themes already known and that made of wholly original ones entrusted to an artist by a youthful composer [...] I left it to him to judge the feelings which [come] over me when I [...] hear this most popular Rhapsody and recall its genesis'. Notable for its orientally colourful gypsy-troupe allusions (shimmering cimbalon, lascivious violin, nasal clarinet), the work is famous in the friska (marked for two fff brio assai statements (C sharp major, E major) each incorporating a pair of Balkan-accented 3+2+3 bars. The bronzed, rhetorical opening quintessentialises bardic magnificence.

#### No 3 B flat minor-major/G minor
Sources: Traditional; MD 11, first half (by 1843)
Dedicated to Count Leo Festetics (who, together with Casimir Esterházy, accompanied Liszt on his historic entry into Pest, 23 December 1839). István Szelenyi (Neue Liszt-Ausgabe, 1972) observes that 'the melodies Liszt uses here presumably come from those parts of Hungary that were then also inhabited by Rumanians'. The opening theme is from a recruiting song included subsequently in the Szini collection (1865): 'The grass is frosty, don't eat it my beloved horse/Take my to my rose tonight'. Featuring cimbalon, double-bass and violin cadenza effects, orientally gapped 'gypsy' scales (Aeolian minor with sharpened fourth and seventh in the quicker middle section, quasi verbunkos), 'heel-clicking' cadences, and accompaniment chords to be played, according to Lina Ramann (1901), 'with popular accent' (detached), the form of the whole, atypically, is ABA rounded off by a coda combining elements from A and B. The shortest of the Rhapsodies (66 bars).

#### No 4 E flat major
Sources: Antal Csermák; MD 7 (by 1840)
Dedicated to Count Casimir Esterházy (with whom Liszt stayed en route between Pest and Vienna, January 1840). 'Play the beginning [recruiting song] like a Knight of the Golden Fleece,' the composer exalted to August Stradal in a masterclass. 'Always slow down, very gypsy-like, at the end of the [Sixth Rhapsody-style Allegretto octave] theme. Each repetition a degree faster' (Rome, 31 December 1885, Göllerich Diary). The expressive qualification of the lassú's opening tempo marking, altieramente, signifies 'stately, proud'. The second subject (Andantino) and the friss derive from Antal Csermák.

#### No 5 E minor Héroide-élégiaque
Sources: Joseph Koschovitz; MD 6 G minor, lassú (by 1840); MR 12 (by 1847)
Dedicated to Countess Sidonie Reviczky. 'A military piece! Like the funeral procession of a distinguished major' (Liszt, Weimar 22 June 1884, Göllerich Diary). Based on a work by Koschovitz, with a cantabile theme in the major, un poco più mosso, remembering in haunted shadow the trio of the Funeral March from Chopin's Op 35 Sonata. 'Not the strength of the victor, but the heroic courage that is born of pain,' Ramann says, informs the epic final climax, fortissimo con summa passione.
