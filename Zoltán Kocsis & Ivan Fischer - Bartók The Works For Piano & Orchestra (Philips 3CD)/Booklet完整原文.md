# BARTÓK'S WORKS FOR PIANO AND ORCHESTRA
*By ANDRÁS WILHEIM*

Bartók's original intention was to become a concert pianist. Though performance later ranked third to composition and ethnomusicological research, which he considered more important to his career, giving concerts being more a way of earning a living, there is no doubt that he remained one of the greatest pianists of his day and an incomparable interpreter of his own works. This too had an influence on his career as a pianist, as concert managers asked him to perform mainly his own works. It would not be appropriate to separate the various genres of Bartók's integral, indissoluble output, since a composer, while writing a particular work, never considers it to be of secondary importance. Nevertheless, Bartók's piano pieces and concertos occupy a special place, since he wrote most of them to perform himself, being able to count on his own abilities in the performance of the most exacting parts. These works, though they may be his most personal and informal ones, are also the most complex and theoretical.

There is a widely held view, presumably because his notations of folk music are particularly detailed and accurate, that Bartók's scores consist of precise manuscripts provided with the greatest possible amounts of information. In other words, performers have only to read his music very carefully and they are certain to find all the performing directions they need. One must not, however, forget that in the case of the piano works Bartók worked out their final form from several concert performances. Indeed, he sometimes made major alterations to the text of the score itself, as is borne out by the revised editions that appeared of some of his works. The copies he used himself (for Bartók always played from music) are full of alterations, large and small, for example in expression and tempo markings. Nor should one forget the fingerings, which can be greatly significant in decisions concerning matters of phrasing and shaping.

Apart from the scores, there is another important source for Bartók's piano works that cannot be neglected: the composer's own interpretations. A good number of Bartók's own recordings (though fewer than we might have wished), have survived, as have numerous amateur recordings with Bartók playing some of his major piano works. Disregarding the random, chance elements in every interpretation, these performances offer the discerning listener a glimpse into a stratum of works scarcely to be obtained from a mere study of the score. Although it is an incalculable loss that there is no commercial recording of Bartók's interpretations of his own piano concertos, there are fortunately a few amateur recordings (of radio programmes, recorded on X-ray foil) in a private collection with Bartók playing his Rhapsody Op. 1 and the Piano Concerto No. 2. The abysmal quality of these copies, which do not even preserve the whole of the works, do no more than hint at the true value of Bartók's performance, particularly his ensemble performance with the orchestra, but the scale of his artistry is conveyed and they have estimable value as documents. A comparison of the recordings with the scores reveals many tiny nuances which are not notated but which in Bartók's playing are almost a structural feature. One can say without exaggeration that they form an integral part of the work. The question arises how a modern performer may use this new information, and to what extent the experience gained here can be applied in other Bartók works.

It goes without saying that these sources must not be ignored. One cannot subscribe to the view (still favoured by many performers) that Bartók's playing belongs to a bygone era, that its value is only historical and need not be considered by performers today. Even if this were so with Bartók's playing of works by other composers (though, in fact, his interpretations of the classics carry the force of revelation), both for themselves and from the standpoint of performing Bartók's own music, it is clearly an error in the case of Bartók's own works. It is not merely the philological task of collecting all the data and evidence to do with Bartók and his works (letters, interviews, analyses, or indeed recordings). This data must in some manner be built into the accepted present-day interpretation.

Inevitably, such a conclusion is related to the problem of the present state of Bartók's output, which remains so far unresolved. Performers of Bartók's works feel more and more a dire need for a reliable, textually authentic edition of his works. A critical complete edition is a basic requirement, not a needless luxury. The editions published in Bartók's lifetime, repeatedly corrected and reworked by him, the copies used by him at concerts, and post-war reprints edited along various lines, sometimes deficiently and at other times erroneously, mean that there is such a tangle of sources that the assistance of a specialist is required to help the performer find his way among them. Sadly studies with the best intentions will go astray if they are not based upon a thorough knowledge of the sources. The urgent need to publish better scores is not assisted by the public's belief in Bartók's meticulous accuracy, and the consequent assumption that there can be nothing wrong with the ones at present in existence.

These recordings represent the first attempt to provide as far as possible scores that are accurate, by the careful collation of sources available in Hungary. They are based on the printed scores in circulation (which means naturally all the editions so far of the relevant works, with the more or less obvious printing errors corrected), the interpretation incorporating what can be learned from authentic Bartók sources, not least the recorded fragments of Bartók's playing of the Rhapsody Op. 1 and the Piano Concerto No. 2.

Space forbids a detailed list of these sources, corrections, and modifications, but an attentive listener may observe that the most significant changes in these recordings are in the Rhapsody, the Piano Concerto No. 1, and — after comparing the editorial suggestions of the printed score with Bartók's manuscript, and thus reinterpreting questions that remain unanswered in the manuscript — the Piano Concerto No. 3.

# HISTORY AND COMPOSITION OF THE WORKS

## RHAPSODY OP. 1

This work was originally written for solo piano in the autumn of 1904 at Gerlicepuszta, and dedicated to Emma Gruber (later Mrs. Kodály). Several versions have been published: a piano version, a shorter version of this including only the first, slow section (ending with a coda that does not feature in the versions performed today), and a later, revised, orchestrated, and partly expanded version. A still later revised edition of this last version is also known, which may be considered the final form. (In 1905, Bartók entered the work with orchestral accompaniment for the composers' category of the Rubinstein competition in Paris, but to no avail. The two-piano version of the Rhapsody owes its existence to this competition.)

---

## SCHERZO OP. 2

This piece dates from the summer of 1904, and the orchestration is thought to have been completed in 1905. The first performance was due to take place on March 15, 1905, but the experience of the first rehearsal, where the orchestra was unprepared and recalcitrant, persuaded Bartók to withdraw the work. It was, in fact, never performed in his lifetime, although there is evidence from a number of sources that the composer did not abandon the idea of a performance until the early 1910's. Though he must have felt the importance of the piece, and never cancelled its opus number, he nevertheless did not include it in catalogues of his works, or insist upon its being published.

The manuscript formed part of Bartók's legacy and was published only in 1961, edited by Denijs Dille. (The composer's transcription of the work for two pianos remains unpublished.)

Bartók completed all but the orchestration of the last 17 bars, the missing bars being provided by Tibor Serly. Although the fair copy of the score can be considered as final, Bartók was prevented from adding the final touches to the expression and tempo markings. He was able to copy the score, but not to revise it.

## PIANO CONCERTO NO. 1

The work dates from between August and November 1926, a time when Bartók was composing several other piano pieces (Sonata, "Out-of-Doors," Nine Little Piano Pieces). These constitute the prelude to a new creative period and, from many points of view, a new style. After several years' silence (when his composing activities were restricted to orchestrating some of his earlier works and writing the "Village Scenes"), Bartók started out in a new direction for himself, both in his general orientation and his technique of composition and orchestration, interpreting individually everything he had come to know in European music from the early 1920's, as well as incorporating elements into his work learnt from early Baroque composers, whose works he frequently played and studied (and transcribed) around this time. But the ample crop of piano pieces from 1926 also had a practical purpose: Bartók felt the need to freshen up his concert programmes. However (and perhaps not by coincidence), the composition of the work he intended as the most important, a piano concerto, caused the greatest difficulty. As he wrote to his wife, "Somehow... after such a long time not working, I had become like a man who, after lying in bed motionless for a long, long time, tries at long last to use his arms and legs, standing up and taking a step or two. Such a man cannot just foot it up step by step."

The Piano Concerto No. 1 had its first performance on July 1, 1927, in Frankfurt, played by Bartók and conducted by Furtwängler. The first edition of the score appeared in time for the première, but with so many mistakes that Bartók had it produced again. Unfortunately, this revised edition is not perfect either, and its inconsistencies and misprints remain to be rectified in a future edition. The performance therefore calls for much circumspect and careful textual preparation.

## PIANO CONCERTO NO. 2

Bartók wrote this work between October 1930 and October 1931. In a description he wrote in 1939, he spoke in unusual detail about what prompted the work, and the circumstances from which it arose, as well as about his opinions on composition in general: "I decided... to compose my Second Concerto as a counterweight to the First, in other words, with fewer difficulties for the orchestra and more pleasant themes. This aim of mine explains the more popular and easier character of most of the themes..."

## PIANO CONCERTO NO. 3

Bartók began composing the Piano Concerto No. 3 in the spring of 1945, and for the first time in the history of his piano works with orchestral accompaniment, intended it not for himself, but for his wife. In all probability he reckoned that his fatal illness would prevent him from being the soloist, perhaps thinking that his wife would later need the performing rights to be reserved for her. The first performance was eventually given by György Sándor, with Eugene Ormandy conducting.

Bartók completed all but the orchestration of the last 17 bars, the missing bars being provided by Tibor Serly. Although the fair copy of the score can be accepted as final, Bartók was no longer able to make the final touches to the expression and tempo markings. He was able to copy the score, but not to revise it.

## MUSIC FOR STRINGS, PERCUSSION, AND CELESTA

Bartók composed this work in Budapest between June and September 1936, to a commission from Paul Sacher, conductor of the Basle Chamber Orchestra. It was first performed in Basle on January 21, 1937, with Sacher conducting.

Although included here among Bartók's music for piano and orchestra, the work is not a piano concerto. The piano has no greater independence and is no more important than the other instruments. (Indeed in some performances it seems not to acquire even this level of significance.) Here Bartók's reply to a request for him to play the piano part of the work must be quoted: "No, because it is not a solo part."

# ZOLTÁN KOCSIS

The enormously successful career of Zoltán Kocsis was propelled by his winning two prestigious piano competitions in Hungary, the Beethoven Competition in Hungarian Radio in 1970 and the Liszt Prize in 1973. Since that time, the Budapest-born artist has toured the world developing a large and devoted international following and garnering enthusiastic accolades. "With fabulous natural technique and matching temperament, he could practically outplay anyone you care to mention in the virtuoso field," said a critic from "The Times" of London. "Kocsis is a blazingly original pianist" who "plays with astonishing variety of tone and rhythmic emphasis," commented Richard Dyer of the "Boston Globe."

Zoltán Kocsis has performed with many of the world's leading orchestras, including the BBC Symphony, Berlin Philharmonic, Chicago Symphony, London Symphony, Los Angeles Philharmonic, Royal Philharmonic and San Francisco Symphony, and has appeared under the baton of many eminent conductors. His summer festival appearances have included the Hollywood Bowl, Ravinia, Edinburgh, Helsinki, Menton, Paris, Prague, Lucerne, and Salzburg.

In 1975, he received a personal invitation from Sviatoslav Richter to perform at his festival in Tours, where he replaced Maurizio Pollini. Not long after, he and Richter played several duo recitals in Tours. He has also captivated audiences in the United States, appearing on the stages of Carnegie Hall and the John F. Kennedy Center, in Singapore, and in the major cities of Japan, and the Philippines.

Highlighting his 1986-87 schedule was a recital tour of North America which included Carnegie Hall, Chicago, Montreal, and Ottawa. He was also engaged to appear with the Toronto Symphony under the direction of Andrew Davis, the Minnesota Orchestra with Leonard Slatkin, and the Indianapolis Symphony.

Born in 1952, Zoltán Kocsis started playing the piano and composing at the age of five. When he was 11 he attended the Bartók Conservatory in Budapest, where he studied piano and composition. In 1968 he was admitted to the Budapest Liszt Academy of Music and studied under Professors Pál Kadosa and Ferenc Rados. He graduated with distinction in 1973 and in 1976 was appointed professor in the academy's piano department.

He was not yet 30 when he was awarded the Kossuth Prize, the highest honour in Hungary. He is also an Eminent Artist of the Hungarian People's Republic. Together with conductor Iván Fischer, he is co-founder and artistic director of the Budapest Festival Orchestra and has been its regular soloist since its inception.

# BARTÓKS WERKE FÜR KLAVIER UND ORCHESTER
*Von ANDRÁS WILHEIM*

Bartók wollte ursprünglich Konzertpianist werden. Obwohl die pianistische Tätigkeit hinter dem Komponieren und der musikethnologischen Forschungsarbeit, denen er für seine künstlerische Entwicklung größere Bedeutung beimaß, erst an dritter Stelle rangierte — das Konzertieren sah er eher als eine Möglichkeit, seinen Lebensunterhalt zu verdienen — der größten Pianisten seiner Zeit und ein unverglichener Interpret seiner eigenen Werke blieb. Auch dies hatte Einfluß auf seine Karriere als Pianist, denn die Konzertagenten ließen ihn hauptsächlich seine eigenen Werke spielen. Es wäre nicht richtig, die verschiedenen Gattungen des Bartókschen Gesamtwerkes voneinander zu trennen, da ein Komponist, während er an einem bestimmten Werk arbeitet, dieses nie für zweitrangig oder nebensächlich erachtet. Gleichwohl nehmen Bartóks Klavierstücke und Klavierkonzerte eine Sonderstellung ein, schrieb er die meisten doch für eigenen Vortrag, bei dem er sich auf seine virtuosen Fertigkeiten bei der Ausführung und auch der technisch anspruchsvollsten Partien verlassen konnte. Obwohl diese Werke vielleicht seine persönlichsten und zwanglosesten Schöpfungen darstellen, sind sie auch höchst komplexer und spekulativer Natur.

Es besteht die weitverbreitete Ansicht — vermutlich weil seine Notierungen ungarischer Volksmusik besonders detailliert und genau sind —, daß Bartóks Partituren aus präzisen Manuskripten mit der größtmöglichen Menge an Worten: die Ausführenden brauchen seine Musik nur sehr sorgfältig zu lesen, und sie finden darin mit Sicherheit alle Spielanweisungen, die sie benötigen. Man darf jedoch dabei nicht vergessen, daß seine Klavierwerke, deren endgültige Form erst nach einigen Konzertaufführungen ausarbeitete und nach Festlegung veränderte er arbeitete auch stets größere Änderungen am musikalischen Text seiner vor. Revidierte Ausgaben, die von ihm selbst erschienen, bestätigen das. Die Exemplare, die er selbst benutzte (Bartók spielte immer nach Noten) sind voller großer und kleiner Änderungen, beispielsweise bei Vortrags- und Tempobezeichnungen außer acht lassen, die bei Entscheidungen über Phrasierungs- und Gestaltungsprobleme von großer Bedeutung sein können.

Abgesehen vom Notentext gibt es noch eine andere wichtige Quelle für das Studium von Bartóks Klavierwerken, und zwar die eigenen Interpretationen des Komponisten. Eine beträchtliche Anzahl seiner Einspielungen (wenn auch weniger, als wir uns gewünscht hätten) ist erhalten, desgleichen zahlreiche Amateuraufnahmen, in denen Bartók einige seiner größeren Klavierwerke spielt. Klammert man die unabsichtlichen, zufälligen Elemente in jeder Interpretation aus, dann gewähren diese Einspielungen dem aufmerksamen Hörer einen Einblick in eine Schicht der Werke, die sich aus einem bloßen Studium der Partitur kaum erschließt. Obwohl es einen unermeßlichen Verlust darstellt, daß von Bartóks eigenen Interpretationen seiner Klavierkonzerte keine kommerziellen Aufnahmen existieren, gibt es in einer Privatsammlung zum Glück ein paar Amateurmitschnitte (von Rundfunkaufnahmen, festgehalten auf Röntgenkonstrastfolie), wo Bartók seine Rhap- sodie op.1 und das zweite Klavierkonzert spielt. Die schlechte Qualität dieser Mitschnitte, die nicht einmal das Werk in seiner Gesamtheit enthält, vermittelt nicht mehr als einen oberflächlichen Eindruck von Bartóks Vortrag, insbesondere von seinem Zusammenspiel mit dem Orchester, doch spiegeln diese Aufnahmen den ganzen Umfang seines

Künstlertums und sind daher als Dokumente von unschätzbarem Wert. Ein Vergleich der Aufnahmen mit den Partituren läßt viele feine Unterschiede erkennen, die nicht notiert sind, die jedoch in Bartóks Spiel fast ein strukturelles Merkmal darstellen. Man kann ohne Übertreibung sagen, daß sie einen integrierenden Bestandteil des jeweiligen Werkes bilden. Es ergibt sich nun die Frage, auf welche Weise ein heutiger Interpret diese neuen Informationen verwerten kann und inwieweit die hier gewonnene Erfahrung auf andere Werke Bartóks anwenden läßt.

Selbstverständlich dürfen diese Quellen nicht unberücksichtigt bleiben. Man kann nicht die (von vielen Interpreten noch immer vertretene) Auffassung gutheißen, Bartóks Spielweise gehöre einer vergangenen Epoche an, habe nur historischen Wert und brauche daher von heutigen Interpreten nicht in Betracht gezogen zu werden. Selbst wenn dies für Bartoks Vortrag der Werke anderer Komponisten zu träfe wäre, sei dies in bezug auf Bartóks eigene Werke nicht, das ist zu sagen, daß in bezug auf Bartoks eigene Werke eine eindeutig irrige Meinung. Es geht nicht nur um die reine philologische Arbeit, sondern alle Angaben und Dokumente zu sammeln, die mit Bartók und seinen Werken zu sammeln, die mit Bartók und seinen Werken zu tun haben.

Unzweifelhaft steht eine solche Schlußfolge- rung in Zusammenhang mit dem bisher ungelösten Problem des gegenwärtigen Zustandes von Bartóks Gesamtoeuvre. Bartók-Interpreten benötigen dringend eine verläßliche textauthentische Ausgabe seiner Kompo- sitionen. Eine kritische Gesamtausgabe ist ein grundlegendes Erfordernis und kein überflüs- siger Luxus. Die zu Lebzeiten des Komponi- sten erschienenen und ihm immer wieder korrigierten und umgearbeiteten Ausgaben, das von ihm bei Konzerten verwendete Notenmaterial sowie die nach verschiedenen Gesichtspunkten edierten, mitunter unzurei- chenden, mitunter regelrecht falschen Nach- kriegsdrucke lassen ein derartiges Durch- einander in der Quellenlage erkennen, daß die Hinzuziehung eines Spezialisten erforderlich ist, der dem Ausführenden helfen hilft, sich in diesem Wirrwarr zurechtzufinden. Leider erwei- sen sich selbst die mit den besten Absichten unternommenen Bemühungen als verfehlt, wenn ihnen nicht eine gründliche Quellen- kenntnis zugrunde liegt. ## CONCERTO POUR PIANO N° 1

Chronologiquement il se situe entre août et novembre 1926, époque au cours de laquelle Bartók rédigea plusieurs autres pièces pour piano (Sonate, «En plein air», «Neuf Petites Pièces»). Tout cela marquait le prélude d'une

nouvelle période créatrice et, à partir de plusieurs points de vue, une nouvelle façon. Après plusieurs années de silence (when his composing activities were restricted to orchestrating some of his earlier works and writing the "Village Scenes"), Bartók started out in a new direction for himself, both in his general orientation and his technique of composition and orchestration, interpreting individually everything he had come to know in European music from the early 1920's, as well as incorporating elements into his work learnt from early Baroque composers, whose works he frequently played and studied (and transcribed) around this time. But the ample crop of piano pieces from 1926 also had a practical purpose: Bartók felt the need to freshen up his concert programmes. However (and perhaps not by coincidence), the composition of the work he intended as the most important, a piano concerto, caused the greatest difficulty. As he wrote to his wife, "Somehow... after such a long time not working, I had become like a man who, after lying in bed motionless for a long, long time, tries at long last to use his arms and legs, standing up and taking a step or two. Such a man cannot just foot it up step by step."

The Piano Concerto No. 1 it had its first performance on 1er juillet 1927 in Frankfurt with Bartok as soloist conducted by Furtwängler. The first edition of the score ap- peared in time for the première, but with so many mistakes that Bartók produced it again. Unfortunately, this revised edition is not perfect either, and its inconsistencies and misprints remain to be rectified in a future edition. The performance therefore calls for much circumspect and careful textual preparation.

Pourtant, il apparaît que Bartók ne put y ajouter les touches finales concernant les indications d'expression et de tempo. Certes, il copia la partition. Mais il ne put la réviser.
