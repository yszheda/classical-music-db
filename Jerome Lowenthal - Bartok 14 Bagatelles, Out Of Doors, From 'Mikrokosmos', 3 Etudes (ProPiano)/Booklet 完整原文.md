## 内页 1 (HIPPO_20221227_0003.jpg)

### JEROME LOWENTHAL

Jerome Lowenthal has distinguished himself as an artist of rare musical vision and integrity. His wide-ranging presence in a career spanning more than three decades has made him an important figure on the international music scene.

Born in Philadelphia, Mr. Lowenthal made his debut at the age of 13 with the Philadelphia Orchestra. He studied with three legendary figures: William Kapell, Edward Steuermann, and at The Ecole Normale de Musique on a Fulbright Grant with Alfred Cortot. His studies in Paris culminated in a premiere license de concert, a recording contract with Pathe Marconi, and performances with The Orchestre de la Societe des Concerts du Conservatoire. While in Europe, he won prizes in the international competitions of Brussels, Bolzano and Darmstadt. Moving then to Jerusalem, he became concert-lecturer at the Jerusalem Academy and performed annually with the Israel Philharmonic.

In 1961, Jerome Lowenthal returned to the United States and since then has appeared with virtually all its major orchestra in the United States including the Chicago Symphony, Boston Symphony, Los Angeles Philharmonic, National Symphony, Baltimore Symphony, Cleveland Orchestra, Pittsburgh Symphony, St. Louis Symphony and the Minnesota Orchestra. He has performed with some of the most distinguished conductors of our time including Barenboim, Bernstein, Dorati, Giulini, Mehta, Monteux, Ormandy, Ozawa, Steinberg, Stokowski and Tennstedt, and has recorded under Sergiu Comissiona with The Vancouver Symphony Orchestra, and The London Symphony Orchestra.

Mr. Lowenthal's voluminous repertoire includes 59 performed concerti. He plays works by many living composers, among whom George Rochberg, Jay Reise and Ned Rorem have composed works expressly for him. He has given all-Rochberg recitals and presented the world premiere of Rorem's Piano Concerto in Six Movements with the Pittsburgh Symphony, later recording it with the Louisville Orchestra.

Chamber music is an important part of Jerome Lowenthal's musical life. He has played duo recitals with cellist Nathaniel Rosen and violinist Itzhak Perlman, as well as two piano programs with his late wife, the Israeli pianist, Ronit Air. He is a regular participant in the festivals of Sitka, Alaska, the Reizend Muziekgezelschap in Holland, and the Music Academy of the West in Santa Barbara. Jerome Lowenthal is a faculty member of The Juilliard School and resides in New York.

## 内页 2 (HIPPO_20221227_0004.jpg)

**Béla Bartók**
14 Bagatelles, Op.6
Out of Doors
From "Mikrokosmos"
3 Etudes, Op.18

In the closing years of our century, Béla Bartók still reigns as the most important 20th-century composer for the piano since Debussy. He enjoyed an active career as a concert pianist himself, and for many years earned his living as a keyboard professor. When he died in 1945 in New York, he left behind a treasure-trove of piano music that still provides an obligatory proving ground for today's most demanding concert works.

Bartók's personal contact with folk music determined the direction his own highly original style would take. It began in rural eastern ethnic music seriously at age twenty-four. With care and passion, he roamed the remote hills and valleys of Hungary and neighboring countries in the early years of this century, collecting folk songs and folk dances with a primitive wax cylinder recorder and phonograph. As he traversed the provinces, he became one of history's first field musicologists and the foremost authority on peasant folk music of Central and Eastern Europe during his lifetime.

His own musical vocabulary grew out of this indigenous music, whose colorful harmonies and rhythms left an indelible mark on his creations.

Bartók was likewise deeply immersed in the great traditions of Western European art music. Overlying his colorful personal idiom and his sensitivity to the sounds of nature, one discovers a rigorous intellectual control inspired by classical formal procedures. His works also harbor a kaleidoscope of colorful modern techniques. In his hands, the piano became an instrument of powerfully percussive rhythms, pungent harmonies and brilliant sonorities. Indeed: his solo piano works rank among the most original statements in the repertoire.

Completed in 1908 in Budapest, the Opus 6 Bagatelles mark the beginning of Bartók's personal style as a composer. Each of these revolutionary little keyboard essays concentrates on a different problem of contemporary style. The first explores bitonality, for example, while the second offers a lively study in major seconds. Chords in fourths, augmented triads, ostinato and other forward-looking techniques that populate later works resonate throughout this early set. Soundscapes span post-Lisztian gestures to barbaric impulses. Moods range from capricious bonfires of folk harmonies met with avant-garde explorations. No.12 reflects the composer's early piano works at the same time that it initiates the climactic so often heard in the rural lands of Hungarian hinterlands. The composer confessed that several of these bagatelles were actually experiments (namely Nos. 1 & 9), and if its more advanced writing beyond what his most forward-thinking contemporaries were doing. The sophisticated set ends with a delightfully whimsical and decidedly modern waltz.

Between June and August of 1926, Bartók created his Out of Doors suite to beef up his own repertoire as a concert pianist. Its five movements offer some of the most vivid keyboard music ever written. The suite opens with a concert piece. In the movements after some of the most vivid keyboard music ever written. The suite opens with a aggressively percussive opening number, a brilliant and colorful showpiece that teeter on the brink of atonality. A poetic "Barcarolle" follows with quartal touches in its melodies. The highly ornamented "Musettes" vibrates with accents of the colorful racket of primitive wind instruments. Then, fourth movement offers an array of night sounds, crickets, frogs, birds, and insect chirrups. The finale, a brilliant toccata-like movement, depicts a wild chase across the Hungarian puszta.

After the piano he composed Out of Doors in 1926, Bartók began toying with the idea of writing music expressly for the teaching of beginners. From conception to finished product, the mighty task of creating the Mikrokosmos occupied the composer for more than a dozen years. He started working on the project in earnest during the summer of 1932 with the young son of his pupil Anni Foder. The final number was reached in 1939. The set comprises 153 pieces which were published in six volumes in 1940. In addition to representing quasi-tonal Bartók, they are doubtless the most imaginative pedagogical pieces ever conceived and have been quoted as a textbook of 20th-century musical devices. The focus of each study in the collection is usually indicated in its title, whether it be a technical problem ("Handschrung"), a contrapuntal device ("Canon"), or the complex syncopations of Middle-European and Balkan dance rhythms. Some of the studies imitate sounds, a few are acoustical experiments, and several are programmatic in nature, recalling the colorful genre pieces of early 18th-century French harpsichord composers, such as Couperin and Rameau.

Bartók designed his monumental project not only as an educational tool, but to encourage thoughtful musicianship and impart an understanding of a wide range of techniques found in 20th-century music: synthetic scales, chords built in fourths, atonal clusters, bitonality. As one reads through the six volumes, the pieces progress from five-finger exercises for beginners to demanding works for the concert hall. The first piece in Volume 6, Free Variations, throbs with rhythmic clusters. The following Subject and Reflection remains true to its title as the left hand presents a mirror inversion of the right throughout the piece.

Though nearly everything in Mikrokosmos was newly created, Bartók does use some authentic folk tunes in the six Bulgarian dances which conclude the landmark series. Their stabbing rhythms and bright, incisive colors provide a dazzling finish for an album devoted to music created by one of the great rhythmic imaginations of modern times. The composer dedicated these six dances to the British pianist Harriet Cohen. Bartók himself often drew on the later volumes for his own recital repertory. In fact, his first public performance of selections from Mikrokosmos on February 7, 1937 in London included seventeen pieces from the set.

Exacting technical studies, the three highly dissonant etudes of Opus 18 have a different aim and are less influenced by folk elements. They further explore new frontiers of keyboard sound and teeter on the brink of atonality. No. 1 focuses on quick expansion and contraction of the hand by way of chromatic octave displacement, as stretches of ninths and tenths constantly challenge the pianist. The impressionistic soundscape of No. 2 touches the realm of Bartók's "night music" while negotiating wide-ranging, asymmetrical, non-repetitive arpeggios. Though less violent than the opening study, the third etude is perhaps Bartók's most atonal solo piano work. Staccato chords in the right hand punctuate moto perpetuo left-hand figurations. Irregular rhythms and complex changing meters inform fleet hand shifts in the first half of the piece before it settles into extended 2/4, 3/4 and 6/8 passages. These virtuosic etudes were composed in 1918 at Rákoskeresztur.

— Kathy Henkel
