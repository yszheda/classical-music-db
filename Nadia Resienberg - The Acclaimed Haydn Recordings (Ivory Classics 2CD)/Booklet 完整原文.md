## Liner Notes

### HAYDN'S PIANO WORKS

"I acknowledge with pleasure the desire of many music lovers to own a complete edition of my piano compositions," wrote Franz Joseph Haydn in 1799, "and I shall set to it in this collection so that which wrongly bears my name shall be weeded out." The collection he referred to was subsequently published in twelve volumes, between 1799 and 1803, but not under the enticing, if erroneous title "Oeuvres complettes." Actually, Haydn was the chief culprit in that misnomer, since by his own admission, he only removed spurious compositions, but deleted as well "those works of my early youth which are not worth preserving."

Later generations, of course, were not at all ready to discard any of the master's works, however slight, and the number of Sonatas which indeed comprise Haydn's "complete works" is up for debate even today.

The original 1800-06 edition contained thirty-four Sonatas. A collection edited by Hugo Riemann, and published in London in 1895, added five more. In 1918, the Austrian Karl Päsler offered fifty-five. Nor is the accordan yet complete. In 1966, a newly published Vienna Urtext edition has upped the total again to sixty-two (including a fragment of a sonata previously considered lost, and attributed to Haydn only very tentatively by Mozarthandlers archivists). The complete Haydn keyboard works fare reasonably well. As many as sixteen concertos are extant and less than twenty works are lost. Of these, Nadia Reisenberg's recital assembles four sets of variations and two superb miniatures – the Sonata and the Capriccio – on which alone Haydn's reputation as a piano composer could safely rest.

Since Haydn considered his keyboard sonatas to be among his minor compositions, he took little care to document them for posterity. The Sonata No.13 in G Major (Hob.XVI:6) was published with five other sonatas in 1768 when the composer was thirty-four years old and in the service of Prince Nicholas Esterházy. The Sonata No.13 in G Major, for Haydn still an early work, follows the style current in Vienna in the middle seventeen hundreds. In some editions it is entitled "Partita" and in others "Divertimento." The opening Allegro presents three melodies in succession accompanied by the left hand; it should well be understood as a piano solo with chamber accompaniment and, in fact, Haydn arranged many of his keyboard sonatas for that combination.

The second movement is a graceful Minuet with a central Trio. The Adagio is a slow, deeply expressive movement, one of Haydn's best. The Finale is a lively sonata-allegro which moves quickly in an almost adagio tempo. The finale is marked Allegro molto – in two parts, each repeated in the manner of Scarlatti.

The Fantasia in C Major (Hob.XVII:4) composed in 1789 for Therese Jansen is also called "Capriccio." Biographer Rosemary Hughes calls this work "bewilderingly free in structure," while Karl Geiringer could "hear in this piece the tone of violins and double-basses, of horns and flutes; moreover a rapid crossing of the hands, the use of large arpeggios, and the distribution of powerful chords between two hands exhibit the composer's concern for purely pianistic devices." The effect that Haydn achieved by holding the notes in the left hand, while the right runs through rapid passages (turns or figures), is rather notable. This fantasia was written by a master who exploited the possibilities of the piano's technique in transcending ideas of an essentially orchestral nature." The marking is simply *Presto*. After an interval in B flat there are two long stretches of predominantly sixteenth-note triplets against sections in A. The prodigal G comes home abruptly, and with even more telling effect than it did in the first place less than a half-dozen times.

The Sonata No.62 in E-flat Major (Hob.XVI:52) was composed in 1794, its afflatus in stature with the very opening chords – dramatic punctuations that set the stage for an unfolding of music of the grandest style. This is, perhaps, one of Haydn's most adventuresome, most original works. In its almost harmonic design, its forays into near-romantic expressionism, its nobility of spirit. Its key relationships are remarkably subtle, and the slow movement has been described as "a tone poem." Haydn's ability in the fact that the middle Adagio is daringly cast in E minor – poles apart, harmonically speaking, from the outer movements in E flat. This slow movement stands as well among the loveliest of all Haydn's keyboard creations. The finale is an ingenious rondo, full of wit and high spirits. By contrast, the Finale is a whirlwind Presto, brimming over with élan and that zesty impertinence which made Haydn at his most inimitable.

The Tema con Variazioni in C Major (Hob.XVII:5) of 1791 permits itself a modicum of temerity. In the first four-part fugato, the thematic index free theme, the Quartet, the story does not change harmonically. With Variation V we are faced squarely into the dark waters of C minor, thence in F flat, and as if some mysterious planet had found itself in G. Variation VI, not summarily, is strongly tonic. The great innovation is not apparent, the last variation returns to the tonic. The work is a C major chord.

The Sonata No.50 in D Major (Hob. XVI:37), published in 1780, is a highly original composition. By 1780 Haydn had acquired an international reputation. The King of Spain insisted that he instruct him in music, Queen Marie Antoinette was honored absolutely by his position with Prince Esterhazy, and the aristocracy of all Europe would count themselves fortunate to entertain anyone once. The most that Haydn was granted were occasional short visits to Vienna where he was heralded by an intrigued group of music publishers who eagerly awaited every new composition that came from his pen. It was for these ladies that Haydn wrote his Sonata No.50 in D Major. Concerning the sisters he wrote to the publisher Artaria: "The Sonatas are rather difficult to play, especially for the ladies, because both their technique and their genuine understanding of the art of music are equal to the greatest masters." Both deserve to become known throughout Europe by music published in their interest. The sonata is constructed in the first movement on two themes, one loud and one soft as is, as progresses by changes in mood rather than by the introduction of new material. The music is unified as if it belongs to a higher sphere. In the second movement, Haydn achieves the most unrelievedly slow movements in the writing of a composer who was famous for his moving adagios. The mood is intensified by the absence of a pause before the final Presto: a good-humored conclusion, but a conclusion nonetheless.

The Arietta con Variazioni in A Major (Hob.XVII:2) dates from 1771. It has come down to us with sixteen variations, although the Haydn-Ellis thematic index lists twenty, the Oeuvre complete edition only twelve. The Landonklaviatur in Weimar has a score with twenty, but not including the No.10 in the Artaria sequence; the minimum total of variations composed is therefore twenty-one. Nineteen is today accepted as final. A theme, however, also having been killed by the composer according to scholars contains. Stylistically the work tends to simulate the gracefully galant manner of Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach, who was Haydn's hero-progenitor. The dialect, not unlike that announced *différentes manières pour le clavecin*. As the work progresses, departing the tonic. Several of the variations are especially delightful: III for its darting sixteenths over a bass in crossed-hand configuration; VIII for its engaging rhythmic displacement with the right hand propelling of Parnassus, XI for its canonic by-play; XV for its feathery leggiero, the usually musing swirl for its immutably Mozartean ways, the final XIX for its Lindler charm.

The Sonata No.53 in E minor (Hob.XVI:34) is dated January 15, 1784. Haydn was then under the spell of a then new, mostly literary, movement known as *Sturm und Drang* (The official translation is "Storm and Stress," however a parenthetical rendering more expressive of its essential nature would be "Storm and Urge".) The language of the opening movement, marked Presto, gives us a glimpse into the future, music that is hovering over the borderline which separates the Here from the Here beyond, the borderline, the borderline of the question and in the answer is *piangendo* (crying). The melody is long and lonely meditation. The last movement (*Molto vivace*) is on, as the strength of its theme is strong and compelling. The movement is a rondo with a coda which provides a fitting conclusion. The three movements, reveal Haydn at the unerring – still disclosed – master of equilibrium. Their sheer gracelessness seems to turn the entire question into a lofty joke, so that the work's final chord leaves us at once both humble and aware.

The Capriccio in G Major ("Acht Sauschneider müssen sein") (Hob.XVII:1) is a longish, rondo, actually composed in 1765, when Haydn was thirty-three years of age. It is dedicated to Haydn's wrote to his publisher Artaria that "I have composed an entirely new capriccio for the piano; its good taste, singularity, and elaborate finish are sure to please." Supposedly, this third (if not first) sonata was composed in 1765/66, and is indeed, the youngest of the Haydn keyboard works. The work is in a single movement, marked *Moderato*. Thematically, it was derived from a Neapolitan *Follia* which Haydn uses as the basis of a sort of minuet before proceeding to write it in D, A minor, F, and G major. Four of these carry some kind of verbal notation except for the *fortissimo* affirmation at the close.

The Sonata No.35 in A-flat Major (Hob.XVI:43), was written in 1783, when the master was 51 years old. At that time Haydn was openly and avowedly taking in the inspirations and influences emanating from his colleague Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. In fact all major pianos neglect Haydn predominate at work on pieces of chamber music. The Sonata No.35 in A-flat Major seems to be a case in point made by Haydn's biographers, namely that it is in point made by Haydn's biographers, namely that it is in point made by Haydn's biographers. The entire work is an apotheosis of graceful gaiety – a beguiling cherub having a happy day. The first movement is a *Moderato* and the second movement has the character of a slow one, but consists of a *Minuetto I* and a *Minuetto II*. The final movement, *Presto*, finds Haydn close to a more actual teasing by substituting in the final statements of the Rondo theme the interval of an octave for the original interval of a fourth; and then, as if to make the point twice, he does not as often as possible. All throughout the work, the economy of means and the transparency of tone remind one of the finely chiseled a rocail jeweler that we are indebted make up the technique of chamber music which happened to be the master's main preoccupation at that time.

With a lyrically dulce, broadly spaced theme stated Moderato, the Arietta con Variazioni in E flat Major (Hob.XVII:3) of 1774 is unfolded in a dozen neatly varied mutations. As in the A major set, there is no harmonic adventure. It is as if Haydn had set out to demonstrate his powers of invention, to show that he could keep an idea fresh and make modulatory diversions in simple proof of its success. Particularly arresting are the trilled-ornamented of V, the busy scampering in VI, the ambiguously lyric-dramatic VII, the triple figuration in VIII, and the gate-and-mannerly *coda* that ends in blithe splendor.

The Sonata No.60 in C Major (Hob.XVI:50) is thorough, by some musicologists, to be the last sonata Haydn wrote for piano. It is the third of the three so-called London Sonatas, the final three Sonatas. In any event, all three were written during the period 1794/5, and stand as masterful souvenirs of Haydn's sojourn in London. They were evidently intended for performance by the pianist Therese Jansen, at whose home Haydn lodged in 1795. Artaria would sell these. The Sonata No.60 in C Major begins with a powerful Allegro. Expansive in concept, it ranges from dramatic sections on pages of the Haydn. The Haydn, subtle though he is, will simply we will not work here – the performer must complement the inherent energy of the score, bring out its assurance and vitality. The middle Adagio, cast in rather free, fantasia form, it glows with themes of the most uplifting nature. Apparently, this movement was composed in Vienna a few years earlier and published as a separate *Adagio* in F. With the final movement (*Allegro molto*) we have a return to Haydn at the tempo of the opening movement, with the added Haydn spellbound humor. Particularly effective are the amusing false starts and the unexpected modulations that Haydn frequently favored in his mature symphonic works as well.

For and away the best known of Haydn's solo keyboard works is the *Andante varié in F minor* (Hob.XVII:6) of 1793. Haydn misleadingly called it a "*picciola divertissementta scritto e composto per la celeberrima Signora Pleyel*." This was the Barbara Pleyel to whom Mozart dedicated his complete K.449 and K.453; the *Andante varié* is not a less "trifle." Ernest Hutchenson deems it "a model of classic style." Foreshadowing the slow movement of Beethoven's *Pathétique* there is a double theme, alternately in mood and minor, with two ornithic variations, the original melody is embellished by the stunning coda. Haydn's biographer, Karl Geiringer, is not reluctant to classify this as "an imposing work," and he perceptively detects in its aspects of color and harmony the "first beginnings" of romantic style.

### NADIA REISENBERG BIOGRAPHY

Nadia Reisenberg was born in Vilnius, Lithuania, on July 14, 1904. On the occasion of the 60th anniversary of her American debut, December 2, 1982, I didn't hesitate pleading till the age of six, for the excellent reason that we didn't have a piano. But then one arrived as a present from a favorite uncle, and I soon became the darling of the family, giving impromptu concerts daily. My talent was obvious, so to Petersburg just so I could study at the Conservatory there, and when I was twelve I began studying with Leonid Nikolayev. Almost everything I know about the physical side of piano-playing, I owe to Nikolayev, a remarkably detailed pedagogue. He gave me what has served me in all the years since."

The Reisenberg family left Russia during the Revolution, traveling to Warsaw (where Nadia made her orchestral debut at the same concert where a young conductor, Artur Rodzinski, was also making his formal debut). London, Berlin, and, in 1922, moving permanently to New York. She had her New York recital debut at Aeolian Hall in 1924. A year and later, also studied with Josef Hofmann at the Curtis Institute of Music. "From Hofmann I got a third in beautiful, sensitive pedaling, something that far too many pianists neglect these days," she remembered.

She gave her first American recital at the Aeolian Hall in New York in 1924. Before an audience that included such celebrities as Jascha Heifetz, Mischa Elman, Efrem Zimbalist, Fritz Kreisler and Leopold Godowsky. Her program included works by Bach, Glazunov, Scriabin, Liszt, Debussy, Medtner, Rameau-Godowsky and Ravel. The New York Herald Tribune declared her to be "a pianist of evidently rare musical talent," while the New York Times reviewer found her to be a "player of brilliance and finish far beyond her years." This auspicious debut began a major career that included solo tours, chamber concerts and appearances with many of this country's most orchestras, and appearances with most of the important orchestras in the principal capitals. In the spring of 1937 she made an important tour, giving recitals of being anointed a soloist with Orchestra in Vienna, Warsaw, Prague, Berlin, Helsinki, Stockholm, The Hague, Amsterdam and London. "She is an extraordinary pianist," wrote the critic of the Vienna *Neue Freie Presse*, "with a clear sense of style, brilliant technique, touch capable of a wealth of nuances and a beautiful and warm singing tone."

Upon her arrival in New York as an orphan of November, 1938, to March, 1939, she appeared on live studio broadcasts over WOR in an historic series of all of the Mozart piano concerti conducted by Alfred Wallenstein. The broadcasts not only showcased the twenty-seven works, including the Concertos for 2 Pianos (K.365) and 3 Pianos (K.365) (K.242) but also featured the two concert rondos, as well as the Johann Christian Bach sonata arranged by Mozart (K.107), for three pianos. "I can remember every day of my entire career, my private year with Mozart! It took more than eight months to complete the program, doing one concert per week, and since I had performed only four or five of them before, I had to learn an awful lot, and also prepare a daily lesson. But I didn't feel it had six concerts on my piano at a time: I would be finishing two, working on another two, and reading through a third pair. It was an invaluable experience, and one I will cherish for the rest of my life." It was also a historic achievement, for although several attempts had been made before to play all the Mozart concertos in sequence, no one had done it for nearly thirty years, until Ida Knef marvelous concert cycle at Town Hall.

During the next decade she continued to perform a wide variety of concertos (Mozart's Liszt, Liszt No.2, Kabalevsky No.2, D'Indy, Prokofiev No.5, Rimsy-Korsakov, and others), and began to devote herself more fully to the two greatest musical joys in her life – chamber music and teaching. She appeared frequently with cellists Joseph Schuster and Leonard Rose, violinists Mischa Kroll, Jozsef Szigeti, Tosha Seidel, Alexander Schneider and others, the Juilliard Quartet, and with Benny Goodman recorded the Brahms Sonata in E flat Major. She remembered with particular pleasure: "There is something wonderful about chamber music. When you experience the elation I find in chamber music, this is music-making in the purest sense, where the performer becomes humble, and far less important than the work being performed. The sense of being part of something bigger, of being part of all music, and part of life, opens into a better musician. That's why I see to it that all my students play as much chamber music as possible."

She taught at the Curtis Institute of Music, was Visiting Professor of Music at the University of Southern California, and guest lecturer at New York University. She also conducted master classes from coast to coast, for such organizations as the National Music Camp at Interlochen, and for the New Jersey Music Teachers Association, and was on the faculties of the Mannes and Juilliard Schools, as well as on the teaching staff of the Salmen Chautauqua in New York. "I value the value of teaching. It contributes enormously to one's development as a musician to be able to analyze the problems of your students, even if they have never been your own problems. It's fascinating to investigate the roots of playing difficulties, the reasons behind them, and the various personalities. I find teaching exciting, gratifying and rewarding, and it's always been that way."

She maintained her very active career to the very end, always committed to music. Her health began to fail in 1980, she died on Friday, June 10, 1983.

These Westminster Haydn recordings, re-issued for the first time in their entirety, were recorded by Nadia Reisenberg during three sessions: December 18, 1956, and March 24, 1958. When they were issued, the response from critics and listeners was unanimously ecstatic. The *Detroit News* said it should be issued as a model of how to record piano. The *American Record Guide* said that Nadia Reisenberg... She has an unflattering understanding of classical form and spirit." *High Fidelity* also praised her pianism: "No other Haydn record of the solo piano strikes the memory as comparably satisfying... In an undistinguished repertory she plays with a distinction that borders on the eloquent effigies derived into a classic geometry by Miss Reisenberg." Pianism of this stature and refinement is rare today, and we at Ivory Classics hope that a new generation of listeners will enjoy, once again, the artistry of this very special pianist.
