The prolific pianist and scholar Antony Gray has always been fascinated by the nineteenth-century tradition of transcribing Bach for piano—the era when, before the harpsichord revival, players turned to the great organ and violin works for recital display in their own concert halls. Here over three discs Gray collects some of the best and best known of these transcriptions, many of which are only infrequently heard today.

This is the first comprehensive collection of such transcriptions on disc, an edition which will be welcomed by all lovers of Bach and piano.

## Introduction

The transcription of Bach's music for the piano, above all of the organ and violin works, was one of the most important traditions of nineteenth-century pianism, and one that continued into the twentieth century through the work of pianists such as Ferruccio Busoni, who did so much to keep it alive. It is only with the harpsichord revival, the growth of the "authentic performance" movement and the development of today's taste that it has fallen out of favour. The modern view tends to assume that any transcription is inevitably an unfaithful travesty of Bach's original intentions, and that anything that diverges from the scoring of the composer's autograph is to be condemned.

But of course things are not that simple. The nineteenth-century transcribers who took on the work of transcription were not being "unfaithful" to Bach. They were simply taking part in a tradition that Bach himself had always been part of—a tradition of reworking and adapting music for new circumstances which goes back to the beginning of music, and which Bach himself did not hesitate to practise. Bach himself was a tireless transcriber and adapter of his own music and that of others; to take but one example, he transcribed several of his own organ concertos for one, two or even three harpsichords. What he did himself, his later followers were perfectly justified in continuing to do.

Moreover, the great nineteenth-century transcriptions are not just simple reworkings of Bach's original. They are also in many cases creative reimaginings of Bach's music that allow the piano to speak in a way that it could not do if it were simply trying to reproduce the sounds of another instrument. That is particularly true of the organ works, which respond to the piano's different sonorities in a quite extraordinary way, giving the listener new insights into the music that are completely different from those that an organ performance gives. Of course the piano can do things that the organ cannot; it can bring a different touch, a different color, a different sense of line to the music, and those differences are not a distortion—they are simply another way of hearing the music, and one that can perfectly valid.

## 第三页 (scans/HIPPO_20220510_0003.jpg)

This album does not set out to be a complete survey of all the great Bach transcriptions for piano that have ever been made—such an undertaking would require many more than three CDs—but it does set out to give a representative selection of the finest work that was done in this tradition. We have some of the greatest names in pianism represented here: Ferruccio Busoni, Ernst von Dohnányi, Ignaz Friedman, Vladimir Horowitz, Wilhelm Kempff, Alfred Cortot—all of them giants of the keyboard who have all left their mark on this repertory. These are all performers and transcribers who understood that transcription was an art in itself, not just a simple transfer from one instrument to another; and they respond to Bach's music with an imagination and a musical understanding that give these transcriptions a life of their own.

Antony Gray is one of the most thoughtful and imaginative pianists before the public today, and he has made this project very much his own. He has studied all these transcriptions deeply, and he plays them with a conviction, a clarity and a poetic feeling that show the music in the very best possible light. He is a pianist who combines the most scrupulous musical scholarship with a natural musical instinct, and the combination is one that serves this music wonderfully well. He has also contributed to this album a number of his own transcriptions, works that are taken from the violin and cello sonatas, which fit perfectly into the tradition represented here.

The result is a fascinating and unique anthology. It allows modern listeners to rediscover a tradition of performance that has too easily been forgotten, but that in the right hands can still speak with extraordinary power and immediacy. For anyone who loves Bach, there is much here to enjoy, much to admire, and much to ponder. These are performance that bring the great music to life in a new way, and they will give many hours of listening pleasure to anyone who gives them the chance.

— **Graham Johnson**

## 第七页 (scans/HIPPO_20220510_0007.jpg)

### **The Transcribers**

- **Ferruccio Benvenuto Busoni** (1866-1924): Italian-German pianist, composer and teacher, one of the greatest pianists of all time and one of the most famous exponents of Bach transcription. He created many of the most famous Bach transcriptions, including the Chaconne, the Chromatic Fantasie, the Toccata BWV 565, In dulci jubilo, Ich ruf zu dir, Herr Jesu Christ and many others, all included here.

- **Alfred Cortot** (1877-1962): French pianist and conductor, one of the most influential piano teachers of the twentieth century. He was particularly noted for his interpretations of French music and romantic repertoire, but he also made a number of distinguished Bach transcriptions, the one included here is his version of Ah, Gott, wie manches Herzeleid BWV 658.

- **Ernö (Ernst von) Dohnányi** (1877-1960): Hungarian pianist, composer and teacher. He was one of the leading Hungarian musicians of the first half of the twentieth century, and he was a distinguished Bach exponent. His transcription of the Siciliano from the Flute Sonata BWV 1031 is one of the best known of all Bach piano transcriptions.

- **Ignaz Friedman** (1882-1948): Polish-born pianist, one of the great Romantic pianists of the early twentieth century. He made a number of very fine Bach transcriptions, four of which are included on this recording: Wachet auf, Vom Himmel hoch, Meine Seele, and the Bourrée from the Orchestral Suite No.3.

- **Arthur Friedheim** (1859-1932): Russian pianist and teacher, a pupil of Liszt. He transcribed the Andante from the Organ Sonata BWV 529.

- **Vladimir Horowitz** (1903-1989): Ukrainian-born American pianist, one of the most celebrated pianists of the twentieth century. He was famed for his stunning virtuosity and his colorful tonal palette, and he transcribed the Bach chorale My Heart Ever Faithful included here.

## 第八页 (scans/HIPPO_20220510_0008.jpg)

- **Karol Karol Koczalski** (1885-1953): Polish pianist and composer, child prodigy who became a prominent European pianist. He contributed the Fantasie in C BWV (from BWV 570) to this collection.

- **Wilhelm Kempff** (1895-1991): German pianist, particularly noted for his Beethoven and Schubert interpretations, but also a distinguished Bach player. His transcription of O Mensch, bewein dein' Sünde groß BWV 622 is included here.

- **Franz Liszt** (1811-1886): the great Hungarian composer and pianist, the most influential pianist of the nineteenth century. He was one of the pioneers of Bach transcription for piano, and his transcription of the Great Prelude and Fugue in E minor BWV 548 is included here.

- **Carl Tausig** (1841-1871): Polish piano virtuoso, pupil of Liszt, one of the most celebrated pianists of the mid-nineteenth century. His Scherzo after BWV 566 is included here.

- **Alexander Siloti** (1863-1945): Ukrainian pianist, conductor and composer, pupil of Liszt. He transcribed the Gavotte en Rondeau from BWV 1006 included here.

- **Bálint Vázsonyi** (1966- ): contemporary American pianist and scholar. He contributes the Passacaglia and Fugue BWV 582 to this collection.

- **Albert Friedman** [sic, correction: it is **Friedheimer**] - transcriber of Schmücke dich, o liebe Seele BWV 654.

- **Pedro G. Bassino** - transcribed the Prelude and Fugue BWV 565 for this collection.

- **August Wilhelmj** (1845-1908): German violinist, known for his famous transcription of the Air on the G string from BWV 1068, which was later arranged for piano.

- **Richard Williams** - transcribed the Prelude from BWV 1006.

**Antony Gray** (1959- ): Australian pianist and scholar. In addition to performing all these works, he has contributed seven of the transcriptions included in this set: the Loure from BWV 1006, the Sarabande and Gigue from Cello Suite No.4 BWV 1010, the Symphonie from the Prelude BWV 542, the Andante from Violin Sonata BWV 1015, the Adagio from Violin Sonata BWV 1016, the Canonica from Goldberg Variations, and the Variations on a Bach Prelude by Mendelssohn.

### **Antony Gray**

Antony Gray was born in 1959 in Hobart, Tasmania. He studied at the University of Tasmania and the Sydney Conservatorium of Music, and was a prizewinner in the ABC Symphony Australia Young Performer of the Year Awards in 1981. He studied further in Vienna with Paul Badura-Skoda, one of the most distinguished pianists and scholars of the twentieth century, and with the Polish composer and pianist Lidia Grychta-King. He has performed widely throughout Australia, in Sydney, Melbourne, Hobart, Adelaide, Brisbane, Perth and Canberra, and has also appeared in Vienna, Paris, London, Scotland and the United States. He has a particular interest in the revival of neglected repertory, and has performed and recorded many rare and forgotten works, including a complete survey of the keyboard sonatas of Muzio Clementi. He lives currently in Sydney.

He has recorded previously for ABC Classics:
- *Fantasy*: neglected piano works 1996
- *Rare Composers, Rare Piano Works* 1998
- 2001: **The complete piano sonatas of Muzio Clementi** (ABC Classics 4CD 465 651-2)

This is his first three-disc project for ABC Classics.
