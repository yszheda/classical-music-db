## 内页 - 说明文章 (by Antony Gray)

**[Page 12]**

So is there a future for the transcription? Modern transcriptions are still being made, of things like Tchaikovsky's symphonies and ballets, perhaps only to be listened to for the display of mind-boggling virtuosity rather than for any great insights they give us into the original works. But perhaps the days of the composer simply arranging the notes from one medium to another are gone. There is perhaps no perceived need for this any more, and if a modern composer does transcribe the work of another, it will be very much his add his or her own personal voice to the original; in many cases making the transcription a new work in its own right. A transcription to me is much like a completely new work, using another as a starting point, no matter how much of the original is left in this process. The British composer Andrew Toovey describes his work together sections from two very different pieces, played all at the same time, and all in the space of little over a minute. He adds no original material, and although the piece has a superficially Bach-like sound, there is no question of it being anything other than a completely original creation. Frank Millward takes the harmonic scheme of a Bach original, and using motifs from that work creates a completely original improvisation, almost completely bereft of any identifying Bach-like features. François Sarhan 'composes' the famous first Prelude and Fugue, Sally Mays takes a chorale prelude and reworks Bach's additions.

Perhaps the particular mystery of Bach's creative genius has always had a fascination for composers – the extraordinary diversity of methods of invention and elaboration that he displays in his music must be, and why! Why did Mozart, who himself was inspired to arrange some Bach fugues for strings in 1782, say 'Now there is something one can learn from!'. The famous Brandenburg Concerto sound almost as good played by an orchestra of kazoo as in its original scoring, although it will probably sound better on the piano.

– Antony Gray

With the possible exception of Franz Liszt, there has probably been no more prolific transcriber than the great J.S. Bach himself, or it hardly seems inappropriate that there have been so many transcriptions of Bach's music made by others over the two and a half centuries since his death. The main difference between Bach and Liszt, apart from the fact that while Liszt transcribed equally as much music by other composers as by himself, Bach, apart from a collection of many Italian concertos transcribed for keyboard, mainly marked his own Bach catalogue, with some works appearing in several different guises over the course of his lifetime. For example, the E major solo Partita BWV1006a exists also in a version for keyboard or lute, as well as the first movement turning up as the richly orchestrated Sinfonia to Cantata BWV29. All three movements of the A minor violin sonata BWV964 are again richly orchestrated versions of keyboard or chamber originals. Equally, the D major trio sonata for two flutes and continuo BWV1048 was rearranged quite directly for viola da gamba and harpsichord. There is much speculation as to the possible origin of many of his works, in works by Bach now lost. The E major harpsichord concerto BWV1053 probably began life as an oboe concerto, and was subsequently used in its entirety in a cantata, the harpsichord part being transcribed to the organ, and various vocal parts being effortlessly grafted on.

Does this give everyone else the right to do as they will with another composer's music? And if so, to what extent? Strangely, Liszt's piano transcriptions of Berlioz' organ works do me much the same as Bach – he incorporates the pedal part into the two hands of the pianist, despite his normal tendency to almost totally immerse the original in an orchestra of virtuoso figuration and cascades of notes. When Liszt made his transcriptions, he was concerned, this was Liszt emulating Roger's bowed approach of simply making this music accessible to a wider public, in the days before recorded sound. In any case, in the 19th century it was standard practice to issue any orchestral, choral or chamber work simultaneously in a version for piano duet – sometimes, in the case of conscientious composers such as Brahms, Schumann and Mendelssohn, by the composers themselves. In many cases, these publications can almost be viewed as self-contained works in their own right: Brahms's four-hand versions of his chamber music, for example, often acquire a symphonic grandeur of their own due to the increased range available on the piano.

At the same time, a music publisher's hack, employed for the sole purpose of making four-hand versions of published works, would burn out unprofessional but unimaginative arrangements, with the only licence being the odd simplification for the benefit of the amateur player. Bach was often object of this particular market. It is possible that the validity of the transcription has only become a question in the last fifty years with the ubiquitous availability of recorded music. Brahms made his four-hand arrangements himself because he loved the music to be able to be played and enjoyed in the home, when there was no other way. With authorities on ever-increasing concern in performance practice, there has inevitably been a negative reaction to 'inauthentic' transcriptions, as if the original work somehow suffers as a result. It is only known, and for some time towards the end of the 20th century, transcriptions were looked on at considerable disfavour. We have perhaps entered a more objective and tolerant age, where individual transcriptions could either be seen as valid interpretations in their own right, or, depending on what they give pleasure or not, and the answers to these criteria will be as numerous as the people who listen to them. Transcriptions raise fascinating aesthetic and musicological questions – and yet it is doubtful the composers of the original works would simply not understand what the fuss has been about.

There are therefore two basic types of transcription: one which simply sets out to transfer a work from one medium to another with more or less alteration, and a second, where the arranger adds their own thoughts or style to the original. Many of the transcriptions of Liszt, Busoni or Rachmaninoff would come into the latter category, while perhaps the majority would belong to the former. Perhaps even playing a work written for harpsichord on the modern piano could be counted under the first category.

Many transcriptions of organ works do no more than incorporate the pedal part into the piano texture, so the change or instrument is, in a way, no different to that from harpsichord to piano.

So why do pianists and transcriptions are nearly always by pianists, pure Stokowski and Elgar choose to play transcriptions when there is so much marvellous music already, in its original state? The obvious reason is that it makes a work accessible to the pianist that would otherwise not be, in its original guise. The audience for piano recitals is far greater than those who would go to hear an organ performance, so why listen to a transcription? Are transcriptions more to do with the interests of the pianist than the benefit of the audience? An interesting sidebar thrown up by transcriptions is what they tell us about how the transcribers viewed the music they were transcribing. The modern pianist, perhaps with some idea of the latest notions of 'authentic' performance practice would no doubt be surprised by the liberties a Liszt took at acceptable modern speeds. Either the pianists who made these transcriptions were formidable, good, or Bach's music was played much more slowly in the 19th century – a fact generally known, but just how much slower is perhaps open to question. In his own day, he was famed for his breakneck speeds and leaving grave notes in (Giuseppe Martucci's version of the first three orchestral suite is as fast as his own 1920s recordings of his own music), but the fugues are considerably 'quasi notturno', and the intricacies of the contrapuntal lines and crossing of hands forbid anything but a moderate tempo. Today both the organ chorale prelude and the cantata aria it is based on are taken at a much quicker pace. The piano texture in Saint-Saëns' version of a Bach Fugue may demonstrate an undoubtedly seen as excessive today, to negotiate some of the leaping broken chords.

What is one to do, then? Play at the speed that the arranger envisaged, if one can realise it accurately? Play out the score to the letter? To do otherwise surely devalues the transcriber's original work. Or is it our sacred right to play one person's version of another's work, as we able to improve it as we go?

There would be very few of us today, confronting such transcriptions as Rachmaninoff, who would feel obliged to play every note, and it's unlikely that it would ever have been expected. It plays any.
