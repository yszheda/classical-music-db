## A LEGEND MADE MANIFEST
### The Vocal Art of Birgit Nilsson
#### Alan Blyth

Without question Birgit Nilsson was the leading Wagnerian soprano of her age. For the 20 years from 1956 to 1976, she triumphed in all the world's major opera houses as Elisabeth, Senta, Isolde and Brünnhilde, singing these, and other parts such as Agathe, Aida, Salome, Elektra and Tosca, with an unflagging energy and determination of purpose that sustained her ringing voice through the most stringent demands placed on it.

How did she reach that pre-eminence? Well, not at first through anything like such a demanding repertoire as she was later to attempt. When she began her career at the Royal Opera in Stockholm during the 1946-47 season it was admittedly as Agathe, always considered as stepping-stone to Wagnerian parts, but her first major success was as Verdi's Lady Macbeth, and in her early years she sang as much Italian repertoire as anything else, her roles there between 1947 and 1951 including Donna Anna, Aida and Tosca. She also appeared as Lisa in Tchaikovsky's Queen of Spades, and when she first appeared in Britain, at Glyndebourne in 1951, it was as that other Elektra in Mozart's Idomeneo, although it is true that she had already in those years dipped into the Wagnerian waters with Senta, Venus and Sieglinde.

Nilsson's formative years were spent in her homeland. She was born in the small town of Västra Karups in southern Sweden, where her father owned a lovely farm and where Nilsson often returned, after she had established her career, for rest and repose. She once commented that she spoke and sang before she could walk - "but then I began to walk very late!" was a typical piece of wry Nilssonian humour. She was given a toy piano by a worker on the farm for her fourth birthday, which became more than a curiosity as she tried to fathom the mysteries of music. "I learnt everything on it in C major," she once told me, "and I played by ear. Guests to the house said that I sang well and I appeared to have perfect pitch, so I suppose I was born with a certain musicality inside of me. Next I learnt to read music and to play the piano - badly, so I wasn't very fond of the instrument."

Her father was not keen for her to take up music as he had ideas about her taking over the family farm, but she sang frequently as a young girl, in the normal manner of many children, in school choirs. Then, at 15, she was invited into the church choir. It was perhaps the first intimation of her future when a teacher spotted her talent but thought that Nilsson's career might be confined to the concert platform. She recalled, at about this time, singing her teacher a baritone serenade!

After finishing school at Gothenborg, where she took part in some concerts, she began to receive some encouragement from her mother, who had come to realise her daughter's potential. She auditioned with 47 others for the Royal Music Academy in Stockholm and was the first to be accepted. After beginning her career in concerts all over Sweden she eventually joined the Royal Opera's School, but the authorities there, including the revered conductor Leo Blech, did not warm to her, even suggested that she was not particularly musical.

Then came that golden chance to step in at three days' notice as Agathe. It proved something of a nightmare: Blech could not understand why she was not note perfect, giving her appalling nerves so that she felt like jumping into the river on the way home. A year passed before her next chance came along. The soprano taking Lady Macbeth at the Royal Opera fell ill. Hans Busch, son of Fritz, who was to conduct Verdi's opera, remembered hearing a bright soprano singing the Sleepwalking Scene at the School. It was Nilsson. An SOS went out and she hurried back from the farm, spending a whole night learning the complete role. "It was a great success and I suppose the turning-point of my life," she told me. "Blech capitulated and even took me to Berlin for a concert performance of Act I of Die Walküre. It should have led to other things, but I felt I wasn't ready for an international career. Eventually I sang my first Brünnhilde [Siegfried] only in 1949 at Stockholm, and gradually and quietly I built up my repertoire."

The early 1950s were the turning-point. Nilsson first appeared at Vienna in the 1953-54 season (Elsa and Sieglinde) and Elsa at the Bayreuth Festival in 1954. During the following season she added Salome, Isolde and the Götterdämmerung Brünnhilde to her repertoire. She also created the title role in Liebermann's Penelope at Stockholm, but by and large she avoided modern scores because of the lack of a canon in the writing. She soon added Turandot and eventually Elektra and the Dyer's Wife in Die Frau ohne Schatten to her list of parts, but she always liked to leaven the heaviest of her roles with Agathe, Leonore (Fidelio), Amalia (Ballol) and Tosca, but inevitably with the increasing paucity of her kind of gleaming voice in the Wagner parts, she had to concentrate to a large extent on those and on Elektra.

However, she never tired of them because on each occasion she could find a new dimension in them. "They are such complex characters that there is always some new facet to discover." I can vouch that this was the case. In 1969 I heard her at the Bayreuth Festival, when she was already over 50. Her Isolde remained quite overwhelming in vocal command and in concept as seen and heard in Wieland Wagner's famous staging, yet four years later at Covent Garden under Solti, with Peter Hall directing, she pierced even further into the heart of the part. It was the same with her Elektra, a part she long avoided because she thought it too taxing. "When I finally sang it at Stockholm, I found how wrong I had been."

She always preferred the spontaneity of recording live performances. She averred: "You can't imitate the tension, the excitement, the 'heart' of an actual performance. Besides, in the studio you may have thought that you have done a passage wonderfully well only to find it has to be repeated because of some mistake in the orchestra. So you have to do it again and again, which can, of course, be very tiring on a singer."

Nilsson caught live at Bayreuth was and is an incomparable experience, as three of the extracts recorded live at Bayreuth on CD 2 confirm. Unless it be the readings of Flagstad with Furtwängler, it would be hard to find a performance of Brünnhilde's immolation more electrifying and involving than that under Böhm made in the sixties at the Festival. In any case there is room for both Flagstad and Nilsson in any Wagnerian soprano Valhalla. Whereas Flagstad envelops the listener with her warm, rich tone, Nilsson electrifies one with the biting strength of her singing, the shattering involvement of her reading as Brünnhilde takes leave of the world and all its power-driven madness. With Böhm so urgent in the pit the results are thrilling.

And Böhm and Nilsson make Isolde's Narration, Curse and Liebestod just as essential for any collection. As the infuriated, frustrated Irish maid of Act I and the transfigured lover of Act III Nilsson makes an incomparable impression, and one is amazed anew at the vocal ease of her delivery allied to her comprehension of the text's inner meaning.

The first item on CD 2, recorded in 1975, is a reminder of the diva's earlier assumption of the role of Sieglinde. Here she is partnered by her fellow countryman, the Heldentenor Helge Brilioth. Together they give a vocal sheen to this conclusion of Act I of Die Walküre seldom heard. Between the operatic excerpts, we catch Nilsson as a concert performer in the Wesendonck Lieder, where she successfully fines down her tone to the more intimate requirements of these songs, two of them studies for the Tristan that follows.

The final item on CD 1, taken from the same LP that opens this portrait, finds Nilsson in yet another Wagnerian part, that of the temptress Kundry, to which she predictably brings a subtlety of expression to match the vocal security with Brilioth as a notable Parsifal. In a review in Gramophone, the eminent critic John Warrack commented that Nilsson's Kundry is "lulling, sensuous, the menace more sinister because it is not revealed in harshness of tone or phrasing," and after Parsifal's great outburst rejecting her wiles, "Kundry's reversal of roles from temptress to supplicant is signalled by a lightening of tone ... It is an immensely subtle performance, a proper reconciliation of great vocal gifts with an intelligent understanding of the most difficult moment of the most difficult of all operas."

So this is another performance that acts as a benchmark for aspiring Wagnerian sopranos. The passing years cannot quench the sheer magnificence of Nilsson's performance, described once by the American musicologist Irving Kolodin as "the kind of singing from which legends are born." Well, the legend is here made manifest.
