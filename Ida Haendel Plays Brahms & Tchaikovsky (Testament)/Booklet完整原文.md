## Liner Notes by Tully Potter

### IDA HAENDEL

Among the women violinists of the 20th century, Ida Haendel holds an honoured place, having been consistently successful for six decades. This artistic longevity has been her reward for acquiring an impressive technique very early in her life, which began in Chelm, Poland, on 15 December – the year varies from 1923 to 1928, depending on the source. As so often happens with prodigies, there was an ambitious parent in the background: her mother Fanny loved music but her father Natan was the driving force. He had wanted to become a violinist but had been frustrated by his own parents who, insisting on his studying for the rabbinate, had even destroyed his violin. It was natural, then, that Ida's elder sister Sara or 'Ala' (later known as Alice) should be encouraged to play a small fiddle as well as the piano. But the real talent turned out to reside in Ida. When she was three and a half, tired of playing with her dolls, she picked up her sister's violin and soon managed to play a song her mother had been singing. She continued to play by ear, copying music she heard on the family gramophone and her father consulted notable violinists – including Bronislaw Huberman, Stefan Frenkel, Szymon Goldberg and Roman Totenberg – as to who should be her future teacher. All of them recommended Carl Flesch. When Ida was six the family moved to Warsaw, where she studied with Mihalowicz at the Chopin Conservatory, making her first public appearance when she was seven, winning a gold medal at nine and carrying off the first Huberman Prize at ten. This success brought a grant from a Jewish charity to study in Paris with Szigeti (who was not able to teach her after all, though he was still to be a major influence). So Flesch became her principal teacher, although she also had lessons from Enescu. She won the special prize for a Polish contestant at the 1935 Wieniawski Competition, in which Ginette Neveu and David Oistrakh took first and second places.

Ida came to England midway through 1936 and was to have made her London debut early that December, at one of Sir Thomas Beecham's Sunday concerts; but London County Council regulations forbade this, as she was too young. So the debut had to wait until 18 December, when her recital at Queen's Hall caused quite a flutter among the critics. 'This remarkable child possesses not only a technique that does not falter but also all the essentials of an impeccable style,' wrote Ferruccio Bonavia of the *Daily Telegraph*, who was also on hand on 31 January to hear her play the Brahms Concerto at the same hall with Sir Henry Wood: 'Her skill is remarkable even in this age when violinists seem to be born with a command of the fingerboard their elders achieved only after long and industrious study. [...] This young girl from Poland is something outstanding even in an era of prodigies.' The following month she gave the Beethoven Concerto with Wood in the same venue and, although at least one critic sternly pronounced that 'this is not a work for children', her reputation in Britain was established. Losing no time, Wood introduced her to his Promenade audience that same year with a repeat of her Brahms performance. He later wrote: 'Her tone and feeling in the concerto were so beautiful that I seemed to hear dear old Ysaÿe at my side once again.' It would seem that the Brahms – not a concerto normally associated with women – was very much Haendel's calling card at this time, as she chose it for her first British broadcast in March 1938; inevitably Sir Henry conducted the BBC Symphony Orchestra.

Haendel became a firm favourite with Londoners, especially at the Proms – where she has now given more than 50 performances. She finally settled here in 1939, having escaped from Poland just ahead of the invading Germans and in 1940 was naturalised. During the war she gave recitals for troops and factory workers, also appearing in the National Gallery Concerts organised by Myra Hess. She played the Dvořák Concerto at the composer's centenary Prom in 1941 and the next year gave her first performance of the Elgar Concerto, at another Prom. On 4 March 1944 she was fittingly chosen to appear at a memorial concert for her mentor Sir Henry Wood, in which all three London orchestras participated. In November 1946, just before leaving on her first tour of the United States, she performed the relatively new Khachaturian Concerto and Beethoven in one programme at the Royal Albert Hall, with Enrique Jorda conducting.

The following year saw Haendel make her debut with the Palestine Orchestra, soon to be the Israel Philharmonic. She has returned to it almost every season and in 1982 was invited to appear in the Huberman Centenary Festival in Tel Aviv. The post-war era brought her another conductor mentor in the remarkable Romanian musician Sergiu Celibidache – she wrote about their relationship in her outspoken memoirs, *Woman with Violin* (Victor Gollancz, 1970), of which a second volume is almost complete. Celibidache's devotion to music could hardly fail to strike a chord with such a dedicated professional as Haendel, and he was a potent influence on her career. In 1952 she went to live in Montreal with her parents, keeping a London base and British citizenship and returning regularly for concerts. 'I love England,' she says. 'It is my country and I would never give it up.' She became an ambassadress for British music, taking the Elgar, Britten and Walton Concertos all over the world – she even played the Elgar in Vienna, the first to do so since Adolf Busch almost three decades earlier. 'I think it's true to say that I revived the Britten Concerto,' she says. 'I'm still playing it everywhere and I shall be doing it at the 1994 Proms.' She has visited Hong Kong and China with the London Philharmonic; Germany and Australia with the BBC Symphony Orchestra and Mexico with the English Chamber Orchestra. This evangelistic work was recognised in the 1991 New Year's honours list with the CBE, an award of which she is justly proud.

Apart from the usual warhorses of the violin – two of which are on this compact disc – Haendel's repertoire includes such relative rarities as the Bloch and Reger Concertos and she premiered Dallapiccola's *Tartiniana Seconda*, for violin and orchestra in a 1957 broadcast from Turin. Her many performances of the Sibelius Concerto – which she recorded – led to her being given the Sibelius Medal in 1982. But perhaps her most amazing contribution has been to champion the Concerto by the Swedish composer Allan Pettersson, who spent many of his 68 years racked with pain and illness. 'He's probably the most controversial composer of this century, but also the most interesting I've come across,' she says. Among her large repertoire of duos, Haendel has a special regard for the Third Sonata by Enescu, one of her old teacher's few works in his Romanian nationalist style. If she has a regret about her career, it is that she has performed so little chamber music. 'Even the Brahms Double Concerto I have not managed to play,' she says. 'I really wanted to do the Beethoven Triple but it never happened.'

Having unusually small hands, Ida Haendel had a long search for the ideal instrument. Her first good violin was a 1726 Stradivarius, although she occasionally borrowed a Guarnerius; at last, in the early 1950s, she found the 1696 Strad she still plays. Her first recordings were made before the war and even then, one could hear her essential attributes in embryo: the well-focused, astringent tone, supported by a technique which was secure right across the violin's range; the dependable rhythm and the penchant for broad tempi and expansive phrases. She has never been a showy violinist on the concert platform – indeed, she rejects the antics of some of her juniors and says forthrightly: 'You don't have to make certain bad expressions to get emotional about music.' The two performances in this programme, which display her in her early maturity, bear witness to that statement.

### SERGIU CELIBIDACHE

For more than 40 years, Sergiu Celibidache has refused to make recordings, saying that listening to a record is like going to bed with a picture of Brigitte Bardot. Because every performance is prepared for a particular hall, with a unique acoustic, he maintains that it cannot be transplanted, or heard under different circumstances: 'The sound and tempo may be right in the space in which I am playing, but not on the record.' For him even a broadcast is not a true reflection of the performance being given in the hall or studio; so it is a rare privilege to hear him in a recorded performance, with a soloist very much on his wavelength.

Roman, Rumania, and began to learn music in Iasi, where his father was the prefect. As a young man, he paid for further studies in Paris by playing the piano in nightclubs, cafes and a dancing school. By 1936 he was in Berlin, where during the war he studied at the Hochschule für Musik, his teacher being the Reger scholar Fritz Stein, the Hochschule's principal and himself a conducting pupil of Arthur Nikisch. Celibidache also read musicology and philosophy at the University (which saved him from conscription into the German Army) and graduated with a thesis on Josquin Desprez. At 33, after winning a radio competition, he was appointed to the Berlin Philharmonic – replacing Leo Porchat, who had been accidentally shot dead by a sentry. The tall, charismatic, Celibidache made an immediate impact on the European concert scene with his Latin good looks and eloquent gestures. In 1948 he became principal conductor, after Wilhelm Furtwängler made his British debut, with the London Philharmonic. After leaving the Berlin Philharmonic in 1952, he spent several years taking stock, was worked mainly with radio orchestras – especially in Stuttgart, Stockholm and Paris. He was associated with the London Symphony Orchestra and the Bamberg Symphony Orchestra, appeared as a guest conductor in South America, Israel, Japan and many European centres and taught at Mainz University and the Curtis Institute of Philadelphia. Since 1979 he has been in charge of the Munich Philharmonic and he is now the city's Music Director. In the 1991/92 season he appeared with the Berlin Philharmonic for the first time since his resignation. He is a keen mathematician and his compositions include four symphonies, a piano concerto and the orchestral suite *Der Taschengarten*.

Celibidache is both a very physical and a very spiritual person. He was a top-line footballer in his youth and will still put everything aside to attend a match or watch it on television. The other side of his nature owes much to his Zen Buddhist faith and expresses itself in a desire to experience – and communicate – music as more than just a string of sounds: 'We need music but the purpose is freedom. The purpose of any art is to free yourself. Your ultimate purpose is to be free of that inclination also.' At Mainz he taught Phenomenology of Music, meaning music as a total entity. He never asks his conducting students for any money but insists that they should not have the score in front of them when working on a piece with him. He early acquired a reputation for needing more rehearsals that anyone else – a dozen with the Berlin Philharmonic, 21 on a visit to La Scala, at least six in recent years. His painstaking approach has often been misunderstood – in 1961 members of the Rome RAI Orchestra threw their music at him during a rehearsal of Beethoven's Ninth. Video films show him performing and even rehearsing from memory, always urging the players to listen to each other; where other conductors demand 'more', he will ask for 'less, less there, then we can hear what's happening'. He says: 'I do not think of myself as special. I ask people to take the pains to come with me and join together. I do not make the music. I create the circumstances where the orchestra can do it. Where music comes to life.'

### SIR EUGENE GOOSSENS

The third generation of the musical Goossens dynasty was amazingly prolific, producing a composer/conductor, an oboist, a horn player and two harpists. The eldest son was called Eugene, as his father and grandfather had been before him.

Born in London on 26 May 1893, he was sent to Bruges – where his grandfather had been born and his father had been educated – to study at the Conservatoire from 1903 to 1906. He then attended the Liverpool College of Music and finally the Royal College of Music, where his teachers were Rivarde (violin), Dykes (piano), Wood (theory) and Stanford (composition). At first he earned his living as a violinist, playing in theatre bands, the Queen's Hall Orchestra and the Langley-Makle and Philharmonic Quartets; but gradually, with Beecham's encouragement, he switched to conducting. In 1921 he gave six successful London concerts with his own pick-up orchestra and conducted for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes and the Carl Rosa Opera Company. His career consequently noticed him as early as 1914. At first he concentrated on chamber music but eventually he wrote for all the media of his day, his oeuvre including two operas, a ballet and two symphonies and leading Noël Coward to pen the line 'My heart loosens when I listen to Goossens'. From 1923 to 1946 Goossens was based in the United States, as conductor of the Rochester Philharmonic and (from 1931) the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra, although he also appeared regularly in Britain – especially to conduct ballet and opera (the latter involving a famous clash of personalities with Chaliapin in *Faust*). From 1947 to 1956 he held the dual post of resident conductor of the Sydney Symphony Orchestra and director of the New South Wales Conservatorium of Music. He died at Iverlingdon in Middlesex on 13 June 1962. Although the composer Goossens has been virtually – and perhaps unfairly – forgotten, the conductor is being rediscovered: the present performance is only one of several re-issues on compact disc.
