## Programme Notes by Keith Anderson

### Heitor Villa-Lobos (1887-1959)

Heitor Villa-Lobos occupies an unrivalled position in the music of his native Brazil. Born in Rio de Janeiro in 1887, son of a senior official at the Brazilian National Library, he began cello lessons as a child with his father, using a viola. By the time of his father's death in 1898 he had a reasonable knowledge of music theory and could play the clarinet and cello, and went on to take guitar lessons from a neighbour, hiding the fact from his mother, who wished him to be a doctor. It was in part through the influence of his father that Villa-Lobos was able to acquaint himself with the varied folk-music of Brazil, while the city itself provided opportunities to hear and later to join in the music of the chôro, the popular street serenade of Rio de Janeiro. At the age of sixteen he left his home, giving up any idea of medical study and finding a more sympathetic lodging at the house of an aunt, an amateur pianist with a particular love of Bach. He now earned a living as best he could, helping to support his family as a playing musician, playing the cello at the Recreio Theatre and performing in cinemas and cafes.

From the age of eighteen Villa-Lobos spent some seven years of intermittent travel to the remote areas of the country, interrupted by a short return to Rio and the National Institute of Music in Rio. To keep himself first sold the remaining books of his father's library and then took casual employment, wherever possible, experiencing, as he travelled, the indigenous music of the country, although never encountering cannibals, as falsely related subsequently in Paris, to the annoyance of the Brazilian colony there. He had already turned his hand to composition of a relatively understanding kind. Settled once again in Rio in 1912, he began to tackle music of greater complexity, with an opera, Izaht, and the eventual completion of a work on which he had been intermittently engaged for some years, the guitar *Suite populaire brésilienne*. In 1915 he began a series of concerts of his works, arousing the anger of critics and occasionally of audiences, while attracting support from a group of young sympathizers. Although his style of writing was not influenced by European trends of the time, it was nevertheless original and alarming enough. An influential critic in 1916, writing of the *Danças frenéticas* of 1919, remarked that the work had the wrong title and should have been called St Vitus's Dance with an explanatory note advising that it should be performed by epileptic musicians and heard by paranoiacs. 75 years later it is difficult to understand such criticism, although there might be some sympathy for the same critic's jibe at the prolific nature of the talent of Villa-Lobos. At this early stage of his career he was already writing a great deal of music, and was to continue to do so.

The work of Villa-Lobos had already attracted the attention of Darius Milhaud, employed by the French Consul in Rio, Paul Claudel and by Arthur Rubinstein. In 1918, but it was the virtuoso Artur Rubinstein who did as much as anyone to persuade the Brazilian Government to provide a measure of sponsorship for a journey to Paris, allowing Villa-Lobos to introduce his music to the French public and at the same time to profit from the stimulating intellectual climate of the city. Through Rubinstein he was introduced to the publishing house of Max Eschig, the sponsor of his first Paris concert in 1924. As in Rio, his music was acceptable to audiences with more advanced musical tastes and soon found an even wider public by the time of concerts of his works given on a much larger scale in 1927, although there were still occasions when his music proved disturbing to a section of society.

His association with leading musicians and artists of the time, helped by Roger-Ducasse, praised by Paul Le Flem and Florent Schmitt, and commissioned to write a ballet by Diaghilev, continued until 1930, when political upheavals in Brazil made it no longer possible to travel to Africa and to return to South America for concerts, not isolating himself from the cultural life of his country.

In 1930 Villa-Lobos returned to Brazil, intending to fulfil a series of concert engagements before returning to Paris. The October Revolution forced a change of plan and brought unforeseen opportunities. The new government gave him a position as a composer whose music fully represented the spirit of Brazil and the potential nature of his people. He was soon establishing a scheme of music education at every level and this led later to the setting up of a conservatory in Rio in 1942, followed by the establishment of similar institutions in regional capitals. When this work came to an end in 1945, with its political changes, he began to win a regular place for himself in the concert life of the United States and, with the war now over, was able to renew his ties with Europe. He died in Rio de Janeiro in 1959. On death was occasion of much respect and of course, he was in the city.

### The Pequeña suíte (Little Suite) for cello and piano was written in 1913. It consists of a series of six short, admirably constructed, to his use. It is, in effect, a cello, and in a relatively conventional style, influenced, it has been suggested, by his admiration for the cellist and composer David Popper. The suite is testament to his friendship with violinist who died in 1919. The same quality and lyrical command is shown in other pieces of these earlier years, the Capriccio, Opus 49, of 1915 for violin and piano, the romantic Prelúdio of 1913 and the wistful Élégie of 1916. These works do nothing to shock but are in a late romantic idiom with relatively little trace of specifically Brazilian folk and are indebted to the musical idiom of Rudolph, seeing often akin rather to French music of the period.

The nine volumes of *Bachianas brasileiras* explore a very different vein, although there is a hint of what is to come in the *Fugato all'antica* of the *Pequeña suíte*. The admiration of Villa-Lobos for Johann Sebastian Bach remained constant. In these works, the techniques that Villa-Lobos draws double inspiration from both Bach and Brazil. Although technical indebtedness to the former may be relatively superficial, providing a framework for the latter, the spirit of Bach here is evoked, the ultimate source of inspiration. The series, variously scored, began in 1930 with a work for an orchestra of cellos, at least eight players and ended in 1945 with the ninth of the series, for string orchestra. From the second of the *Bachianas brasileiras* comes *O trenzinho do caipira* (The Countryman's Little Train), the third movement of the suite of 1930. It offers, in a version for cello and piano and used by him in this version during concert tours over a period of nine years, an evocation of a steam-train in 1930 Brazil, the rhythmic repetition of the steam-train of the interior of Brazil and suggests both the train itself and the Brazilian hinterland through which it passes.

### The Ária, the first of the two movements of the fifth of the *Bachianas brasileiras*, originally scored for soprano and orchestra of cellos, was written in 1938. A second movement *Dança* was added in 1945. The first of these two movements is among the best known of all and is more commonly heard as a vocal work. *O trenzinho*, which is a world away from eighteenth century Leipzig. The *Ária* itself offers a very Brazilian melody over a Baroque bass accompaniment for the eight cellos of the original scoring, with the first cello shadowing the soprano melodic line. The movement, one of particular lyrical beauty, is equally effective in the arrangement for Rio de Janeiro in 1945, at the first of the two movements.

The sixth of the *Bachianas brasileiras* for flute and bassoon was written in 1938 and first performed in Rio de Janeiro in 1945. The first of the two movements, in the style of the chôro, declared in the title, is combined with an element of contrapuntal Bach. The limitations of the medium are triumphantly overcome in the second movement *Fantasie*, with a first section in the chôro-tradition. An opening melody offers the flute to suggest the counterpoint of Bach, while the bassoon offers suggestions from Brazil, with its more percussive Brazilian. The *Fantasia*, marked *Allegro* after the first movement *Largo*, is one of lively invention, calling for considerable virtuosity in performance, both in the chromatic sequences of the upper part and the answering figuration for the bassoon.

*Assobio a jato* (Jet Whistle), for flute and cello, was written in 1950, the year of the eightieth of the eleven symphonies of Villa-Lobos and the twelfth of his works, arousing the anger of critics and occasionally of audiences. The composer was overtly concerned with instrumental virtuosity, particularly in a series of concertos for guitar, for harp and for harmonica, his five piano concertos, and the famous *Fantasia* for soprano and tenor saxophones. *Assobio a jato* makes considerable demands on the players. The first movement seems to echo in its texture the *Bachianas brasileiras*. Its middle section explores polyphonic possibilities, in a texture filled by the double- and multiple-tonguing of the cells. The final Vivo opens with the ostinato of the cello, to which the flute adds an angular and sequential melodic line in a movement that allows both instruments brief moments of relaxation into a more lyrical mood.

### About the Artists

**Rebecca Rust**
The American cellist Rebecca Rust was born in San Francisco and had her first piano lessons at the age of five from her mother. Four years later she began her study of the cello, winning an award in the Mendelssohn Competition at the age of thirteen. After study with Bernard Greenhouse at the University of New York, with Paul Bazelaire at the Cologne Conservatory, with Ivan Petrushansky at the Accademia Musicale Chigiana in Siena and with Tibor de Machula in Düsseldorf and Switzerland, she settled in Munich, with a career in Europe, America and Japan as a soloist and chamber-music player. Rebecca Rust plays an Italian cello of the seventeenth century, the work of the Florentine Giovanni Gagliano Pazzini, a pupil of Maggini.

**David Apter**
The pianist David Apter was born in New York and studied at the famous Juilliard School, at Manhattan School and at Yale University, as a pupil of teachers who included Sascha Gorodnitzki, Nadia Boulanger and Paul Badura-Skoda. A recent concert tour of South America led to a Fulbright Scholarship to Munich, where he met Rebecca Rust.

**Emmanuel Pahud**
Emmanuel Pahud was born in Geneva in 1970 of Swiss and French parentage. In 1992 he was appointed principal flautist with the Basle Radio Symphony Orchestra, moving to a similar position with the Munich Philharmonic in 1992 and in 1993 to the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra. He had his first flute lessons at the age of six, studying in Rome, Paris and Geneva. He continued his studies at the Supérieur in Paris, where he won first prize for flute and chamber music in 1990. Other prizes include the Brest Competition, the Geneva International Competition, and his career has brought concert appearances throughout Europe, in the United States of America and Japan.

**Friedrich Edelmann**
Friedrich Edelmann was born in Baden-Württemberg and studied the bassoon with Alfred Rindensbacher. After winning first prize in the Federal Jugend Musiziert Competition, he studied with Klaus Thunemann in Hamburg and with Milan Turkovic in Vienna. In 1977 he became principal bassoonist with the Munich Philharmonic Orchestra under Sergiu Celibidache, and from 1987 to 1992 was a member of the board of administration. Friedrich Edelmann has travelled widely as a soloist and player of chamber music, with concerts, recitals and masterclasses in Europe, the Americas and Japan.
