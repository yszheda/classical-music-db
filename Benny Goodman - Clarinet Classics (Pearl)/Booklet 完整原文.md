### BENNY GOODMAN

Around 1937, when swing was really hot and bandleaders were desperate for new material, Benny turned to classical music, material that they could swing, royalty and without royalties at all. When Benny Goodman was well known for his versions of Alec Templeton's Bach Goes to Town arranged by Henry Brant, (Templeton is available on PAST CD 7057). But the Goodman introduction into the classics was not by his own contention, he was approached in the spring of 1935 by jazz impresario John Hammond, a man of many interests but who was interested in swing music. John had been listening to the new music, which he had never heard before. That seems to have started, practically simultaneously, a quartet of good swing players. Since it was not until they thought that Goodman was ready to try it in public. They did a successful test concert before 200 or so in Hammond mansion, Mildred Bailey and Red Norvo in attendance.

Goodman decided to make a commercial recording.

The recording session with the Pro Arte String Quartet was aborted and almost immediately. From the group an oboist arrived, who was unprepared and inadequate for the task. He had also come in cold, having played a gig the night before, he packed up and walked out very much embarrassed. Typical of Benny Goodman, though he practiced even harder on his classical technique – on attack, tone and colouring, sustaining notes down and finishing phrases properly. After all, it's a very demanding classical technique, and different expectations as well as a different kind of reed and mouthpiece; a good deal less vibrato.

In April 1938 he did record the Mozart Quintet for RCA, with the Budapest String Quartet. The notes to the original issue of the Mozart Quintet began: "There may be some curiosity as to the musical association of Mr. Benny Goodman, clarinetist, whose fame in the minds of the general public rests decidedly in fields far removed from chamber music, with so mature a group as the Budapest String Quartet." The note went on to assure the public that this was not a stunt. It was recorded in accordance with Victor's policy of adding to its library of masterpieces music by the best obtainable artists.

Benjamin David Goodman was born in a family of twelve children. Born in Chicago on 30 May 1909, to David and Dora Goodman, immigrants from the Ukraine. They had met and married in Baltimore and moved to the Jewish ghetto in downtown Chicago in 1905, where David scratched out a desperately poor existence working as a tailor and sometimes as a shoveller of stinking raw lard in the stockyards. David loved music and singing. Benny's first experience of it was probably as Sunday afternoon theatre concerts. David fell in with a new neighbour, a tailor shop foreman, and noticed that his children played instruments and earned a bit of money on the side. The nearby Kehelah Jacob Synagogue had a boys band, which supplied instruments and music for free. Most, if not all, three of his boys down and signed them up for the band. Benny, assigned a clarinet. Rudimentary lessons from bandmaster Charles Harrison followed. The Goodman boys would have played marches, collected money in synagogues, and at bar mitzvahs of popular music. But serious music made little impact on him and the other boys because jazz was coming in – big time. Just after the formal concerts, the boys found out-of-the-way places to jam for hours.

Benny's next teacher, at around the age of ten, circa 1919, was the great Franz Schoepp, a strict teacher in America. He gave Benny the fundamentals of a sound classical clarinet technique – though Benny had no idea of what that meant. Schoepp instilled in him the correct technique – proper embouchure, correct fingering, proper breath control, as well as the absolute necessity of steady, progressive practice. That relentless drive to practise served Goodman all his life. Even when he was by far the best instrumentalist in his own band, he worked at scales and exercises day for hours on end. Franz and Benny went through all the major clarinet instruction books in the two years they worked together. Then Benny would have been exposed to all sorts of arrangements of classical pieces and exercises. He would have got to know a piece like Mozart's Quintet, he was thrilled by the music.

Incidentally, Schoepp defied social convention and taught black students on Tuesday. Rapp, a fellow pupil of Goodman and a student who played clarinet, said Noone was also a Schoepp student. After his father died, 14-year old Benny helped support the family by playing at dance halls, unfulfilled by John Pollack hired him. Between jobs he jammed with the Austin High Gang and was there.

He left Pollack in New York in 1928, and joined Red Nichols as a studio musician. In 1935 Benny accepted John Hammond's urging to record for Columbia and auditioned hire Teddy Wilson and Lionel Hampton along with Gene Krupa and in the next year to try out Fletcher Henderson's hot arrangements. When Goodman introduced Henderson's charts at the Palomar Ballroom in Los Angeles on 21 August 1935, the Swing Era was born and Benny was dubbed the King of Swing.

It was Hammond, through his acquaintance with Ben Schneider, who arranged for the Budapest Quartet to make its American recording of the Mozart Quintet with Benny Goodman. The set was received politely by the classical critics: Goodman had "the tone you expect to see with the music; the phrasing was impeccable; the playing accurate, but the tone was inconstant; there was trace of a jazz vibrato. The jazz press, intent on legitimizing jazz and jazz crossover, was very enthusiastic. Goodman reflected, "...it struck me that I really would like to know what the hell I'm doing", so he went to study with Simeon Bellison of the New York Philharmonic main. In the Late 1940s he studied with Reginald Kell, the British clarinettist whose small, light sound and vibrato made him one of the dark, resonant American sound, changed Goodman's sound to make it thinner and less resonant. (Kell may be heard in the Brahms clarinet quintet, on GEM 0007).

In the 1930s Nadia Reisenberg (1904-83), the wonderful Russian-born American pianist, appeared several times on The Benny Goodman Music Festival, a radio show, and they practiced a lot together. Goodman was working on the Brahms Trio with Piatigorsky. Benny and Nadia recorded the Grand Duo Concertant in 1947 (unreleased) and Brahms' Variations on a Theme.
