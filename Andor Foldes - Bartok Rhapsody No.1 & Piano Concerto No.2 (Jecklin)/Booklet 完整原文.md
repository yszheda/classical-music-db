## Andor Foldes

Andor Foldes ist einer der meistgersehrten Künstler seiner Generation, gefeiert in den Musikmetropolen der ganzen Welt. Er wurde 1913 in Budapest geboren. Den ersten Klavierunterricht erhielt er von seiner Mutter, die selbst Pianistin war. Mit vier Jahren debütierte er mit einem eigenen Konzert in Budapest. Im Alter von acht Jahren debütierte er mit einem eigenen Konzert in Budapest und erhielt ein Stipendium am Konservatorium. Mit zwanzig Jahren gewann er als Schüler von Ernst von Dohnányi das Lottostipendium, den ersten Preis beim Rubinstein-Wettbewerb in Budapest und den ersten Preis beim Internationalen Franz Liszt Klavierwettbewerb in Budapest. Danach begann seine internationale Karriere, die ihn durch ganz Europa, Nord- und Südamerika sowie in die USA führte. In New York debütierte er mit dem NBC-Orchester, an der Spitze von Arturo Toscanini. Er unternahm ausgedehnte Tourneen durch Nord- und Südamerika. Er ließ sich sogar in den USA nieder und heiratete dort 1947 amerikansche Staatsbürger. Die erste europäische Nachkriegstournee absolvierte Andor Foldes 1949. Seitdem konzertiert er in allen wichtigen Städten Europas. Seit 1954 bereiste er wiederholt Südamerika, Indien, Australien, Neuseeland und Japan. Während der letzten Saison gastierte Andor Foldes acht Japanese, den ihn der heutige Kaiser von Japan, damals zu einem Privatkonzert ein. Für seine Verdienste um den Wiederaufbau der Bonner Beethoven-Halle, zu deren Gunsten er Konzerte gab, wurde ihm in New York, London, Buenos Aires und Tokio gehuldigt, und für seine künstlerischen Leistungen wurde ihm 1964 das Grosse Bundesverdienstkreuz verliehen.

In Frankreich wurde er mit der "Médaille d'Argent de la Ville de Paris" sowie mit der Auszeichnung "Commandeur de la Mérite Culturel et Artistique" geehrt. Auch schriftstellerisch ist der Künstler hervorgetreten. Sein Buch "Wege zum Klavier", in einer Neuauflage bei Ullstein 1990 erschienen, wurde bisher in vier Sprachen übersetzt. Seine Schallplattenaufnahmen des Klavierwerks von Béla Bartók erhielten den Grand Prix du Disque und den Preis der Deutschen Phonoakademie. Seit 1961 lebt der Künstler zusammen mit seiner Frau, der Schriftstellerin Lili Foldes, in Herrliberg bei Zürich.

## Personal Introduction by Andor Foldes and Béla Bartók

**Béla Bartók's handwritten note:**

*I have the pleasure in stating that Mr. Andor Foldes played several of my works to me and to my high satisfaction and I had never, from musical point of view, heard such a performance of my works.*

*BÉLA BARTÓK, Amsterdam*

*II. KONZERT für Klavier und Orchester*

*Here made a great impression, and he seems to be an excellent colleague of my music.*

*New York, May 16, 1940*
*Béla Bartók*

## An Introduction by Andor Foldes

When I arrived in America in December 1939, I had two treasures with me: a Bodola-Banknote and a photocopy of Béla Bartók's Second Piano Concerto. The fifty dollars didn't last long, but it was eight years before I finally had the chance to play the piano concerto in New York.

This masterpiece, which I have always believed to be one of the finest piano concertos of the 20th century, had a strange history. I began to study the work with Bartók himself while still in Budapest. While I had considered Bartók as the greatest figure among modern composers, for a long time – some pages of the first movement of his concerto – with its unmistakably to Bach, without being reminiscent in any way neo-Classical style practiced by Stravinsky – I found this music revolutionary in the extreme in the early 1930s. I even thought it was unplayable at first, and complained about this to Bartók. But he just smiled and said that I'd find it easy in a few years' time. Actually I never did find it easy, but at least the music became playable after lots of practice, and that was some consolation.

I spent eight years going from one conductor to another in the States, asking them in vain to let me play this wonderful work. One of the best-known conductors, a Hungarian American, said it would take too much rehearsal time, and its success was more than questionable. But I didn't give up: I went on working on the music and waiting for the right opportunity. Finally, it wasn't the New York Philharmonic or the NBC Orchestra, but a small conductor of the American Youth Orchestral Association – that dared to put the concerto on its programme! After a series of rehearsals, once a week late in the evening for six weeks, since the members of the orchestra all had other jobs during the day, week saw the concert performance in Carnegie Hall. The hall wasn't full, but the concerto was nevertheless a huge success. On the next evening, the critics were unanimous in expressing amazement that this work had not been played earlier in first New York performance. (Bartók completed the concerto in 1933, and I didn't play it in New York until 1947).

A year later, the New York Philharmonic was suddenly interested in the concerto, too, and wanted to put it on their programme. And so it came about that I was able to play the concerto twice in succession, in New York City, after waiting for eight years. And this triggered off the triumphant progress of this extraordinary work through the world's concert halls. In the next few years, I played it no less than 41 times – in Paris, in Italy (in the purpose of Prom Concerts), in Oslo, Stockholm, Johannesburg, Tokyo with the Japanese Radio Orchestra (NHK), in Buenos Aires (in one of the most beautiful concert halls in the world, the Teatro Colon), in Sydney, Australia and in Wellington, New Zealand, to name just a few. In Copenhagen I even played it three times: first, in two successive concert seasons, and then I was invited back again when the Danish Radio Orchestra celebrated its 40th anniversary.

To mark the occasion, a concert was given featuring the most successful works in the orchestra's forty-year history. I was delighted that Bartók's Second Piano Concerto was among the pieces chosen. Looking back today, I am filled with even greater sadness that the composer did not live to see the triumphant success of his work. I accompanied Bartók during the suffering of his last years in the USA, when he finally died of leukemia in 1945, in exile from his own country, neglected and impoverished. "Years pass without a single orchestra playing even one note of my music," he said to me one day, and added with bitter humour, "it's a disgrace – but not for me."

Bartók was in the audience one evening in Budapest, when at an early age I played three Beethoven piano concertos (nos. 1, 4 and 5) in a single concert. When he came backstage to congratulate me, I told him, almost in a few years' time I shall perform one concerto by you on the same evening. He smiled and said I shouldn't talk nonsense – he had only written two, after all. Many years later, he wrote his Piano Concerto no. 3. I have since played all three frequently, and actually did give them all in one concert in Bruxelles once. Bartók was unfortunately no longer alive, but I had kept my word.

I have often played the Rhapsody op. 1 with considerable pleasure. Bartók wrote it when he was 24. When I wrote this virtuosic work, it is striking music, and demands great brilliance from the pianist. In terms of style, it belongs to the composer's youthful period of Hungarian nationalism, and still shows the unmistakable influence of Liszt, Wagner and even Richard Strauss. The listener will certainly find it interesting to listen first to the Rhapsody and then to the Second Piano Concerto: the radical development Bartók went through is brought out strongly in this comparison. Incidentally, Bartók had entered the Rhapsody without success in a competition for composers and soloists for the Rubinstein Prize. Bartók appeared as both pianist and composer, but the pianist's prize went to Wilhelm Backhaus, while a totally unknown Italian by the name of Brugnoli was awarded the composer's prize.

Brugnoli's name has long since sunk into oblivion, but Béla Bartók will never be eradicated from the pages of music history. The music of the 20th century is inconceivable without him.

**Andor Foldes**
*Translation: Clive Williams*
