## Liner Notes by Peter Grove

The festival "Rarities of Piano Music", held annually in Schloss vor Husum, offers something different from many summer festivals in repertory which is largely unexplored. The pieces new to me were played without blemish and I had the impression that they were not intended for self-indulgence. The recordings of the past have been forgotten because their music is unflashable, and because their output was small. Other pieces may be better-known now, but less often played than their merit would warrant. The art of piano recital in Husum is something else: another area of music which is explored at Husum.

The eight concerts took place in a fairly small room in the castle with an audience of 100. It is a wonderful intimate venue and the acoustics were perfect. The concert, but conveying it on CD has posed a few problems for this series because the acoustic is somewhat dead compared with more conventional concert halls. In this latest recording, Asinus Studio has made to give more depth and resonance to the sound and we hope that this has been successful. However, unlike many "live" recordings, the tracks here are completely unedited and have been presented in the concert, with no "patching" of passages which were not be perfect. The artists know that, and always co-operate in the choice of pieces chosen for an album.

It is not easy to convey the full experience of the festival in a little over an hour of music, but we have chosen one work of major beauty and all the other items drawn from both the official programmes and the encores.

**Steven Osborne** is a Scottish pianist whose recordings for Hyperion have made him a fine reputation already. His programme in Husum included two of a set of fairy-tales preludes by the Russian composer Kapustin, and a number of pieces by Medtner. The first is marked "Allegro con espressione" and the second is marked "Allegro con spirito" (theoretically, with menace) and with the sub-title "Sonata" evokes the sound of bells.

Medtner's music always includes the piano, whether in songs, chamber music, concertos or solo pieces, and in his late fifties he produced three sonatas and a ballade. He was greatly admired by Rachmaninov and both had a reputation in his homeland before leaving Russia in 1921. Rachmaninov came to arrange and finance an American tour; his reception in Paris was less enthusiastic. He died in London in 1951, and was greatly helped in his final years by the Maharajah of Mysore. His music reflects the traditions of the Mother Society and enabled him to make recordings of important concertos.

The Sonata-Ballade, played here by **Konstantin Lifschitz**, was written from 1912 to 1914 and dedicated to Medtner's second Galina Prize for composition. Its three movements are marked to be played without a break.

**Steven Osborne** belongs to a new generation of British pianists. He began to play the piano at the age of 4 and to a music school founded by Prince Michael Olginsky, who later paid for him to study piano composition at the Moscow Conservatory from 1894. He later went further in Leipzig, travelled to the Caucasus, and settled finally in London.

**Nicholas Walker** tells the English composition: "In the mid 1920s, Walker tried to create two miniature pieces that were a relatively new kind of voice of which Enrico Tamberlik was perhaps the great exponent. It lends particular historic significance to these recordings even though they were made in 1901, when he was sixty and a veteran of a career that he was virtually retired from the concert platform.

**Kolja Lessing** is a legal virtuoso in Hamburg who has earned every second year since the age of fifteen. His programme contained two long and complex sonatas by Godowsky and Szymanowski, as well as a piece by the Brazilian composer Villa-Lobos which was a favourite of Artur Rubinstein.

**Frédéric Meinders** is a particularly interesting pianist who makes many of his own transcriptions and is not afraid to venture into the popular "cocktail" repertoire at times. First we hear his arrangement of a piece as "Softly as a Morning Sunrise" from the opera of Sigmund Romberg (1886-1951). It began as a piece for left hand alone. The second had added much later in the piece. Then comes one of the popular songs of Fats Waller (1904-43), written in 1929. Finally, Meinders plays a short study-like piece of his own, entitled "Choro".

The final selection on the disc is played by the outstanding duo **Yaara Tal & Andreas Groethuysen**, who played works for four hands at both ends and two pianos. The composer Ignaz Friedman (1882-1948) was born near Krakow and was best known as a concert pianist. However, his musical output was lengthy and impressive. A rather pleasant fantasy of Hummel in this piece. The artists discussed the performers and the audience, and the score for these five Waltzes was kindly provided by Professor Neil McGee from New York, who has a special interest in Friedman and is a regular visitor to the Husum concerts.
