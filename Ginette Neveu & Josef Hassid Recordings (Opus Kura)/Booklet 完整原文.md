## Ginette Neveu (1919-1949)

Born in Paris into a musical family, Ginette Neveu became a violinist and her brother Jean Neveu a pianist. She was also the granddaughter of composer Charles-Marie Widor. A child prodigy, Ginette took lessons from her mother and made her solo debut at the age of seven with the Colonne Orchestra in Paris by Bruch's Violin Concerto No.1. Her parents decided to send her to study under Lise Talluel, and after further studies with Jules Boucherit at the Paris Conservatoire, she completed her training with instruction from George Enescu, Nadia Boulanger, and Carl Flesch.

At age 15, Ginette Neveu achieved worldwide celebrity status when she won the Henryk Wieniawski Violin Competition over 180 contestants, including the future virtuosos David Oistrakh, who finished second. Neveu was immediately signed to an extensive concert tour that, over the next two years, saw her give solo performances at the leading concert halls of Germany, Poland, the Soviet Union, the United States, and Canada.

Neveu's international career was interrupted by World War II, but she finally was able to make her London debut in 1945. Jean accompanied her and toured post-war Europe extensively, as well as visiting Australia and South America. They also made return engagements at major venues in the United States. Noted for her intensity, power, and impeccable sonority, Ginette Neveu is recognized as one of the world's great violinists, despite a career that ended at a very young age.

Neveu gave her last concert on October 20, 1949. A week later, on October 27, she and her brother boarded an Air France flight en route to another series of concert engagements. The plane crashed on an island in the Azores, killing all 48 passengers on board.

Her actual recording career started in 1945 at EMI Records in London with Sibelius Violin Concerto and Brahms Concerto in 1946 (the OPRK2061). She made recordings with her brother Jean and with Philharmonia Orchestra in 1946 and 1948 (Debussy). However, Chausson's Poème and Debussy Violin Sonata were not issued as 78rpm. They were issued as LP in 1957 for the first time. Therefore, French LP was used for the transfer here.

## Josef Hassid (1923-1950)

After lessons with a local violin teacher he studied from 1934 at the Chopin School of Music in Warsaw. Huberman was impressed and arranged for Hassid to study under the Hungarian virtuoso Carl Flesch at his summer course in 1937 at Spa, Belgium, where fellow students included Ginette Neveu.

Hassid came to London with his father in 1938 at Flesch's invitation, to continue studies with him. Flesch wrote that "Hassid was no doubt one of the strongest violin talents of his time."

Hassid made his public debut at a recital with Gerald Moore in the Wigmore Hall on 3 April 1940.

Fred Gaisberg of EMI arranged for a test recording of Elgar's La Capricieuse and accompanied Ivor Newton at the Abbey Road Studios on 9 January 1939 when Hassid had just turned 15; and then Walter Legge produced a further eight recordings on 12 and 28 June and 29 November 1940, this time accompanied by Gerald Moore (all in this CD).

But by February 1941 it became apparent that he was suffering from a severe mental disorder. He was diagnosed with schizophrenia and in June he was admitted to a hospital for insulin coma therapy and electroconvulsive therapy. In 1943 Hassid was transferred to an asylum in Epsom. He remained there for the rest of his life and the following year after an unsuccessful brain operation (lobotomy). Hassid developed meningitis and died on 7 November 1950.
