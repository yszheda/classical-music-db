## Liner Notes by Andrew Rangell

### INVENTIONS, BWV 772-786 | SINFONIAS, BWV 787-801

Toward the end of 1722, the earliest versions of these 30 small but remarkable specimens of 2 and 3 part polyphony were entered into the "Clavierbüchlein" for Wilhelm Friedemann Bach, each 2-part work labeled "praeambulum", each 3-part work, "fantasia". The next year Bach's autograph fair copy retitled the pieces as "Inventions" and "sinfonias" respectively. Additionally, both sets were rearranged into a final format: C-c-D-d-E flat-E-e-F-f-G-g-A-a-B flat-b. (Lower case = minor mode). In these works no key signature of more than four accidentals appears - this, of course, in distinction to Part I of the tonally all-embracing Well-Tempered Clavier, which appeared at about the same time. The 1723 title page clearly states Bach's didactic purpose in these ordered collections. Yet what shines through these thirty pieces is the sheer poetic content and character of each small adventure. It is natural that greater weight and/or intensity is to be found in the sinfonias. (For me, the pearl of these is the heavily chromatic F minor - whose three interlocking themes perform a unique, solemn and magical ritual.) The common feature of all of these compositions is that each unfolds from a single "idea" developed to its conclusion. In most of the inventions the "idea" is a single line, then restated with counter-material. In others the immediate and combined interaction of two voices constitutes the idea. Episodes intervene, advancing the discourse. Invertible counterpoint is routine. Most of the sinfonias begin with a two-voice interaction, soon adding a third voice, in what resembles a fugal exposition, continuing, with motivically related episodes, to a conclusion. Clearly the inventions and sinfonias were seen by Bach as an early and comprehensive preparation for the preludes and fugues of the Well-Tempered Clavier.

They rock.

---

### ARIA VARIATA, BWV 989 (c. 1708)

This early and colorful set of 10 short variations (the last one an embellished restatement of the theme) stands together with the late and monumental Goldberg Variations to form the bookends of Bach's only efforts in keyboard variations. The work attests to Bach's earliest study of Italian stylistic models. The theme, in four voices, is harmonically rich; the variations, reverting to two voices, are brilliantly decorative rather than probing. Convention would dictate that these be repeated yet I strongly prefer to play them (i.e. experience them) without repeat. For me, this helps keep the lettuce crisp.

### PRELUDE IN D MINOR, BWV 926 | JESU MEIN FREUDE, BWV 753

These short contrasting creations appear early in the "Clavierbüchlein" for Wilhelm Friedemann Bach, and to me suggested a pairing of sorts. The Jesu, a fragment from an organ chorale-prelude, is a strangely moving piece in three-part polyphony, the upper line retaining the melody, presented in nine successive closely-related phrases. The little prelude, an unpredictable and hemiola-filled ride, breaks into sixteenth notes suddenly near the end, yet returns, just as suddenly, to eighths in its few concluding measures. Quite a shock, in such a tiny piece!
