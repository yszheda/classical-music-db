## Liner Notes

Verdi had been reluctant to return to the composition of operas after *Aida*. The latter's overwhelming artistic and financial success after its premiere in 1871 seemed to put a seal on a career spanning more than 50 years. This reluctance may also, in part, have stemmed from an awareness that the musical world was changing, and that Italian audiences and composers were increasingly looking outside Italy – to France and Germany – for new aesthetic ideas. He withdrew into self imposed retirement at his house and farm at Sant'Agata. He was by now a wealthy man and could look back on a lifetime at the forefront of musical tastes, and had nothing more to prove. And yet, extraordinary as it seems, he felt himself to be something of an outsider in the musical world unfolding around him. This made him crotchety. In a dark mood he declared: 'When I want to make music I can make it in my own room, without hearing the verdicts of the learned or the imbeciles.'

Ironically, it was the death of the writer Alessandro Manzoni, whom Verdi idolized, that enabled him to throw off this gloomy state of mind, for it inspired the *Messa da Requiem* of 1874. After a performance of this at La Scala in June 1879, which was marked by huge demonstrations of affection for the composer both in the theatre and on the streets, he began to think that he was not a forgotten man after all. When, during a dinner with friends, his publisher Giulio Ricordi steered the conversation around to Shakespeare and the work of the librettist and composer Arrigo Boito, Verdi's instincts were aroused. The result was the masterpiece *Otello*, which was given its first performance at La Scala in 1887. That surely was to be the composer's last word? His friends and admirers hoped not and, in 1889, Boito sent him a synopsis of a proposed libretto to be called *Falstaff*.

## Page 5 (scans/HIPPO_20230225_0005.jpg)

Initially, Verdi had reservations about starting yet another opera at the age of seventy eight. However it did not take much for Boito to persuade him that writing a comedy would be a rejuvenating experience. Verdi replied soon afterwards: 'I'm amusing myself by writing fugues! Yes, sir, a fugue: and a comic fugue which would be suitable for *Falstaff*!'

In Boito, Verdi had found an inspired and intelligent librettist who was also a composer in his own right. Shakespeare's 23 characters in *The Merry Wives of Windsor* were reduced to just 10 in *Falstaff*. Boito drastically pruned Shakespeare's text until he had a libretto that was quite skeletal. Characters frequently utter only single words as they propel the action along at break neck speed. Verdi was concerned to have syllables that were singable, and he harped on and on to librettists about the need to be succinct.

Verdi's 'little enjoyment' as he had once called *Falstaff*, was a triumph at its premiere at La Scala on 9 February 1893, the year in which he turned 80 years of age.

Fernando Corena (1916-1984), who sings Falstaff on this recording, was born in Geneva to a Turkish father and an Italian mother. He studied theology at Fribourg University but after winning a vocal contest, turned his attention to music, studying in Geneva and then Milan. His professional debut in 1947 was in Trieste as Varlaam in *Boris Godunov*, and then he performed throughout Italy, singing the standard bass repertoire. He made his first appearance at La Scala in 1948, and his Metropolitan debut was as Leporello in *Don Giovanni* in 1954. For almost three decades he performed all the major comic and character roles, including Falstaff, which he sang at the Met, Edinburgh Festival (1953 and 1956), Glyndebourne (1955), and for Israeli Opera (1959) amongst others. Corena became highly regarded on stage for his wit, style and flair for improvisation.

## Page 6 (scans/HIPPO_20230225_0006.jpg)

In 'Ehi! paggio! ... L'onore!' we hear Falstaff summoning Robin, the page. He then abuses Bardolph and Pistol for refusing to deliver love letters to two (married) ladies on the grounds that honour wouldn't allow it. He gives the page the letters and then rounds on the pair, asking what right they have to speak of honour, and observing that 'honour is no more than a word'. It is a memorable scene inspired by Shakespeare's *Henry IV, Part One*.

'Presenteremo un bill', comes from Act II, and involves Alice Ford, Mistress Quickly, Meg Page, Nanetta and Falstaff. Alice suggests that a bill should be presented to Parliament for a tax on fat men. They are all getting ready to give Falstaff the reception he deserves. Nanetta is sad because her father wants her to marry the pedantic Dr. Caius, and a large linen basket is carried in, which will be emptied (with its human contents) into the brook later on. Falstaff arrives and begins his seduction of Alice Ford by describing his former good looks when, as a young man, he was a page to the Duke of Norfolk. In those days, he says 'I was so slim, so supple and so agile that I could have slipped through a ring'.

Ilva Ligabue (1932-1998) was an Italian soprano, best known for her performances of Alice Ford, which she also recorded with Sir Georg Solti and Leonard Bernstein. She studied in Milan and went on to sing at La Scala, Vienna State Opera, Paris Opera, Glyndebourne Festival, Buenos Aires, Chicago and New York. A sensitive and intelligent singer and a fine actress, she was also highly regarded as Amelia in *Un ballo in maschera* and as Desdemona in *Otello*.

## Page 7 (scans/HIPPO_20230225_0007.jpg)

The American mezzo soprano Regina Resnik (1922-2009) began her career when, with one day's notice in 1944, she substituted for Zinka Milanov as Leonora in *Il trovatore*, with great success. She enjoyed two internationally successful singing careers, first as a soprano, and later as a mezzo soprano. In both repertoires, she was noted for her outstanding musicianship and superb dramatic abilities. She performed in a remarkable variety of roles ranging, in the early days, from Micaela and Butterfly to Aida and Sieglinde. Later she would add Mistress Quickly, Amneris, Eboli, Herodias, Fricka, Orlovsky, and the title role in *Pique Dame*, amongst others. She sang in most of the world's great houses, including Bayreuth, and made some outstanding recordings. Her career spanned 60 years and more than 80 roles. She came to be regarded as the definitive Mistress Quickly.

'Ehi! Taverniere! Mondo ladro' sees Falstaff in Act III, sitting in a courtyard outside the Garter Inn. He is brooding over his humiliation at the hands of the women and takes consolation in the wine, but concludes that the world is in a sorry state. No contrast could be greater than the opening of the second scene in Windsor Great Park, when the young Fenton under Herne's Oak sings the gentle love song 'Dal labbro il canto estasiato vola' (From my lips a song of ecstasy flies) to the orchestra's exquisite accompaniment. 'Lips that are kissed lose none of their allure. Indeed, they renew it like the moon', replies Nanetta in the moonlight in the garb and persona of the Fairy Queen. 'Sul fil d'un soffio etesio' (On the breath of a fragrant breeze) she sings, leading her fairy followers in song and dance.

Fenton is sung by Luigi Alva, born in 1927 in Peru. He quickly achieved an international reputation, especially in Mozart and Rossini and combined a beautiful voice with a dashing appearance and fine acting ability. His Fenton at Covent Garden in 1961 was hailed as unsurpassed.

Italian soprano Lydia Marimpietri (b. 1933) began her career in the 1950s, and in 1959 made her debut at La Scala as Nella in Puccini's *Gianni Schicchi*. She sang there many times afterwards and performed in other major Italian houses as well as at Glyndebourne (Drusilla in Monteverdi's *Incoronazione di Poppea* and Susanna in *Le nozze di Figaro*), Mexico City, Barcelona (Marguerite in Faust), Covent Garden, Chicago (in 1968 as Nannetta) and the Vienna State Opera as Mimi in *La boheme*.

## Page 8 (scans/HIPPO_20230225_0008.jpg)

The final two tracks of the Falstaff excerpts return to earlier scenes of the opera. In Act I in Master Ford's garden ('Alice... Meg... Nanetta'), Alice and Meg have received Falstaff's love letters. They compare them, see that they are identical and, together with Mistress Quickly and Nanetta, resolve to punish him.

'Siam pentiti e contritti ... Reverenza' occurs in Act II, in the Garter Inn. Bardolph and Pistol are penitent and contrite. 'Man returns to his vices like a cat to cream,' responds Falstaff. 'And we've returned to your service' they reply. Mistress Quickly enters and makes a deep obeisance to Sir John with an exaggerated 'Reverenza'. Her words 'Povera donna' ('poor lady' in reference to Alice Ford's supposed infatuation with Falstaff) and the accompanying music appear to be a direct quote from Violetta's self pitying recitative in Act I of *La traviata*. Quickly delivers messages from Alice and from Meg Page, the upshot being that Alice will receive Falstaff from two till three when her husband is absent. Falstaff repeats the words with evident delight, confirms his attendance and, unwittingly sets the stage for his own undoing. Ford (Alice's husband) is announced, in disguise as Fontana ('Fountain' – in Shakespeare, 'Brook'). Fontana presents Falstaff with a purse of silver and tells him he is in love with Mistress Ford, whose chastity he cannot overcome, and begs Falstaff to court her and make the way easier for him. Falstaff gleefully reveals his rendezvous that very afternoon. When Fontana (Ford) asks if Falstaff knows her husband, he hears himself described with a torrent of abuse. When Falstaff leaves the room to change into more elaborate clothes, Ford is left alone and muses on how he will avenge this outrage. In the process, he praises the jealousy in the depths of his heart.

Ford is sung by Renato Capecchi (1923-1998), an Italian baritone who sang in the Italian premiere of Shostakovich's *The Nose* and Prokofiev's *War and Peace*. In 1950 he began an association with La Scala and, shortly thereafter, he was engaged by the Metropolitan Opera, making his debut in New York as the elder Germont in *La traviata*. He remained on the roster there until 1954 and, after a period mainly in Europe, returned in 1975 as both singer and stage director. He sang *Falstaff* at Glyndebourne in both 1977 and 1980.

On this disc Fernando Corena also sings excerpts from operas by Rossini and Donizetti. In 'A un dottor della mia sorte' from *Il barbiere di Siviglia*, Dr. Bartolo is keeping Rosina captive inside his house, but when he learns that she has feelings for another man and has attempted to escape, he questions her intensely. The wonderful tangle of thoughts and words contains lines like: 'Ah, you do not need the grimaces, your face looks like a dead cat'. Opera magazine said of Corena's performance of Bartolo in 1954 that he was 'the very picture of self satisfied middle age' and that it was a performance of *Barber* which was 'dominated by the Bartolo'.

In 'Il mio piano è preparato' from *La gazza ladra* the Mayor comes to renew his unwelcome amorous overtures to Ninetta. In his soliloquy he mimics her rejection.

In Donizetti's *L'elisir d'amore* is represented by the aria 'Udite, udite, o rustici' (Hear me, hear me, o peasants) in which the peripatetic quack Dr. Dulcamara makes his grand entrance. In 'Ah! Un foco insolito mi sento addosso' from *Don Pasquale*, Pasquale feels young again after hearing from Malatesta that a beautiful young wife has been found for him. He can already imagine himself married to her with a half dozen children around him! Both arias require all of Corena's sense of character and vocal dexterity, and he delivers admirably.
