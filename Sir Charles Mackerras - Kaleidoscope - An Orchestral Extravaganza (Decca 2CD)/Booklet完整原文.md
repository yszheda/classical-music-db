## Biography - Page 1

Introducing a 1971 BBC interview, the writer, broadcaster and raconteur John Amis had the measure of Charles Mackerras: 'A conductor who is not only more versatile than any other I know' – and Amis had known a few – 'but he's also unique in being accepted as versatile by the public at large,' continued Amis. 'Who else is allowed by the public, managers, radio stations and the like to be a symphonic conductor, an operatic conductor, a ballet conductor, a light music conductor, a specialist in Janáček operas and a specialist in Baroque music?'

Mackerras was born in the New York town of Schnectady, simply because that was where his parents happened to be at the time. His father was an apprentice engineer, studying there on a scholarship with General Electric, but he was Australian by birth. He learnt oboe, piano and composition at the Sydney Conservatorium, and when he emigrated to Europe after the war, he was awarded a British Council scholarship to study in Prague with Vaclav Talich. This was his first opportunity to engage in serious study of conducting.

Mackerras began to conduct a little when he joined the Sadler's Wells Orchestra as an oboist, but he first came to the attention of the British public as an arranger, through a suite of Sullivan's work which he crafted as a ballet for Sadler's Wells in partnership with the South African choreographer John Cranko: *Pineapple Poll* was an instant and lasting success. Sitting week after week in the theatre pit of the Theatre Royal in Sydney, he had often thought that Sullivan's music would make a good ballet, and his stroke of luck was that the copyright ran out on Sullivan's music just when it became possible to make the ballet – which itself was propitious timing for the Festival of Britain in 1951. In June that year he took the Sadler's Wells Orchestra into Abbey Road Studios to record *Pineapple Poll* for HMV – his very first recording – and the ballet's breezy, extrovert humour caught the spirit of the moment.

By the end of the decade, Mackerras was making records at a rate of knots. Twenty days of 1956 were spent in the studios. In May 1958 he made his first album for an organisation other than HMV/EMI and the BBC, when he recorded the Grieg and Sibelius items on the present compilation at the Walthamstow Assembly Rooms in north London. For contractual reasons, the orchestra was named the 'London Proms Symphony Orchestra', though its members were probably drawn largely from the London Symphony Orchestra. The album was originally released by RCA as part of a business venture which had begun the previous year after the American label severed its ties to EMI. Decca took over UK distribution of US-made RCA albums from EMI, and began to press RCA records for release in Europe as well as making some new recordings with its own, fabled engineering team, which included Gordon Parry and Kenneth Wilkinson – the latter being Balance Engineer.

Frequently played recordings included not only opera but Stravinsky directing the *Rite of Spring* (on 78s – so the Columbia recording from 1929), much to the dismay of his neighbours, as well as other modern repertoire including Koussevitzky conducting Debussy's *La Mer* and Sibelius's Fifth Symphony, and Stokowski in Ravel's *Rapsodie espagnole* and Shostakovich's Fifth. His lifelong love of Delius was also established at an early age: 'I used to pester a certain record shop in Sydney every day, after school, when Beecham's recording of *Appalachia* first came out.' More recently he had enjoyed Karajan's Philharmonia recordings of Hindemith and Bartók, as well as Toscanini in Debussy and Ravel.

At least prior to those ad hoc sessions for Mercury/Philips, Mackerras was of the mind that 'A lot of thought and trouble has to go into a recording if it is to have big sales, whereas concerts in London are normally done on one rehearsal! Thus the standard of playing on a record is liable to be higher than at most of our dull and unadventurous concerts.' He would have been gratified to read a Gramophone review of the album's first CD reissue in 1995, observing how 'The performances reveal all the zest, colour and care for detail that have been a hallmark of Mackerras's music making'.

Four years after those 'Kaleidoscope' sessions, the LSO and Mackerras were back in Wembley Town Hall, with more unusual repertoire on the slate, at least for the orchestra: ballet suites by Gluck and Rameau, and overtures by Cherubini and Cimarosa. There was also time at the sessions to record a pair of evergreen overtures by Johann Strauss the younger, to *Die Fledermaus* and *Der Zigeunerbaron*. These achieved limited circulation within compilations of popular overtures. Produced for Philips by Vittorio Negri, they may not share the sheer brilliance of the Mercury recordings but then, as Mackerras remarked: 'Musicians are always more impressed by the musical rather than the technical excellence of a recording.' Nevertheless, he shared the passion of any true record collector: 'I find more and more that my only chance to really enjoy listening to music is to sit at home, relaxed, and hear the finest performances, both old and new, on the gramophone.'
