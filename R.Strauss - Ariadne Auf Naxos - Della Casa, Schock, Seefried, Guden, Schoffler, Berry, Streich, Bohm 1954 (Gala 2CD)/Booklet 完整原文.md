## Prologue

The scene is the house of the richest man in Vienna where a sumptuous banquet is being held. What we witness, however, is the banlieu in another part of the house where preparations are at hand for the lavish post-prandial entertainment which the unnamed host—a millionaire patron of the arts—has ordered for his guests. First in the programme is the heroic opera Ariadne commissioned from a young composer; this performance represents his debut; it is to be followed by a farce in the Italian buffa style: The Faithful Harlequin and her Four Suitors; and the evening is to end with a grand fireworks display.

During the preparation of the opera, the composer's music teacher discovers the straight wig destined for Ariadne to be destined also for the Harlequinade. He objects indignantly to this as a desecration of true art. The Music Master, however, informs him that it is not he who sets the programme but the master of the house, who after all is paying for everything. The music master manages with some difficulty to calm his pupil.

Zerbinetta, for her part, makes fun of the solemn, loving opera with her dancing master and tells him they must try to make the work more acclaim for her own production. The major-domo returns to announce that his master has changed his mind. The Harlequinade will neither precede nor follow Ariadne, but be played simultaneously with it. It remains the damned company that they are professionals and should be able to arrange a double bill with or without the public. Informing them all that the guests are even now on their way, he bids them make haste and wishes them luck.

The composer thinks the world has come to an end and wants to withdraw his work. Straight to the heart, Zerbinetta tries to calm down the situation. He suggests cutting the opera slightly and weaving the ballet into the body of it. The Composer agrees, seeing as Zerbinetta is brilliant at improvising with her four partners. Reluctantly, the music teacher and the composer, who is charmed, consigned by Zerbinetta, agree to this perilous experiment in mixing seriousness with merriment.

## Opera

### The background to ARIADNE AUF NAXOS

Once upon a time, the kingdom of Crete exacted an animal payment of tribute from the kingdom of Athens. This tribute took the grisly form of a number of young men and maidens who, on arrival in Crete, were sacrificed to the Minotaur, a monster, half-bull, half-man, born of the union of Pasiphae, queen of Crete, with a white bull. Now the doomed party was led by the son of the king of Athens, who was determined to slay the monster and thus free Athens of this terrible obligation. The Minotaur was kept in a labyrinth. But Theseus, having killed the beast, was able to find his way out of the labyrinth with the aid of a ball of wood given him by the daughter of Pasiphae, Ariadne, who has fallen in love with him.

When the curtain rises again, it is for the performance of the revised version of Ariadne, as it was shown together in a brief stop of the island of Naxos, for it has abandoned her there while she slept and sailed on to Athens without her. The action of the opera Ariadne auf Naxos begins at this point.

The nymphs, shows deep sympathy with her predicament. As she wakes from troubled dreams Ariadne has only one remaining goal, to die. Thus she longs awaits Hermes, the dark messenger of the Gods.

At this moment Zerbinetta trips by with her happy band. The Harlequinade goes into a song-and-dance number to entertain her. Such grief is healed by the passing of time, they sing. After casting a professional connoisseur's eye over her four potential suitors, Zerbinetta notices that Ariadne is not listening and dismisses them. She will crack this hard nut on her own, talking woman to woman. Although princesses may feel things more deeply, do not all women suffer in this way?

She has herself, several times, yet has never learned to curse men. Ariadne affects not to hear and withdraws to her cave; Zerbinetta is left alone to impart her worldly wisdom: Men are beasts, but what woman can resist them? Sometimes she feels she loves one of them truly, but her instinct for freedom intrudes and she finds herself deceiving him in spite of herself. Yet each affair was more than just a caprice: each man came to her like a god, and was unable to resist.

The nymphs announce the approach of the youthful god Bacchus, who has just triumphed in his first adventure with the sorceress Circe. The heroic god appears, looking radiant. Ariadne, supposing him to be Hermes, the harbinger of death, approaches him with sorrowful timidity. Bacchus, however, deeply moved by Ariadne's beauty, which is transfigured by suffering, awakens her to new life by the miracle of love. Pleased with this outcome, Zerbinetta steps forward, warbling derisively, points to the pair of lovers and disappears with Harlequin and disappears at the same time as Ariadne, left alone, sings in praise of love, the crowning glory of life.

## Lisa Della Casa

Lisa Della Casa is one of the most celebrated sopranos of the post-war era. Born in Lugano, Switzerland, she studied in Geneva and Zurich before making her debut in 1943 at the Theater St. Gallen. She joined the Vienna State Opera in 1950 and became one of its most distinguished members. Her repertoire included roles in operas by Mozart, Strauss, Wagner, and Verdi. She was particularly acclaimed for her interpretations of the Marschallin in Der Rosenkavalier and Ariadne in Ariadne auf Naxos.
