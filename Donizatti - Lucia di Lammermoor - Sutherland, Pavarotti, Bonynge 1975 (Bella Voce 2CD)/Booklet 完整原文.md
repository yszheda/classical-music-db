## Liner Notes

**Lucia di Lammermoor** (1835) is the forty-sixth of the sixty-five operas composed by Gaetano Donizetti (1797-1848). In the years between the death of Bellini (1835) and the emergence of Verdi with *Nabucco* (1842), Donizetti was the dominant figure in the world of Italian opera. Before Salvadore Cammarano adapted Sir Walter Scott's novel, it had been the basis of three earlier Italian libretti. Cammarano's libretto simplifies the action, and focuses on the young Lucia's descent into madness after being tricked by her brother (Enrico) into marrying a man she doesn't love (Arturo). She thereby betrays her true love (Edgardo) - a man his brother hates. The surprise appearance of Edgardo at the wedding provides the setup for one of the greatest ensembles in all of Italian opera - the famous sextet in D flat "*Chi mi frena in tal momento*." It also gives Lucia the final push into dementia. When she appears in the following scene, she has already killed her bridegroom and sings the famous "mad scene," "*Ardon gl'incensi*," as the wedding guests look on in horror. In the final scene, Edgardo, upon learning of Lucia's unfortunate end, sings the brilliant "tomb scene," "*Tombe degli avi miei*," and takes his own life.

The sextet, the mad scene, and the tomb scene are the musical glories of a score that has retained its popularity for a century and a half. *Lucia* has served as a vehicle for practically every soprano with a gift for *fioriture*, from its first interpreter, Fanny Tacchinardi-Persiani, to the great Dame Joan Sutherland (born in 1926), the Lucia of this recording. Maria Callas (1923-1977) brought new life to Lucia in 1955, when she toured in a production by Herbert von Karajan. Until then most people thought of *Lucia* as a showcase for sopranos to display their coloratura skills along with an E flat *alt* at the end of the "mad scene." Callas invested her performance with such emotional depth and range of color that the work itself seemed to be more profound. (Although Callas tended to have that effect on just about everything.) It was probably that portrayal, along with her Anna Bolena at La Scala in 1957, which made the world look afresh at many forgotten works of Donizetti, Bellini, and Rossini, triggering a *bel canto* revival that continues to this day.

Whereas Callas usually gets credit for starting the trend of looking more deeply into the *bel canto* roles, which were considered light fare compared with the *serismo* heroines of the later nineteenth century, it was Joan Sutherland who gets credit for actually showing the world how well these roles could be sung. The tall, shy, Australian soprano appeared as the handmaiden Clotilde on Callas's *Norma* at Covent Garden in 1952, and few would have guessed that there on the same stage with *la divina*, as Callas was called by the Milanese, stood the singer who would soon be seen as the next great Norma, Violetta, and Lucia, and be dubbed "*la stupenda*" by the Venetians. In the early stages of Sutherland's career it was assumed, because of her height and sizable dark voice, that she would eventually grow into the dramatic soprano repertoire. Early on she sang Amelia in Verdi's *Un ballo in maschera* as well as Aida and Eva, and the administration of Covent Garden thought that she eventually would be at least a second-rate Brunnhilde. It was Richard Bonynge (born in 1930), the young coach who would eventually become Sutherland's husband and executive conductor, who recognized the potential in her voice to sing the heroines of the all-but-forgotten *bel canto* operas. Sutherland's appearance in 1957 as Handel's Alcina in concert performances at the Handel Opera Society gave the public a good taste of her capabilities as a coloratura soprano. After much campaigning on Bonynge's part, Covent Garden finally gave Sutherland a new production of *Lucia* with Franco Zeffirelli as director in 1959.

*Lucia* was not all that familiar to Covent Garden audiences at the time. Nellie Melba had made an inauspicious debut as Lucia in 1888, and then Toti del Monte sang it in 1925, it only survived one performance. Callas's two recordings of the opera had done much to reawaken interest in the work, and as a program poll put *Lucia di Lammermoor* high on the list of operas the audience most wanted to see. The night of 17 February 1954 proved to be one of the most important of Sutherland's entire career. Her performance was a triumph in every way. The role put her on the map, vindicating the choice of *Lucia* as the vehicle for her graduation to the international class.

Her singing was exquisite; particularly notable were the sustained notes, followed by an octave drop. Her decorations were tastefully and justly conceived and beautifully executed.

During their twenty-five-year partnership, they made many legendary stage appearances together and numerous classic recordings, including her second *Lucia* recording. (The first was with the vastly inferior Cioni.) Three years before he retired, they performed one act each of *Lucia, La Traviata*, and *Rigoletto* in a special gala performance at the Met (1987) honoring their peerless artistic collaboration. When Sutherland made her stage farewell at Covent Garden in a gala performance of *Die Fledermaus* (New Year's Eve 1990), it was only fitting that Pavarotti should join her for the last act duet from *Traviata*.

This recording catches the pair in peak form. The Chicago audiences went crazy for them, and Sutherland, who had been feeling "too old" to play the young Lucia, suddenly didn't feel so old. (She had turned forty-nine five days before the performance.) Richard Dyer, the music critic of the Boston Globe, described her voice at the time:

*"The voice retains the clarity of its youth in its springing upward to the exultant high E flat. But now it brings into play the chiaroscuro, the mingling of light and shade, the warmer colors that come only with disciplined development and physical maturity."*

Amazingly, Sutherland would return to the Met in 1982 to repeat this success (this time with Alfredo Kraus, her very first Edgardo), defying the years and the skeptics. And the two of them gave it one last run in Barcelona in 1988. Sutherland as Lucia in 1959, and Kraus at sixty-two and Sutherland at sixty-two and Kraus at sixty-one. With a total of 221 stage performances as Lucia (not counting the numerous times she sang excerpts in concert), Sutherland was the supreme *bel canto* heroine of our time.

**- Kendall Clark**

**Andrew Porter**, Financial Times, February 1959:
*"Arpeggios were delicate and lovely, trills were confident. But beyond this there was meaning in everything she did. A singer who can make fioratura beauty in sixths with the flute beats—reading its effect, has understood the secret of Donizetti's music."*

Sutherland's Lucia was suddenly in great demand all over the world. Her debuts as Lucia at the Paris Opera (25 April 1960), La Scala (14 May 1961), and the Metropolitan (26 April 1961) were all highly acclaimed.

Richard Bonynge, always on the lookout for young singers to sing with Joan, was an early champion of the young Luciano Pavarotti (born in 1935). Although the tenor from Modena had already scored a success at Covent Garden in *La Boheme* (replacing an indisposed Giuseppe di Stefano), he had yet to establish himself as a world star. His first appearance with Sutherland was as Edgardo to her Lucia in Miami in 1965, when Renato Cioni cancelled at the last minute to sing Cavaradossi to Maria Callas's Tosca in Paris.

The "Sutherlands" took him under their wing and engaged him for their Australian Tour. Pavarotti was an ideal partner for Sutherland—tall, he had a charismatic personality, and he was able to sing the difficult tenor parts in her operatic repertoire.
