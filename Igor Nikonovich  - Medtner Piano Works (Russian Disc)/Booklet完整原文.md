## Inside front cover

[Image: Portrait of Igor Nikonovich black and white photo]

(left page)

The art of Nikolai Karlovich Medtner - composer and pianist - is one of the most interesting phenomena in the history of music of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Together with Rachmaninov and Scriabin, Medtner belongs among the leading figures of Russian musical culture.

The destinies of these three great contemporaries diverged widely: while Rachmaninov and Scriabin are unanimously recognized as Russian classics, Medtner and his works still await the judgement of history.

By the time Medtner became a composer, Scriabin and Rachmaninov had already achieved world renown. Scriabin's innovative music captivated the audience with its impulsive passion and radiant pathos and Rachmaninov's compositions appealed with their profound emotion.

Deeply intense, the somewhat obscure works of Medtner found the path to the hearts of the audience much more difficult. But already by the beginning of this century, Medtner's name was being spoken as the third great Russian composer of his epoch. Medtner broke no laws of harmony but rather found new possibilities within the major-minor system. Glazunov, giving him his portrait, made a telling inscription on it: "To an artist who stands guard over the eternal laws of art."

Besides over a hundred songs and some chamber compositions, most of Medtner's considerable output is for the piano. Stylistically late Romantic, his works owe much to the influence of Schumann, which is evident even in such external features as poetic titles and notations, and of Brahms, which is apparent the refined contrapuntal textures and architectural structures.

**Medtner (1880-1951)** graduated from the Moscow Conservatory as a piano student of Safonov, but left his composition class after only a year. Afterwards, when asked where he learned composition, he invariably replied that he considered himself a student of Beethoven. He left Russia in 1921 and did not return from exile except for a brief concert tour in 1927. His sensibilities and inspirations, however, ever remained Russian poetry and folklore. He went first to Germany and later settled in Paris, where there was a thriving community of Russian expatriates. The musical life of Paris, however, was foreign to him. He expressed his misgivings about "modern" trends in music then popular in his book *The Muse and Fashion*, which his good friend Rachmaninov published in 1935. Medtner found England more congenial and lived out the remainder of his life there. In the late 1940s, Sir Jaya Chamaraja Wadiyar, the Maharajah of Mysore, lent his financial support to the creation of a Medtner Society for the purpose of recording Medtner's works. It is due to this project that almost all of Medtner's compositions are available on record in the author's interpretations.

In 1903, Medtner published his Opus 1, the **8 Stimmungsbilder** (Mood-pictures). If for many composers the first opus is still a step on the path of finding a personal language, Medtner's early compositions immediately revealed his great and individual gift. "Not too many composers can boast of an Opus 1 like Medtner's Stimmungsbilder," wrote Alexander Goldenweiser, "These are not some timid attempts to compose, but works of a mature, original talent. Mr. Medtner can be proud of his first opus." While influences of Schumann and Brahms are still very apparent in these pieces, those features that would become characteristic in Medtner's art are already present: polyphonic texture, laconic exposition of the principal material, incisive rhythms, and a programmatic inspiration. The first piece of the set, entitled Prologue, carries an epigraph by the great Russian poet Lermontov: "An angel flew in the midnight sky, singing a quiet song..."

...few in the midnight sky, singing a quiet song..." No. 5 is also accompanied by a Lermontov quotation: "A storm rages, snow falls; but through the din of the wind, a faraway bell breaks in - it is an echo of a funeral."

Medtner's favorite genre, which he himself created, is the **skazka**, to which the unfortunate title of "fairy tale" has been given. Skazka, properly translated, is a folk tale, and in Russian folk tales, sparsely populated with monsters, have no fairies in them at all. Most critics have agreed that the best features of Medtner's compositional style are evident in these poetic miniatures. Written during different stages of the composer's life, the skazki paint pictures of autobiographical emotional upheavals, of folk heroes, maidenly melancholy, stern poetic legends of great deeds, the many moods of Nature, and the unrestrained spontaneity of the dance - all told with simplicity and wisdom, sometimes with a smile, sometimes with concealed sadness. The skazki are divided into ten cycles of two to six each.

The first two sets, Opus 8 and Opus 9, were composed in 1906. The characteristics that distinguish the later skazki are already present here: the contrasting images traceable to a common thematic basis, widely used contrapuntal devices, relatively simple piano textures. The C major skazka, Opus 9, No. 2 possesses a somewhat Spanish flavor, to which the G major No. 3 is a fragrant, bittersweet foil.

The two works of **Opus 14** are provided with programmatic titles. The first, "Ophelia's Song" was conceived as a violin piece, and part of an "Ophelia" cycle. This is not Shakespeare's tragic heroine, but rather an image of a simple maiden of an ancient legend. Medtner chose modest musical means for her portrayal - chorale-like texture, simple harmonic progressions and a plain repetitive rhythm all create the mood of quiet, elegiac sorrow.

The four skazki of **Opus 26** are among the most commonly performed. They are full of Medtner's special lyricism - not emotionally overt and pathetic, but rather intimate, restrained and undemonstrative. The first is appealingly melodic. An unhurried narrative describing a lovely image, this skazka stands out for its inner warmth and delightfully light-hearted mood.

The different cycles of skazki are arranged on various principles: internally - through thematic or tonal relationships, or externally - by contrasts in their imagery. The four programmatic skazki of **Opus 34** use sonata or symphony construction instead. Thus, the first piece plays the part of a Prologue that serves to introduce the leading ideas of the entire cycle, and can be considered as the first, the most active movement; the slow, lyrical second skazka and the flighty, fantastic scherzo are typical of the second and third symphony movements; the large-scale programmatic last skazka makes a fitting finale. Each skazka has a programmatic description: the elegiac thoughtful "Magic Violin" is first, the song-like second has an epigraph by Tyutchev, another great Russian poet: "Whatever we used to call our own has now left forever", the third entitles "A Forest Devil (but a kind and plaintive one)", and the majestic fourth is based on Pushkin's poem "The Poor Knight".

The cycle of **Opus 42** was the first composed outside Russia, later followed by Opus 48 and 51. Perhaps as a measure of Medtner's nostalgia, the nationalistic imagery is most pronounced in these works, more emphasis is placed on song and dance inspirations.

Medtner was especially busy as a performer between 1910 and 1915. During this period he completed his Sonata Opus 30 and the Three Pieces of Opus 31 for his own use. Opus 31 consists of: "Improvisation", "Marche funèbre" and skazka. The Marche funèbre is the most impressive of the three. Heinrich Neuhaus wrote of this work: "Only two pages of text, and yet how much intense expression, how much profound sorrow, that rejection of death that seizes us when a young and beautiful thing dies (this is why there is no moment of acceptance, like in Beethoven or Chopin)... The massive climax in the middle reminds me of a Beethovenian gesture at a sudden storm... For the power of its expression and its laconic compactness, I consider this March a masterpiece." The March is based on the octatonic scale, while the melodic line emphasizes the tritone. This lends the piece remarkable tension and eloquence.

In 1909 Medtner was offered the post of Professor of Piano at the Moscow Conservatory. With no affinity toward pedagogy, he worked there for only a year. During this year he wrote the **3 Noveletten, Opus 17**. Only the first Novelette is given a programmatic title - Daphnis and Chloé. The second paints a picture of a stately procession. The last, in the spirit of a ballade, tells an epic tale.

**The Arabesques, Opus 7**, of which Idyll is the first, were completed in 1905 and, thus, belong among Medtner's early compositions.

Medtner's 14 piano sonatas have been compared to a mountain range along which tower fourteen independent peaks. According to Taneyev, Medtner was born with an innate and perfect understanding of sonata form. So natural and flawless are his sonata constructions, that for years they were used to teach sonata form to the students of the Moscow Conservatory. Following the example of Liszt and Scriabin, Medtner gravitated toward one-movement sonata construction, which he used in Opus 30. The **A minor Sonata, Opus 30** of 1916 is the only mature sonata without a descriptive title. Reflecting the time of its composition, immediately preceding the revolutionary upheavals of 1917, the sonata's mood is turbulent and dramatic, but, nevertheless, optimistic. The intense clashes of the exposition and development lead to a joyously exultant climax which culminates, following a short recitative, in a stormy, impetuous coda in the major.

## Page 3 (biography of Igor Nikonovich)

**Igor Nikonovich** was born in 1935 in Tashkent (Uzbekistan) and began music lessons in 1942 in the music school of the Tashkent Conservatory under M. Polyanskaya. At the age of 14, he moved to Moscow where he continued his studies at the Music School of the Moscow Conservatory where his teacher was Ye. Nikolaeva and then in the Moscow Conservatory with Heinrich Neuhaus. His close communication with another great pianist, Vladimir Sofronitsky, made a tremendous impact on the creative career of the young pianist.

Nikonovich began to appear as a pianist in 1960 and since then has made numerous appearances in many cities of the ex-USSR. Recently he also performed in Italy and Germany. Since 1962 he has taught at the Russian Academy of Music. His basic repertoire includes works by Beethoven, Chopin, Schumann, Liszt, Rachmaninov and Prokofiev. Of special interest are his interpretations of Scriabin and Medtner. During the 1950s and 60s, Nikonovich worked as a researcher in the Scriabin Museum in Moscow and is presently a member of the Board of the Scriabin Foundation.

Russian critics have noted that his association with Sofronitsky can be observed in his prevailing deep, dense piano sonority, combination of free rubato with a restrained severity of playing and a striving for monumental and profound interpretations. In view of the fact that Sofronitsky's pianistic style developed under the influence of Medtner. Igor Nikonovich stands at the end of a significant continuity of Russian pianistic performance tradition and his performances of Medtner's music are uniquely authoritative.
