## 内页说明（德语）

### Bagatellen und Serenaden

> Wandelt sich rasch auch die Welt
> wie Wolkengestalten,
> alles Vollendete fällt
> heim zum Uralten.
>
> — Rainer Maria Rilke

Die Idee, Valentin Silvestrov als Pianisten aufzunehmen, hatte Manfred Eicher schon längere Zeit beschäftigt. Im Januar 2001, als in der Festburgkirche in Frankfurt am Main Kaggeno, gezimmte, die erste Silvestrov-Einspielung bei ECM, entstand, spielte der Komponist in den Pausen ein kurzes Klavierstück, *Hymne 2001*, das die Platte als Postscriptum abschließen sollte. Die Fortsetzung folgte im selben Jahr in Ferrara, wo das Rosamunde-Quartett Valentin Silvestrov ein Porträtkonzert widmete. Eine Uraufführung bildete den Abschluss des Abends: Als Zugabe spielte der Komponist seine gerade entstandenen Zwei Stücke (Benedictus – „Nacht" und Sanctus – „Morgen").

Und dann, in der Münchener Himmelfahrtskirche, während der Aufnahmen für die vorliegende CD, ergab es sich wie von selbst: Sehr früh am Morgen, noch bevor der Arbeitstag begann, aber auch abends nach den Aufnahmesitzungen, ging Silvestrov zum Flügel und schlug sehr behutsam die Tasten an. Die Musik, die aus dem Augenblick heraus zu entstehen schien, war mehr als ein Präludium, mehr auch als ein Postscriptum.

„Die Genres, die gemeinhin als klein bezeichnet werden, sind nur dann klein, wenn man sie kleinlich behandelt", schrieb der französische Historiker Charles-Nicolas Cochin 1780 über die so genannte Genremalerei.

## 内页说明（英语）

### Bagatelles and Serenades

> The world too changes swiftly
> Like ranks of clouds,
> and all creation topples
> home to the primordial.
>
> — Rainer Maria Rilke

The idea to document Valentin Silvestrov as a pianist had long been on producer Manfred Eicher's mind. Already in January 2001, during breaks in the making of Silvestrov's ECM debut in Frankfurt's Festeburg Church, he had recorded the composer playing a short piano piece, *Hymne 2001*, subsequently including it as a postscript to the leggiero, pescante album. The idea gained further impetus in Ferrara, also in 2001, when the Rosamunde Quartet gave an all-Silvestrov recital, the evening ending with the world premiere, as the composer played his newly composed *Two Pieces* (Benedictus – "Night" and "Morning") as an encore.

In February 2006 at Munich's Himmelfahrtskirche (Church of the Ascension), during recordings for the current CD, further documentation was to take place quite naturally. Early in the morning, before the day's work had begun, and also in the evenings after the sessions, Silvestrov would walk up to the piano and gently touch the keys. The music that emerged, seemingly taking shape in the moment, was more than a prelude or a post-script.

"The genres usually referred to as small are only small when treated in a small-minded way." Thus the French historian Charles-Nicolas Cochin, writing about so-called "genre paintings" in 1780. His words apply equally well to musical miniatures. For some they are a trial run or artistic laboratory, for others the heart of the creative process. Apparently both views apply to Silvestrov: He began with simple-sounding piano pieces that he later enriched with dynamic and rhythmatic nuances (e.g. *Mazurka*, 1954/1992, and *Enfantines Musical*, 1956/1993). Later he produced his early experimental works *Quattres pieces* and *Five Piano Pieces* (both 1960). But even his large-scale works reveal his experience with musical aphorisms, with subtle workmanship and fragmentary forms that are open-ended in some directions – hence his high regard for the genre of the "postlude".

Another impetus to write miniatures for various combinations of instruments came from a romantic tale that Silvestrov only learned about in 1998. On 17 September 1826 a wedding took place in Vienna. The Austrian painter Leopold Kupelwieser married Johanna von Lutz. The names of the bride and bridegroom hardly mean anything to us today, but that of the friend who supplied the music for the occasion is immortal: Franz Schubert. As was customary, Schubert played the entire evening, returning several times to a waltz that was never written down but survived by oral tradition within the family. It was not until 1943 that the piece was committed to paper – by Richard Strauss. The manuscript was published in facsimile by Universal Edition in Vienna and the pianist Paul Badura-Skoda in his history as the Kupelwieser Waltz (D.893 A. Anh. I, 14).

The part of this story that mainly interested Silvestrov was the phenomenon of oral tradition, which assures a piece a certain degree of anonymity. It struck a chord in the composer that has never stopped vibrating – namely, his belief that the language of music has no owner, that only the word itself has value, regardless of who speaks it or when it is spoken. As Osip Mandelstam wrote in *The Sounds of Ossian*: "The skald shall concoct a strange song / And declaim it as his own."

One immediate response to this tale was the piano cycle *Oral Music* (1998–9), followed by a growing multitude of waltzes, lullabies, nocturnes, postludes, serenades and so forth that the composer refers to as, quite generally, as *Bagatelles*. For a long time these pieces only existed in 'oral' versions; only later did Silvestrov begin the process of committing them to paper, or what he calls the "materialisation of the immaterial" – a process that merely codifies what has long been fixed in his memory and all his fingers. This same process applies, incidentally, to the whole of Silvestrov's output. Not only his chamber music but his symphonies and choral works, too, are composed, played and recorded at the piano. The first section of our new CD captures the composer 'playing' pieces that have not yet been set down in writing.

A bagatelle, the composer claims, arises from the momentary 'flash' of a motif, a cry, a snippet. It is thus a "moment music"? The association springs immediately to mind, and it is visceral rather than extrinsic. "Moments musicaux", to quote the musicologist Horst Weber, "was the term that a cagey publisher applied in 1828 to Schubert's op. 74 piano pieces (D.780). With this he lit on part of what happens in Schubert's music: namely, a moment is given duration." Weber continues: "Schubert thereby developed a new form of musical time, which emerges not so much from the linking of notes into discrete blocks, but rather, in a manner of speaking, from the sounds of the notes themselves."

Something similar happens in Silvestrov's music. For him, the most important thing is to capture the moment as such and to make it singable, without subjecting it to so-called motivic-thematic manipulation. It is no accident that he often uses rather monothematic concept: the "song without words" – i.e. a song that is always recognisable and can therefore be 'played' by any voice or 'sung' on any instrument. This applies not only to the Bagatelles but to a song that sometimes wanders from one combination of instruments to another, as happens with several pieces on our recording.

Like their classical forbears in the piano cycles of Beethoven, Schubert, and Schumann, Silvestrov's pieces, depending on their purpose, cluster together in 'colonies' or 'families' in which each is borne of the others and all are related. The bagatelles on our recording (except Nos. IV–VII and X–XIII) represent various 'families' congregated beneath a 'roof' tool for this one occasion. They form a sort of improvised cycle played in a single breath, a cycle whose basically ceaseless flow is framed by the repeat of the second piece. Yet what we hear is not improvisation in the strict sense and only seemingly 'oral music': everything has been fully crafted in the composer's mind down to the nthsmost detail.

This composed quality is not immediately audible. Rather than the wide variety of technical devices and timbral effects typical of piano music of the 20th century (and the beginning of the 21st), we are instead given a text that is surprisingly plain for all of its Silvestrovian frequent 'weak' And yet it is precisely this simplicity, this paucity of notes, that allows a variety of a completely different kind to unfold, a variety made up of minute dynamic agogic, tempo and rhythmic graduations. The living flow of the music is sped up or restrained by a prevailing sense of *rubato* (accelerando, ritenuto, a tempo and unexpected rests, fermatas, emphases). The dynamics are governed by the softest pianissimos (almost always using the una corda pedal) that seem to expand infinitely in the interior of the church. All of this makes the text multi-dimensional and defines what is known as the composer's "metaphorical style".

Here the listener is granted an opportunity to experience one of the composer's unique autographs, a sound-ideal with his characteristic weightless attack (a fist grip on an open space): his inimitable "maximum delicacy of touch" (to quote one of the most common expression marks in his scores). In this performance, rather than a sharply etched image, we are given a slightly unfocused, diaphanous image. Indirectly, metaphorically, we are reminded of the essence of music: melody. In this 'genre music' the bagatelles cease to be musical trinkets and become, as Silvestrov has it, simply put, "sublime insignificance". Yet universes may lurk within them like a World in a Grain of Sand / And a Heaven in a Wild Flower, / Hold infinity in the palm of your hand / And Eternity in an hour (William Blake, *Auguries of Innocence*, 1801–3).

Music as a "collection of resonances": as a response, a postlude to things already said, sung or sounded; this is the connecting thread that runs through Silvestrov's entire oeuvre. Recently this theme has been reflected on a series of tributes to his great predecessors – Bach, Mozart, Glinka, Schubert, Schumann, Chopin, Tchaikovsky, Schoenberg, Berg, Webern – and in his many dedications to relatives, friends and fellow-musicians. The result is a sort of reverie-ending that can easily be mistaken by analysts willing to listen to the composer's calm, quiet voice.

But before Silvestrov begins to speak he prefers to listen, as in the work he dedicated to his friend and colleague Arvo Pärt, appropriately titled *Two Dialogues with Afterword* (2001/2). Many 'interlocutors' have gathered together in this two-hundred-year-long'congers'. The most important are Franz Schubert and Richard Wagner. Their music forms the basis of the *Dialogues*: the above-mentioned Kupelwieser Waltz in the case of the former; (I. *Hochzeitswalzer* – Wedding Waltz) and the so-called *Theme in A flat major*, WWV 93, in the case of the latter (II. Postludium). Like the Kupelwieser tune, there is a romantic story behind Wagner's theme. This fragment, though never put to use, has come down to us in two manuscripts. The first, consisting of eight bars, was written on a leaf whose reverse side, we are told, "contains a sketch for scene 2 of the second act of Tristan, presumably dating from December 1858". The second dates from the period of *Parsifal* (1882).

Silvestrov himself avoids calling his versions an arrangement. But he "collects" (to paraphrase Mandelstam) a "starving song in his soul" and uses ellipses to distinguish his piece and the original when citing the names of the 'authors' and the dates of composition ('Schubert ... Silvestrov 1826... 2002, Wagner ... Silvestrov 1882 ... 2007'). In the 'afterword' that brings the cycle to a close (III. *Morgenserenade* – Morning Serenade) the composer again returns to the past, this time however to his own past: "...", V. Silvestrov 2002", enclosed in parentheses. Whether it refers to a particular piece of music is anyone's guess.

*Elegie* (2000–2002) commemorates the death of Silvestrov's long-time friend and colleague, the Ukrainian musicologist Ivan Karabits. The composition is based on a pencilled sketch from the notebook that Ivan Karabits took with him to hospital, thinking of his next work. Thus Silvestrov's note in the score, where the 'authors' are named in the same way as above: "Karabits ... Silvestrov". It too is structured as a dialogue, unfolding in an alternation between unbridled melody and static recitative until the music is suddenly interrupted by a bitter sigh from the solo violin.

*Abschiedsserenade* (Farewell Serenade, 2003), likewise dedicated to Ivan Karabits, consists of two movements. The first, set in the same tragic vein as *Elegie*, sounds like an introduction to the serenade proper, which is enshrouded in 'diaphanous sorrow' (A. Pushkin).

Manfred Eicher accidentally discovered *Stille Lieder* (Silent Songs) in the early 1980s. It was the first piece by Silvestrov he had ever heard. Without understanding Russian or knowing the name of the composer, he was held spellbound by the magic of the music. *Stille Lieder* was also performed when the two men first met in 1999.

The three-piece cycle *Stille Musik* (Silent Music, 2003) is a present to Manfred Eicher. The three miniatures in this cycle – the gossamer "Walzer des Augenblicks" (Waltz of the Hours), the melancholy "Abendserenade" (Evening Serenade) and the bright "Augenblicke der Serenade" (Moments of the Serenade) – were scored from bagatelles originally written for piano (I) and cello (II–III). All three sound like quiet recollections of the beginning of this fruitful collaboration.

The title *Der Bote* (The Messenger, 1996) is taken from the writings of Yakov Druskin, a scholar and thinker from St. Petersburg who combined the qualities of musician, musicologist, mathematician and, above all, philosopher. His "messenger" is an imaginary figure symbolizing a bond or dialogue between this world and the world beyond. The figure is depicted as a Mozartean melody ("as it enveloped in mist, dolcissimo, lontano, bright and sad") and an artless sonatina in 18th-century style. As always with Silvestrov's music, here too a classical theme is transformed in its inmost workings. Hardly do the messenger's melodies appear than they dissolve into finely calibrated scales of dynamic hues and free changes of tempo, surrounded by a flickering echo. ("Play the entire piece with the softest possible attack and a 'light' touch. Keep the lid of the instrument completely shut. Press the pedal immediately so that the preceding sonority reverberates.") In the orchestral version the work is framed by a quiet "sighing of the wind" – an effect much loved by the composer, and one that accompanies both the arrival and the departure of the "messenger".
