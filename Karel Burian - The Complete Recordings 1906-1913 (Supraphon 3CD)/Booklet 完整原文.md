## Karel Burian's life (English)

The Czech tenor **Karel Burian** (12 January 1870 – 25 September 1924) was mainly celebrated between 1902 and 1913, enthralling audiences at the major opera houses in Europe and America, including in Dresden, Munich, Zurich, Brussels, Vienna, Budapest, Bayreuth, Paris, London, New York and Chicago. A sought-after performer of heroic roles, he was invited to the Metropolitan Opera and highly appreciated by Gustav Mahler and Arturo Toscanini. When asked why he did not appear in German operas, Enrico Caruso answered: *"I can sing something, whereas Burian can sing everything."* Before Burian's illustrious career was prematurely interrupted by World War I, he was broken by ill-fated love and human spite.

Karel Burian was born in Rousinov, Bohemia. He attended the Akademisches Gymnasium in Prague, where he was referred to as smart, sociable, not tall, yet possessing a robust voice. The star pupil of his class, he went on to study law. Yet his true passion was music and singing. While taking voice lessons from Moritz Wallerstein, he began earning his living as a singer too. At the age of 21, Burian participated in an audition at the National Theatre in Prague, yet failed. Upon the advice of Kapellmeister Mořic Anger, he then tried his luck in Brno, where he was duly engaged.

Burian made his operatic debut, on 28 September 1891 as Jenik in *The Bartered Bride*, following which, a day later, he performed as Dalibor. Encouraged by the success he earned on the stage, after completing four semesters, he abandoned his law studies and began fully devoting to opera.

He learned new roles rapidly and easily, gaining experience in numerous places – Reval (Tallinn), Leipzig, Berlin, Cologne, Bremen, Aachen, Hanover and Hamburg. During these years of "apprenticeship", he appeared on stage next to the best singers of the time, including Demuth, Goetze, Kalisch, Knote and Niemann, maturing under the guidance of such superb conductors as Blech, Bülow, Mottl, Muck, Richter and Weingartner. Classically educated and possessing a good general knowledge, he was a sophisticated actor, placing high demands on himself. Owing to his being thoroughly trained, mastering a reliable technique and having an extraordinary voice, Burian sang without manifesting any traces of fatigue. Over the nine seasons between 1891 and 1900, he portrayed some 70 roles, among them Ottavio, Tamino, Max, Raoul, Arnoldo, Erik, Wilhelm Meister, Cellini, Lyonel, Manrico, Riccardo, Radames and Chevalier des Grieux. Ambitious by nature, he gravitated towards the Wagner repertoire.

Nine years after his operatic debut, and after previously having been rejected, in 1900 Karel Burian was engaged at the National Theatre in Prague. Bearing not the slightest grudge, he became one of the major singers during the first year of the opera company's fabled era under Karel Kovařovic. He appeared in 60 performances of opera productions and in four concerts, with his repertoire encompassing the parts of Tannhäuser, Lohengrin, Canio, Werther, the Prophet and Otello. Yet despite having gained acclaim abroad, the National Theatre clearly indicated that he would never enjoy popularity as great as Vladislav Florjanský, a true idol of the Prague ladies. Even though he was afforded the opportunity to sing Diavolo and Dalibor, Jeník and Jaroměř (Zdeněk Fibich's *The Fall of Arkun*), it by no means satisfied his ambition. With his patience running thin, Burian demonstrated his discontent by refusing to perform in the world premiere of Antonín Dvořák's *Rusalka*, in whose production he would only subsequently appear on two occasions. In the summer of 1901, he had had enough and left Prague.

Nevertheless, his departure – now the second – was a loss greater to the National Theatre than Burian himself. The 31-year-old tenor in full bloom received a much more attractive engagement, from the Royal Opera in Budapest.

Karel Burian made his debut as Tristan in the Hungarian premiere of the opera *Tristan und Isolde*, which earned him critical acclaim:

*"Mr. Burian performed Tristan with the utmost refinement, sheer chivalry and inner passion. In the grand scene with the hero fatally wounded, he masterfully rendered his feverishness, tremulous desire and crushing despair. He has proved to be a first-class Wagner singer."* (Pester Lloyd, Budapest 1901)

Later on, Burian accepted invitations from the National Theatre in Prague to perform Lohengrin and Dalibor, and, seeking to make amends to Antonín Dvořák, he sang the part of Bořivoj in the oratorio *Saint Ludmila*, conducted by Oskar Nedbal.

For ten years, from 1902 to 1912, he worked at the Königliches Opernhaus (Semperoper) in Dresden. Known as "Carl Burian", he was the company's feted, celebrated and revered first tenor. Under the baton of Ernst von Schuch, he dazzled in Wagner roles, as well as in the German premiere on 21 October 1902 of *Tosca*, next to the soprano Irene Abendroth, and in the world premiere on 9 December 1905 of *Salome*, with Marie Wittich in the title role. Moreover, he performed as a guest in Stuttgart, Nuremberg and Mannheim:

*"Wagner himself would have deem his portrayal of Siegfried utterly faultless. Although a Czech, German singers could learn from him."* (E. Détschey, *Tagespost*, Dresden 1906), "His Loge is the best I have ever seen!" (*Deutsche Wacht*, Dresden 1905), "The most magnificent voice in German opera!" (Ludwig Hartmann, Dresden 1906)

His debut at Covent Garden, on 3 May 1904, in the role of Tristan, opposite Josefine Reinl as Isolde, met with an enthusiastic response on the part of the English critics:

*"Mr. Burian performed Tristan with the utmost refinement... what more can one say? – the English critics were enthusiastic."*

During the six wartime seasons, which have been referred to in the Hungarian capital as the "Burian era", the tenor sang all his Wagner roles, as well as the parts of Faust, José, the Evangelist, Samson, Rodolfo and Canio. From the fateful year 1913 to the end of WWI in 1918, Burian gave more than 150 performances in Budapest. Adding insult to injury, in 1919 Budapest too turned against him, because he was a Czech.

The holder of three Kammersänger titles (Saxon, Württemberg and Anhalt), a Chevalier of the French Légion d'honneur and a recipient of other prestigious honours and accolades, Karel Burian retired to his manor in Senomaty, Bohemia, before reaching the age of 50. Amidst the tranquillity of the countryside, he spent his time translating the librettos of *Tristan und Isolde* and *Salome* into Czech, and the text of František Picka's opera *Reiner the Painter* into German. Together with the composer, pianist, teacher and ethnographer Jindřich Jindřich, he compiled a collection of folk songs. He also wrote his memoirs, which contain vivid recollections of Mahler and Toscanini. Although suffering from diabetes-related health problems, Burian had no intention of giving up, rejecting the idea that his illustrious global career was over: *"If I am to live, I must sing, if I am to sing, I must drink."* He retained his specific sense of humour. Yet while envy, hatred, addiction and misfortune continued to plague his life, he had fewer opportunities to overcome them by rising to new challenges.

Owing to his having been granted Hungarian citizenship before WWI, Burian could at least sing in Budapest: a day after his 50th birthday, he returned to the stage as Tannhäuser, following which, between January and June 1920, he would appear in another 30 or so performances. He also brought to Budapest his brother, the baritone Emil Burian.

Reputedly, after the war, he also accepted an invitation to sing in Dresden. Yet when he was in the dressing room just about to go on stage, he was delivered a note with the warning that if he did so he would be shot dead. He cancelled his performance.

In December 1921 in Budapest, Karel Burian celebrated three decades of pursuing artistic activities with his 40th portrayal of Tristan on the local stage. By the end of the season, he had given another 25 performances. In June 1922, he finally reconciled with Prague, when, upon the invitation of Otakar Ostrčil, who had succeeded Karel Kovařovic in the post of director, he appeared at the National Theatre on three occasions. The ovations with which the audience welcomed him so moved Burian that he found it difficult to sing. Whereas the critics mocked his advanced age, the spectators were truly impressed by his accounts of Tannhäuser and Dalibor. On 13 June 1922, he gave his 87th, and very last, performance at the National Theatre in Prague, where he had been assigned 17 roles.

Yet his physical and mental powers were waning. During his final season in Budapest, in the spring of 1923 he sang the parts of Canio and José, and gave two solid performances of Herod in *Salome*, but in his last (123rd) appearance in the role of Tristan he only took on Act 1. His last performance in Budapest was on 26 September 1923, portraying Siegmund in *Die Walküre*.

During a concert tour of Slovakia in 1924, Karel Burian was dealt a fateful blow. At a barbershop in Nitra, he sustained a cut. The wound was subsequently infected, allegedly with erysipelas, which caused sepsis. On 25 September 1924, Burian died before his time at his manor in Senomaty. He was 54 years of age. His death was marked by a black flag draped on the National Theatre in Prague. It is said that it was hung on the rear building and crumpled.

## Karel Burian's recording legacy

In June 1906, Karel Burian worked in the Gramophone & Typewriter studio. With an orchestra conducted by Karl Ludwig Bruno Seidler-Winkler, he recorded two *Werther* ([III/15] and [II/14]) and two *Lohengrin* ([I/4] and [I/5]) arias. Accompanied by the piano, he added Strauss's *Zweignung* (Dedication) ([III/26]) and two Czech folk songs, as arranged by Jindřich Jindřich ([III/1] and [III/2]). The pianist – the conductor Seidler-Winkler – was only mentioned in the case of the first recording, but it is highly likely he accompanied both Czech songs too. All these recordings may have been made in Berlin, yet the original labels of the records read "Dresden" – perhaps indicating more than the singer's home theatre. The studio technician was Max Hampe.

Karel Burian made his debut at the Metropolitan Opera on 30 November 1906 as Tannhäuser, next to Katherine Fleischer-Edel and conducted by Alfred Hertz. During his first season in New York, he also sang Lohengrin, beside Emma Eames; Siegfried, alongside Katherine Fleischer-Edel; and Tristan, with Johanna Gadski:

*"The performance was a beautiful one, and made a deep impression on the audience. Mr. Burian being the Tristan and making a strong and effective figure, his singing was uncommonly good."* (New York Times, New York 1909)

On 22 January 1907, Burian excelled at the Met as Herod in rare production of *Salome*, featuring Olive Fremstad in the title role and conducted by Alfred Hertz. In the spring of 1907 in Paris, he appeared in the legendary production of *Salome* under the baton of Richard Strauss, which also starred Emmy Destinn and Fitz Feinhals. His performance was lauded by the critics as follows:

*"Burian's Herod is truly impressive, like a painting by Francisco Goya."* (Louis Schneider, *Gill Blas*, Paris 1907) "Mr. Burian's account of Herod is one of the most profound, most sophisticated, most intellectual and most comprehensive character studies ever seen on an opera stage." "Burian is a phenomenal singer-actor." (The Sun, London 1907)

Despite his enormous global success, The Czech Prague did not seem to be overly impressed. Therefore, in the spring of 1907, Burian performed in this town at the New German Theatre (Neues deutsches Theater) in the title role in *Siegfried* and as Siegfried in *Götterdämmerung*. Remaining a Czech patriot, he soon sang in the Viennese premiere of Dvořák's cantata *The Spectre's Bride*.

At the beginning of August 1907, Burian and another Czech soloist of the Semperoper in Dresden, Bedřich Plaške (Friedrich Plaschke), recorded for Gramophone & Typewriter four duets (in German) from *Götterdämmerung* ([IV/10]), *La muette de Portici* ([II/8]), *La forza del destino* ([II/9]) and *La bohème* ([II/18]). Burian also made two recordings (in Czech and German) of Dalibor's introductory aria, yet neither of them was released ([I/15], [I/16]). The orchestra was again conducted by Karl Ludwig Bruno Seidler-Winkler, and again it is not clear whether the recording took place in Berlin or Dresden.

During his guest appearances from November 1908 to January 1909 at the Hofoper in Vienna, Burian requested that he perform the title role in Smetana's *Dalibor*. The local audience were enthused by his portrayals of Tannhäuser, Walter Stolzing and Siegfried:

*"Burian performed the young Siegfried as a full-blooded hero, teeming with vitality and strength. From the first to the final tone, his voice sounded so vigorous, round, vibrant and brilliant that it seemed to be simply superhuman." (Neues Wiener Journal, Vienna 1912)*

In February 1908, he returned to the Met to give 15 performances. On 20 March, he sang Beethoven's *Florestan* (without having an understudy), conducted by Gustav Mahler. He concluded his second American season in Chicago. Furthermore, in 1908 he was granted the greatest honour as a heldentenor: Richard Wagner's son Siegfried invited Burian to the Bayreuther Festspiele to portray Parsifal on seven occasions, conducted by Michael Balling and Karl Muck.

In the first half of September 1908, he embarked upon yet another Gramophone & Typewriter studio project, which took place in Dresden. To piano accompaniment, Burian first recorded songs by Novotný ([III/13]), Nedbal ([III/7]), Malát ([III/16]) and Tregler ([III/17]), and two Czech folk songs ([III/3], [III/4]). Afterwards, accompanied by an orchestra, he separately recorded two duets from *La bohème* with Minnie Nast ([II/17], [II/19]), Novotný's song *Jako do skoku* ([I/22]), as well as the romance from *Fra Diavolo* with Erika Wedekind ([II/6]). The duets were sung in German. The recording engineer was again Max Hampe, the pianist is unknown.

The National Theatre in Prague only started to give due recognition to Karel Burian after his third season at the Metropolitan Opera, during the course of which he gave six performances of Tannhäuser, four of Siegfried and three of Parsifal: in the summer of 1909 he was allowed to launch a Smetana cycle as Dalibor and Lukáš, and he also sang in Prague the roles of Kozina, José, Tannhäuser, Lohengrin, Werther and Canio. Moreover, Burian returned to the Neues deutsches Theater, to perform in a production of *Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg*. His portrayal of Stolzing met with a "hurricane of enthusiasm". As regards the rebukes as to why as a Czech he was singing in Prague at a German theatre, Burian had responded previously: *"Arts do not know nationality."*

The 1909/10 season at the Met was truly extraordinary for Karel Burian: he appeared at 26 opera evenings, which besides Manhattan were held in Brooklyn, in Philadelphia, Baltimore and Boston. He sang in all four parts of Wagner's *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, including two performances of the complete tetralogy. Arturo Toscanini requested Burian for the adaptation of *Tristan and Isolde* he conducted. From 27 November 1909, Burian sang under the baton of Toscanini in all 18 reruns. Burian also appeared in the anniversary 100th performance of the production conducted by Toscanini at the Met on 8 December 1909, featuring Lillian Nordica, Louise Homer, Pasquale Amato and Robert Blass. The self-same production was subsequently presented in Paris, with Olive Fremstad cast as Isolde.

After more engagements in London, in late August and early September 1910 Karel Burian was busy in the Gramophone Company's studio in Prague. The recording engineer was Max Hampe, with Jindřich Jindřich probably providing all the piano accompaniments. Of the 16 recordings made, 13 Czech and one German have survived. Two of them are folk ([III/5], [III/6]) and the rest art songs, written by Vymětal ([III/8]), Picka ([III/10], [III/11], [III/12]), Novotný ([III/14], [III/15]), Tregler ([III/18], [III/19]), and Jindřich ([III/20], [III/21]), while of the three pieces sung in German (including variants of Jindřich's and Tregler's songs) only Strauss's *Zueignung* was released ([III/27]). The last of the series was Edwards's ironic couplet *Můj koleg' Caruso* (My Cousin Caruso), set to Burian's text ([III/28]). In the cases of the recordings of the songs by Jindřich and Tregler translated into German, only the matrix numbers have survived.

During the course of eight sessions between April and July 1911, Karel Burian made some 30 recordings from his German, French, Italian and Russian operatic repertoire. Within the first series, from 7 to 11 April in Prague, he sang in Czech translation excerpts from *Tannhäuser* ([I/1]), *Lohengrin* ([I/6]) and *Dalibor* ([I/20]), supervised by the recording engineer Franz Hampe. The next session was held under the recording engineer Fred W. Gaisberg, therefore one of the discographers is not certain whether it took place in Prague or in Berlin, yet the former is more likely to have been the case, it has been noted that over half (17) of the recordings were made in Czech, even when it came to Italian, French, Russian and, in part, German operas. The five recordings made on 25 and 27 June 1911 feature arias in Czech – from *The Dogheads* ([I/27]) and *Eugene Onegin* ([II/10]) – and German – from *Götterdämmerung* ([I/11], [I/12]) and *Der Evangeliman* ([II/12]). After the recording engineer Fred W. Gaisberg was joined by Edmund J. Pears, on 1 and 2 July 1911 recordings were made of arias of Dimitrij ([II/11]) and Dalibor ([II/17]), in Czech, of Lohengrin ([I/2], [V/3]) in Czech and German, and arias of Siegmund ([I/7]), Tristan ([I/13]) and Canio ([II/21]) in German. Finally, during the sessions on 6 and 10 July 1911, recordings were made under Gaisberg and Pears from scenes from *Pagliacci* ([II/20]), *Fra Diavolo* ([II/7], [II/13]), Werther ([II/13]) and *Der Freischütz* ([II/1], [II/2]), in Czech, and from *Der Freischütz* ([II/3], [II/4]) in German. The series concluded with a recording of Fibich's *Tragedy* ([III/22]) with piano accompaniment.

On 7 July 1911, other songs were recorded with piano: in Czech and German – by Neumann ([III/23], [III/24], [III/25]) and by Picka, to Burian's text ([III/9]). The final recordings were those of two parts of Mahler's *Revege*, which, however, were not released and have not survived (Burian would then sing *Reveige* at his sixth concert at the Met).

Karel Burian also made six recordings for the French label Pathé, which feature different versions of arias from *Die Walküre* ([I/8]), Dalibor ([I/18] [I/19]), *Der Freischütz* ([II/5]), Werther ([II/16]) and *Pagliacci* ([II/22]), with those from Dalibor performed in Czech and the rest in German. The place and time of the recording were originally thought to have been connected with Burian's stay in Paris in 1907, yet according to later findings it is much more likely that they were made in Vienna, in either the spring or autumn of 1912. The discs are extraordinary due not only to the vertical belting with texts referring to Karel Burian's expected receipt of the title "Kaiserlicher königlicher Kammersänger" (imperial-royal chamber singer). For the occasion, Pathé even prepared matrices with Burian's name, intended for two Czech waltz songs (53926, 53927). Yet, owing to scheming against him, Burian would ultimately not receive the title, and the songs were recorded by an unknown tenor. During Burian's lifetime, two fakes bearing his name were made.

Burian's very last release is deemed to be the one and only he made for Parlophone, featuring his account of a Siegmund aria from *Die Walküre* ([I/9]), recorded in Vienna in either the spring of or September 1912, or, more likely, 1913.

His global career made it virtually impossible for Karel Burian to lead a family life. He was married to the Czech singer Františka Jelinková, whom he knew from Berlin and Cologne. Both of them were also engaged in Hannover. Moreover, he performed in Prague in productions of *Tannhäuser*, Lohengrin, Le Prophète, Dalibor, *The Bartered Bride*, Werther, Carmen, Otello and *The Fall of Arkun*. Even though their marriage would later fall to pieces, his wife refused to divorce. One of the other two women whom Burian loved and who inspired him, Dorys (Dorothy), died prematurely of typhoid. Ill-fated too was the relationship with Elsa, whose husband not only rejected the idea of divorce, but also was instrumental in destroying Burian's career. The enormous psychological pressure ruined the singer's voice, whereupon he had to cancel his performance in Tannhäuser at the National Theatre in Prague, which in turn gave rise to extreme malice against him. The theatre's management duly sued Burian, seeking compensation. Another lawsuit, lodged by Elsa's husband, resulted in the end of Burian's engagement in Dresden. A third legal action was instituted by the Semperoper for breach of contract.

When, on 6 February 1913, within his seventh season at the Metropolitan Opera, Burian gave his sixteenth performance of Siegmund in *Die Walküre*, he had not the slightest inkling that it would be his very last stage appearance there. It was his 133rd performance at the Met. His Muse Elsa was seriously ill and unexpectedly passed away. In the wake of her death, Burian cancelled all his scheduled performances once the season ended the following May. The Dreseden court's verdict finding Burian guilty of "adultery" and subsequently withdrew from the contract with the singer.

Although the news of the legal proceedings did not reach the Austro-Hungarian Empire (Vienna, Budapest and Prague) after his final portrayal of Parsifal in London (1914) Burian, not being allowed to travel freely, had to turn down an invitation to St. Petersburg. The rest of his career fell victim to World War I. Burian could sing in Prague, but only at the Neues deutsches Theater, in the spring of 1915, he performed in Tristan, Tannhäuser, Parsifal, Canio and Siegfried in Götterdämmerung, singer, against whom a warrant had been issued, was never addressed again by any record company. And once the scheming of the Prague musical circles reached Vienna, the only stages left for Burian were those at home – in Olomouc, Ostrava, Brno, Plzeň – and in Budapest.

## Karel Burian's recorded legacy (continued)

Fate continued to be unkind to Burian even after his death. When in 1991 Supraphon planned to re-release his then known 70 gramophone records, the tapes with copies of the recordings mysteriously disappeared and have yet to resurface. In the meantime, the collection has been sold out. It would take almost three decades to put the unique set together again – from Czech archives and collections in the UK, Germany, Switzerland and the USA. These efforts were most vehemently supported by the music writer Jiří Černý.

When it comes to the number of recordings, Karel Burian's discography amounts to one third of those made by Emmy Destinn and one fifth of Otakar Mařák's recordings. Nevertheless, Burian's international triumphs, which no other Czech tenor has attained on such a scale, have never been forgotten. During the singer's lifetime, several chapters of his memoirs, which were bound together into the volume titled *Z mých pamětí* (My Memoirs, 1913), were published in magazines. J. V. Hradčanský privately issued in Český Brod a compilation of Burian's humorous letters in verse (1916). Ten years after the artist's death, Josef Bartoš published in Rakovník a book of reminiscences (1934). Three decades after Burian's passing, the Prague Spring festival attached the title of its vocal competition to the names of Emmy Destinn and Karel Burian (1954).

Re-editions of Burian's recordings began in 1959, when the Supraphon Gramophone Club released an LP featuring his joint recital with Emmy Destinn (DV 5558). To mark the 50th anniversary of the singer's death in 1974, A. Novotný prepared for Supraphon two independent LPs containing Burian recitals (SU 0121510 and SU 0121579), while other recital LPs were released in the UK (Rubini GV35), Austria (Preiser CO306) and Canada (Rococo 5338 and 5379). Individual recordings appeared within other re-issues, some of them on CD. The most frequently re-released recordings were Burian's performances of Tristan's monologue ([I/13] 7 times) and the Evangelist's song ([II/12] 5 times).

Scholars in various countries have written a number of specialist papers and essays about Burian in the new millennium too. At the Liszt Music Academy in Budapest, Ferenc János Szabó's extensive doctoral thesis was titled *Karel Burian és Magyarország* (2011). Burian's revelatory biography served as the basis for Roman Hartl's book about significant figures from Burian's Native Region (2013). At Palacký University in Olomouc, Petr Vengláf focused on Burian in his BA dissertation *The Career of the Tenor Karel Burian in the Czech Lands* (2019).

The very first list of Karel Burian's records (26) was contained in the book *Pamětní bratří Burianů* (Memorial to the Burian Brothers) by his nephew E. F. Burian (1929). Six records were described in detail by Mirko Očadlik in his *Smetana Discography* (1939). Thirteen of Burian's records were mentioned by Roberto Bauer in *The New Catalogue of Historical Records*, published in London (1947). Paul Wilhelm wrote an account of 51 Burian records for the magazine *Record News* (1960). Thirty-five opera arias sung by Burian were highlighted by J. Dostal and T. Kaufmann in Rodolfo Celletti's book *Le grandi voci* (1964). The complete discography of 70 original commercial releases was drawn up by collectors Dennis Brew and Jiří Sova for a Burian biography, penned by James F. E. Dennis for the renowned English magazine *The Record Collector* (1969). A number of details – the dates and places – pertaining to Burian's records were sought and prepared in manuscripts for other collectors and biographers by Alan Kelly (69 records; 2002), Rainer Lotz (67 records; 2010) and Christian Zwarg (70 records; 2010). All the available data were meticulously summed up into a most complete and detailed discography of released and not released items by Ferenc János Szabó (2011).

The fact that the surviving 72 Karel Burian gramophone recordings were made during the relatively short period between 1906 and 1913, when the singer was at the height of his powers, has made it possible to divide the recordings spread over three CDs into thematic, repertoire-based, wholes. We refer to the individual recordings below directly by their placement within this complete set: the Roman numerals in the square brackets mean the ordinal numbers of the CDs, while the Arabic numerals after slashes are the ordinal numbers of the tracks.

## Technical note

Technical and aesthetic requirements for reconstruction of old shellac records have advanced to such an extent that for this edition it was not possible to use any of the previous transfers, nor was it feasible to use any of the 64 copies from the private collection of Jiřího Sovy (Praha, CR). Sve sbírky grafonových desek a jejich početné varianty dal nové k dispozici Milaš Guth (Kladno, CR) a Jan Králík (Lázně Toušeň, CR). Z dalších českých zdrojů byly použity snímky z Archivu města Ústí nad Labem (Ústí nad Labem, CR), z fonotéky Českého muzea hudby (Praha, CR) a z Českého rozhlasu (Praha, CR), where archival and private records were transferred by Miloslav Turek (Praha, CR).

Precious, otherwise unavailable recordings were selflessly provided by collectors from abroad – Richard A. Copeman (London, UK), Willy Kyburz-Lechmann (Basel, Switzerland), Vivian A. Liff (Port Erin, Isle of Man), Marc Matzner (North Hollywood, California, USA), William Roesch (Hope, Idaho, USA), Michael Seil (Notzingen, Germany) and Axel Weggen (Berlin, Germany).

Variants with the best preserved sound were selected from all these sources. During the course of their digital reconstruction, all the recordings were modified in terms of intonation in line with the original tuning, including three recordings that were deliberately transposed a semitone lower back at the time when they were made ([III/8], [II/15], [II/16]). In the cases of suitable duplicates, new sonically ideal wholes were created. Only point defects were removed from the audio tracks. All the original recordings were consistently respected with a view to retaining the full width of their frequency spectrum.

Jan Králík
