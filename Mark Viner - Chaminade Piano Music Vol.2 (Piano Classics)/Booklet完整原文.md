### Cécile Chaminade

Cécile Chaminade was born in Paris on the 8th August 1857. Her interest in music was first kindled by her mother, from whom she received preliminary instruction, before pursuing studies under Félix Le Couppey (1811-1887) for piano and Benjamin Godard (1849-1895) for composition and at the age of eight, she played some of her compositions to Georges Bizet (1838-1875) who was most impressed by her talent, dubbing her 'little Mozart'. All this was pursued, however, on a private basis due to her father's disapproval of a musical education being undertaken by a young lady of his daughter's social standing and it was not until the age of eighteen that she gave her first concert. It was not long, however, before she began to make a serious impression on the Paris music scene, undertaking tours of her native France while her music steadily gained in popularity, garnering, from Ambroise Thomas (1811-1896) the famous epithet, "This is not a woman who composes, but a composer who is a woman". In 1892 she made her début in England where her music was already immensely popular and one of her biggest fans, none other than Queen Victoria (1819-1901), invited her to Windsor Castle. She made many subsequent return tours and in 1897 was awarded the Jubilee Medal from the Queen. In 1901 she married the Marseilles music publisher, Louis-Mathieu Carbonel (1842-1907), a man some years her senior and the union was rumoured to be one of convenience. After his death in 1907 she never remarried. She incidentally never published anything with her husband, instead maintaining a close and productive relationship with Enoch & Cie. who continue to issue her music to this day. In 1908 she toured the USA where her populanty was immense and such that around two hundred Chaminade Clubs sprung up. By 1913 she was admitted to the Légion d'honneur: the highest order of merit granted to French civilians and a first for a female composer. The outbreak of the First World War, however, witnessed a decline in her compositional activity, preferring instead to nurse wounded soldiers from the front line while her remaining years were spent in increasing isolation and debilitation. A medical complication in 1938 led to her left foot being amputated – something her family ascribed to her 'strict' diet: she was a vegetarian – quite unheard of in France at the time. Her refusal to use a wheelchair left her bedridden and the outbreak of the Second World War and subsequent occupation of France brought further difficulties when she lost all royalties from her Jewish publisher, which was liquidated by the nazis. She died in Monte Carlo on the 18th April 1944 at the age of eighty-six.

While Chaminade is best remembered today as a composer of 'salon' music, a casual perusal through her catalogue reveals that she had her ambitions: among the familiar salon pieces which brought her fame and fortune we find piano works on a more ambitious scale viz. the Sonata, Op.21 (1895); large-scale balletic, chamber, operatic, orchestral and vocal works. By around the turn of the 20th century, however, such large scale 'serious' works appear few and far between and the offerings are, more often than not, of the salon variety. The reasons why her compositional output followed such a trajectory remain unclear and while some have attributed the repression of Chaminade's more serious compositional aspirations to the misogyny of the time, let us not forget that not only does it take a certain type of composer to write 'salon' music of such elevated quality – not to mention this well – but it was immensely lucrative and a genre in which she arguably remains unsurpassed to this day. Among the most enduring creations we must count the ravishing and still ubiquitous *Automne* from the 6 *Etudes de concert*, Op.35 (1896) which once graced every other music desk either side of the Atlantic and beyond: the *Pas des écharpes, 3e air de ballet*, Op.37 (1887) – an extract from her ballet *Callirhoé*, of the same opus number; the entrancing *Pierrette, air de ballet*, Op.41 (1889); the beguiling *Les Sylvains*, Op.60 (1892) and the splendid *Thème varié*, Op.89 (1898).¹

### L'Ondine, Op.101

Chaminade's *L'Ondine*, Op.101 first appeared in 1900, some eight years before Maurice Ravel's (1875-1937) flickering and sinister personification of the aforementioned water nymph in his *Gaspard de la nuit* (1908) and thirteen years before Claude Debussy (1862-1918) portrayed the same in the eighth number of the *Préludes II* (1913). As one could imagine, Chaminade's conception couldn't be further from either, its lithe contours and naive harmonic scheme immediately redolent of playfulness, innocence and purity. In the key of E flat major and marked *allegretto con moto*, a gracious opening theme sets the scene and is fortified on repetition with bass octaves. A second theme is stated in chords high in the treble before being reiterated amid whirling eddies of notes. All at once, a series of powerful gestures in the bass signal Poseidon brandishing his trident, calling his nereids to attention. Ondine's theme returns, making a surprise second entry in G flat major before an episode, redolent of a shoal of nereids, draws to the second theme amid the flashing of scales and flickering of fins. The previous course of events follows suit before Poseidon's rebuke returns, evermore powerful. Ondine's gentle theme emerges a final time and Poseidon, now placated, is ushered away by a flurry of our aquatic heroine's brethren.

### Pas des écharpes, 3me air de ballet, Op.37

Chaminade's ballet *Callirhoé*, Op.37 was composed in 1887 and first staged at the Grand Théâtre de Marseille on the 16th March 1888 before being published the following year. Of its twenty-one movements, six were arranged for the piano and four for orchestra. Many of the ballet's numbers proved immensely popular and the *Pas des écharpes* – (Scarf dance) remains one of her most popular pieces to this day. Given the piece's popularity, it was issued in a plethora of different editions of varying and sometimes dubious authenticity on either side of the Atlantic, some consisting entirely of the *Pas des écharpes* and others comprising, as a middle section, the *Pas du voile* – (Veil dance) which forms the following movement in the ballet. Some consist of both but with the latter making a full repetition within. Given the relative proliferation of such works it can be difficult to ascertain precisely which version/s of the piece was/were sanctioned by the composer and, as such, the present writer has used the Enoch & Cie. edition as a source which comprises the *Pas du voile* which, without repetition, makes for a more balanced and varied conception. The opening *Pas des écharpes* is a charming and straightforward dance in three and in the key of A flat major marked *mouvement modéré de Valse*, one minute coy and the next brazen. The ensuing *Pas de voile*, on the other hand, lithe and sensuous, is in C sharp minor and marked *andantino*. Reaching an impassioned climax, it gives way to a few playful reiterations of its theme before a brief quasi-cadenza dissipates and signals the return of the *Pas des écharpes*.

### Six pièces humoristiques, Op.87

No.1 Réveil
No.2 Sous bois
No.3 Inquiétude
No.4 Autrefois
No.5 Consolation
No.6 Norwégienne

The *Six pièces humoristiques*, Op.87 appeared in 1897 and comprise some of Chaminade's very best music. The opening piece, entitled *Réveil* – (Awakening), is in D major and provides the perfect curtain-raiser; marked *allegro*, its resolute nature is punctuated by sporadic moments of lethargy reminding us of the humorous nature of this collection of pieces. A more worrisome central section in the relative minor is soon dispelled by the reprise and the close leads one to wonder whether the subject of this little piece indeed awoke or fell back into a slumber. The second piece, in G major and marked *allegretto*, is entitled *Sous bois* – (Beneath the boughs) and depicts an idyllic woodland scene in which a gracious opening theme is introduced before a fragment of it is expanded upon in a more urgent middle section. The urgency dissipates at the reprise and the piece closes with the resigned simplicity with which it began. The third piece of the set, entitled *Inquiétude* – (Unrest), is in A flat major and marked *allegro vivo agitato*. It opens with a breathless little tune which chases its tail for three whole pages of anxious entreaty before landing squarely on a C minor chord, signalling the middle section; at once more resolute in character, it gives way to wisps of the opening melody in search of the home key. The reprise ensues and the piece closes amid a host of placating iterations of a fragment of the opening theme, quelling the pervading anxiety, before drifting into oblivion on a skyward wisp of arpeggio. The fourth piece is, undeniably, the jewel of the set. Entitled *Autrefois* – (Yesteryear), it achieved a brief vogue under the velveteen advocacy of Shura Cherkassky (1909-1995) whose pioneering reading of it is in a league of its own.² It is obvious from the outset that the piece is a nostalgic evocation of *les clavecinistes*; a depiction of the style libre and the style sévère of harpsichord playing of the previous century, and falls into that intriguing category of 'music about music' which seemed to fascinate so many French composers over the centuries. In the key of A minor and marked *andante*, the piece opens with a series of improvisatory realised ornaments and flourishes, redolent of the silvery timbre of the harpsichord, sounding for all the world like a free prelude of Louis Couperin (1626-1661) albeit through a distorted lens. A more fulsome theme is then stated in the relative major before the opening theme returns. A lively middle section then ensues in the tonic major, with all the sparkle and panache of a sonata of Domenico Scarlatti (1685-1757) before leading to the reprise which draws the piece to a hushed and spectral close. The fifth number of the set, in B major, is also a fine piece and well worthy of attention. Entitled *Consolation* and marked *andante con moto*, an expansive and benevolent melody unfolds over a buoyant left hand accompaniment and, with its shared metre and key sequences, bears a striking resemblance to the slightly later *Thème varié*, Op.89 (1898). A hushed middle section in the tonic minor, with its dappled shades and pastel hues, reveals an affinity with Gabriel Fauré (1845-1924) before settling on the dominant and gliding into the reprise. An echo of the melody of the middle section is heard in the major before a sequence of hushed arpeggios closes the piece with benign solemnity. The sixth and final piece of the set, entitled *Norwégienne*, is in B minor and, as its title suggests, is of a distinctly Scandinavian flavour. Marked *allegro*, a strident dotted rhythm underpins much of the piece and only by the final page does its Norse frost begin to thaw in the tonic major before a filigree glissando lands on the blinding light of a B major chord.

### Guitare, caprice, Op.32

Chaminade's *Guitare, caprice*, Op.32 was published in 1885 and dedicated to the musicologist, Albert Lavignac (1846-1916) who, albeit a minor composer, is best remembered today for his raucous *Galop-Marche à huit mains* (c.1860). This piece, marked *allegretto* and in the key of E minor is, once again, an example of 'music about music' in its imitation of strummed chords and tremolo technique. A languorous middle section in the tonic major, punctuated by lively outbursts, affirms the piece's Spanish origins before the reprise, leading to a brief coda which plays on ideas from both the opening theme and the middle section.

### Danse créole (2e havanaise), Op.94

The *Danse Créole*, Op.94 was first published in 1898 with a dedication to the composer's sixteen-year-old niece, Antoinette Chaminade (1882-1971), and subtitled (2e havanaise) – the first being the *havanaise*, Op.57 (1891). In the key of G flat major and marked *allegretto*, its opening theme, languid, capricious and with a touch of mischief, tiptoes over a left hand habanera. A more virulent theme in B flat minor enters and is stated at a powerful fortissimo before petering out for the ensuing reprise where the piece is rounded off with a flash of light.

### Au pays dévasté, Op.155

*Au pays dévasté*, Op.155 – (In the devastated country) is arguably one of Chaminade's most profound conceptions, and is certainly the more serious work of her later compositional period. It was published in 1919 in the wake of World War I, a period during which she published nothing save four pieces on the cusp of the war in 1914. Much of the inspiration of this beautiful work must have been the result of her involvement in nursing wounded soldiers from the frontline and it is little coincidence that the *Berceuse du petit soldat blessé*, Op.156 (1919) – (Berceuse of the little injured soldier) appeared just after. In the key of G minor, it opens with a mournful theme which is presented in a host of different keys before an impassioned interlude gives way to a series of rhetorical gestures leading to a second theme in the relative major. A truncated entry of the previous series of rhetorical gestures leads to a false reprise in the dominant before the reprise proper ensues in the home key, unexpectedly building in intensity and leading to the second theme now stated in the tonic major. Reaching A grandiose climax, it peters out and gives way to a final, tragic iteration of the opening theme deep in the bass, casting a long shadow over the work.

### Six Etudes de concert

Etude romantique, Op.132
Etude pathétique, Op.124
Etude mélodique, Op.118
Etude humoristique, Op.138
Etude symphonique, Op.28
Etude scolastique, Op.139

These *Six Etudes de concert*, though initially published independently, were later issued together with the same cover art as the earlier set of *Six Etudes de concert*, Op.35 (1886) as a 'second recueil' – (second book) in the above order which has been adhered to for this recording. Whether this union and order was at the behest of the publisher remains to be seen though we might comfortably assume that Chaminade gave her assent. The first étude of the set, entitled *Etude romantique*, Op.132 and marked *allegro vivo*, is in G flat major and was first published in 1909 bearing a dedication to the Russian-born American pianist, Ossip Gabrilowitsch (1878-1936). It is one of the very best of all Chaminade's études and demonstrates her ample ability to breath fire when she wants to. The opening section presents a series of coruscating ideas which soar inexorably across the keys. A more reflective theme in A major, not dissimilar to an idea visited in the opening section, then enters and develops, in breathless search of a key in which to settle; a false reprise in G major witnesses the opening theme struggle through an ever-tightening straightjacket of keys when, in a desperate bid for freedom, it takes flight on wings of flame, the second theme now resplendent in the key of D flat major. The reprise proper now ensues before short-circuiting to the second idea now in the home key, marked *appassionato* and at a blistering fff. Giving way to the previous sequence of ideas explored in the opening section, the étude closes with an aerial fluttering of wings prefixing a triumphant chordal ending. All in all, it is a work of bewitching effulgence and its neglect is as inexplicable as it is un-warranted. The *Etude pathétique*, Op.124, which forms the second number of the book, fares no less well. First published in 1906 and marked *presto, energico, appassionato*, it is a blistering concert étude of pulverising verve in B minor. The time signature of 6/8, together with virtuosic gestures such as frequent hand crossing, put one in mind of a tarantella and, indeed, no respite is to be had until the middle section, marked *andante* in the relative major, which explores fragments of the opening theme. A short-lived interjection to restore the movement of the tarantella is placated by a more luxuriant reiteration of the theme of the middle section before a brief reprise witnesses the single-minded drive of the tarantella left unchecked as it soars headlong to a grandiose conclusion. The *Etude mélodique*, Op.118 is the next étude in the series and also first appeared in 1906. Marked *con moto appassionato* and in the key of G flat major, the étude is effectively in loose rondo form whereby a main section is interpolated by two contrasting ideas. The opening section features a right hand melody underpinned by eddying triplets and left hand octaves and the writing is beautifully pianistic. A brief interjection in F sharp minor leads back to the music of the opening section, its melodic outline further impassioned as it remains anchored in the key of the latter section. The opening section now resumes in the home key when, all at once, a more strident entry in F sharp minor leads to an impassioned episode in A major. A condensed version of the earlier F sharp minor section then gives way to a final reprise of the opening section which closes the étude with quiet simplicity. The next étude is the *Etude humoristique*, Op.138 which was first published in 1910. Marked *vivo* and in the key of F sharp minor, the opening section consists of mercurial quicksilver figurations which flash from the top to the bottom of the keyboard. A contrasting idea, marked *pesante molto sostenuto*, sets an uncompromising stance and is answered by a passage of sparkling jocundity before the former idea is reiterating and the quicksilver filigree of the opening section resumes. The middle section then ensues in the relative major: full of capricious wit and bombast, it enters an exchange

A final statement of the theme from the middle section is iterated in bolder language before a will-o'-the-wisp coda ushers the étude to its close. The *Etude symphonique*, Op.28 forms the fifth number in the book and was first published in 1890 with a dedication to none other than the famed pianist, composer and former Prime Minister of Poland, Ignacy Jan Paderewski (1860-1941). In the key of B flat major, it opens with a suave and coaxing melody floating over a shimmering left hand accompaniment. The urgency of the rhetoric intensifies before it is reiterated in octaves over a more fulsome accompaniment, its passionate languor giving way to a middle section of greater urgency marked *allegro moderato*. The reprise then ensues in octaves before a brief revisitation of the middle section draws the étude to its close. The sixth and final number in the book is taken by the *Etude scolastique*, Op.139 which was first published in 1910. What unruly classroom inspired the frolics and mischief of this étude, one wonders. In the key of A minor and marked *allegro energico*, the opening section presents an obsessive madcap scherzo held within a steely, disciplined grip. An expansive middle section in the relative major, curiously prophetic of William Walton (1902-1983) witnesses a sober theme stated in chords before being clad in swirling arpeggios. The madcap scherzo then returns only to be cut short by a grandiose return of the theme of the middle section in the tonic major before the scherzo, now also in the major, wins the upper hand.

### Sérénade, Op.29

Chaminade's *Sérénade*, Op.29 is perhaps the first work in which one is able to detect a Spanish flavour. First published in 1884, it has proven to be something of an evergreen ever since. In the key of D major, it opens with a gently coaxing theme in the treble before a second theme enters an octave lower in the dominant and a dialogue ensues between the two voices. The urgency increases before the lower voice takes the lead at the reprise, followed by a reiteration in the treble, some rhetorical gestures in far-flung modulations and a final statement of the theme at a hushed pianissimo, roles reversed, with the left hand crossed over the right, as the work fades into the sunset.

### Lolita, caprice espagnol, Op.54

The rambunctious *Lolita, caprice espagnole*, Op.54 was published in 1890. Even a cursory glance at the score indicates that one could never accuse our heroine of being too closely with the virtue of prudence. A storming left hand ostinato sets the scene and we are launched into a lively dance replete with the swish of fans and the click of castanets. A middle section of burning intensity marked *con fantasia* then ensues in the key of C sharp minor; its fervour tamed only momentarily by gentle rustles of taffeta before the theme of the middle section is stated at a blazing fortissimo in the home key. After a few far-flung modulations, the reprise ensues, building to a brief coda before the ostinato which opened the work summarily rounds things off. Raucous, bold and with a flagrant disregard for politesse, it is a work of unrivalled sass and utterly irresistible.

# MARK VINER

Described by *International Piano Magazine* as "one of the most gifted pianists of his generation". Mark Viner is steadily gaining a reputation as one of Britain's leading concert pianists; his unique blend of individual artistry combined with his bold exploration of the byways of the piano literature garnering international renown.

Born in 1989, he began playing at the age of 11 before being awarded a scholarship two years later to enter the Purcell School of Music where he studied with Tessa Nicholson for the next five years. Another scholarship took him to the Royal College of Music where he studied with Niel Immelman for the next six years, graduating with first class honours in a Bachelor of Music degree in 2011 and a distinction in Master of Performance in 2013; the same year which afforded him the honour to perform before HRH the Prince of Wales.

After winning 1st prize at the Alkan-Zimmerman International Piano Competition in Athens, Greece in 2012, his career has brought him across much of Europe as well as North and South America. While festival invitations include appearances the *Raritäten der Klaviermusik*, Husum in Germany, the Cheltenham Music Festival and Harrogate Music Festival in the United Kingdom and the Festival Chopiniana in Argentina, radio broadcasts include recitals and interviews aired on Deutschlandfunk together with frequent appearances on BBC Radio 3. His acclaimed Wigmore Hall début recital in 2018 confirmed his reputation as one of today's indisputable torchbearers of the Romantic Revival.

He is particularly renowned for his CD recordings on the Piano Classics label which include music by Alkan, Chaminade, Liszt and Thalberg, all of which have garnered exceptional international critical acclaim. His most important project to date is a survey of the complete piano music of Alkan; the first of its kind and which is expected to run to some 18 CDs in length. Aside from a busy schedule of concerts and teaching, he is also a published composer and writer and his advocacy for the music of Alkan and Liszt led to his election as Chairman of both the Alkan Society and the Liszt Society in 2014 and 2017 respectively.
