## GEORGE GERSHWIN PIANO MUSIC

George Gershwin's executive virtuosity is happily documented on records, and also by numerous testimonials from authorities such as Josef Hofmann: "He is a pianist. He has complete command of the keyboard." One of his great talents, chronicled in many theatrical memoirs of the period, was to improvise paraphrases of his songs at every possible social occasion. In fact, as playwright S. N. Behrman recalls, "George Kaufman complained that he played his stuff so much at parties that by opening night, when the audience heard the overture, members of the cast had to be revived." In the course of these impromptu performances of the songs, wrote Gershwin, "I have naturally been led to compose numerous variations upon them, and to indulge the desire for complication and variety that every composer feels when he manipulates the same material over and over again."

In 1932, Simon and Schuster published the handsome Song Book, dedicated to Kay Swift; each of the eighteen songs was presented not only in the original sheet-music version for voice and piano but also in a matching transcription, selected and polished by Gershwin from among those "numerous variations." Only the choruses, never the verses, are given, and in only two cases (Liza and I Got Rhythm) did he publish more than one variation. (On the evidence of his recordings, Gershwin usually played the verse, sometimes at the beginning, sometimes after the first chorus; the variations played there—usually three choruses—hardly ever correspond to the published ones.) The careful shaping of the eighteen written transcriptions as a body invites comparison with the Chopin preludes—one speculates that the earlier piano virtuosi, had they submitted their own improvisations to the same distillation process as Gershwin did here.

In his preface to the Song Book, Gershwin mentioned some of the predecessors to whom he was indebted: "Mike Bernard, Les Copeland, Melville Ellis, Lucky Roberts, Zez Confrey, Arden and Ohman [the piano-duo team who played in several Gershwin shows], and others. Each of these was responsible for the popularization of a technique, or a new wrinkle of playing... Several of the effects I use in my transpositions derive from their style of playing the piano." Indeed, the effects are familiar enough, once you've heard them a few times, to think about them in an historical way. Gershwin thought about them quite personally, as he had grown up with the style. "There was Bernstein's habit of playing the music of the left hand, while he wove a filigree of counterpoint with the right; for a time this was all the rage, as it sounded pretty well to ears that were not accustomed to the higher mental processes—and, when used in moderation, it sounds fine to some sophisticated ears as well: note the beginning of Somebody Loves Me."

**Rialto Ripples**, a ragtime piano solo, was composed with Will Donaldson in 1916 when Gershwin was seventeen years old, and was published the following year. The two Preludes were introduced by the composer on December 4, 1926 at the Hotel Roosevelt in New York, during a joint recital with the singer Marguerite d'Alvarez; these two fast pieces framing a slow, meditative one were Gershwin's only published concert works for solo piano. The first, according to Kay Swift, was written in one sitting. Gershwin referred to it as the "Spanish prelude." The ostinato bass of the second recalls similar patterns in Chopin's preludes, but owes its form to the 12-bar blues; note how, too, the melodic ornaments are varied at the return of the final Prelude is dedicated to Rudi Blesh. Ten years of increased sophistication can easily be measured.

**Impromptu in Two Keys, Three-Quarter Blues, and Merry Andrew** are early—have-not-yet-dated works dating from the teens. **Merry Andrew** was composed around 1924 and was later intended for incorporation in an unproduced show; **Three-Quarter Blues** was not used in the final version of *Porgy and Bess*, but used as a dance number in the 1928 show *Rosalie*. **Piano Playin' Jazzbo Brown** (known in the publisher's catalogue as Jasbo Brown Blues) was not used in the final version of *Porgy and Bess*; it remains that it was in fact the original opening sequence; it remains little known to this day. The stage direction from the published score is as follows: "As the curtain rises on Catfish Row, Jasbo Brown's room which can be exposed to view by use of a sliding panel. Jazzbo is at the piano, playing a low-down blues, while half a dozen couples are seen dancing to its most hypnotic rhythm." Later the dancers begin to sing—or, rather, chant—scat syllables, and at the conclusion of the solo players here the orchestra joins in, leading up to the music of the now-familiar opening. Apparently the expense of constructing the special set for Jazzbo Brown's room brought about the excision of this episode, which establishes an entirely different emotional ambiance at the start of the opera. Kay Swift recalls that Gershwin often played the sequence as a piano solo.

Although not published until 1960, **Promenade** was composed in 1936, and will be familiar to Astaire-Rogers buffs as the "Walking the Dog" music from *Shall We Dance*—one of Gershwin's last Hollywood film and the last he lived to see.

— DAVID HAMILTON

## SONGS BY GEORGE AND IRA GERSHWIN

In the years immediately following World War I, the American musical experienced a great flowering of talented creators and performers. Comedians were at their zaniest. Gongeous girls were never so glamorous. Songwriters blessed with unprecedented opportunities to perfect their craft reached a burgeoning audience, catching the pulse and energy of our expanding national pride and translating it into countless songs that ranged from the patriotic to the optimistic. Standard-bearers in this golden age of the American musical were two New York-born sons of Russian immigrants, George and Ira Gershwin. Their best songs—elegant, vital, sophisticated—endowed our musical theater with a special grace and distinction.

Some of the Gershwins' earliest songs were written for revues—those loose assemblages of tunes, sketches, satire, and opulent presentation that had a particular appeal to a nation as pluralistic as America. A mixture of the minded show, vaudeville, and English music hall, the revue ranged from the lavishness of the Ziegfeld Follies to the wit and intimacy of The Little Show. It was a parade ground where talent could test and refine its skills. Here, the songwriter could enhance his versatility by writing, as was required to fit a show's costumes, the fads of the day, or the disparate vocal ranges of assorted stage personalities.

In 1920, George White, having launched his Scandals the previous year to compete with the Ziegfeld Follies and the Shubert Passing Shows, hired George Gershwin as his staff composer. Gershwin wrote the music for five complete Scandals, from 1920 to 1924. The upbeat *I'll Build a Stairway to Paradise* was the first-act finale of the 1922 edition. Paul Whiteman and his Palais Royal Orchestra led a host of principals and ensemble members in putting the number across. This was the start of the association between George and Whiteman that led two years later to the handiwork's commissioning of the *Rhapsody in Blue*. The verse to *Stairway* has a joyful, riveting energy typical of all of George's contagiously high-spirited music. "By Strauss" (1936) and "Arthur Francis"—the latter a pseudonym Ira Gershwin derived from the first names of his brother Arthur and his sister Frances.

The year 1924 was a turning point for the Gershwin brothers. Ira cast aside his pseudonym, feeling he had won his spurs as a writer without cashing in on his younger brother's rapid rise to prominence. George, meanwhile, was receiving extraordinary acclaim for the *Rhapsody in Blue* and the musical *Primrose*. Later in the year, the brothers wrote the score for *Lady, Be Good!*—the first of the Gershwin musical comedies to captivate Broadway and the first major collaboration between the Gershwins and the brother-and-sister team of Fred and Adele Astaire. *The Man I Love* was written for *Lady, Be Good!* and later became the best-known ballad the Gershwins ever produced; yet it was dropped from the show during its Philadelphia tryout. In fact, though slated for inclusion in two later shows, it was fated never to have a secure place in any production during George's lifetime. Helen Morgan's famous rendition, however, helped establish the song as a classic shortly before the end of the decade. Though a wistful song, *The Man I Love* more than hints at the possibility of a happy resolution, its long-drawn-out phrases that the singers impose upon it misrepresent the authors' intentions.

In any Gershwin musical, one could almost always count on what Ira called the "impossible love" song. One of the finest examples of this genre is *Someone To Watch Over Me*, written for Gertrude Lawrence in *Oh, Kay!* (1926). *How Long Has This Been Going On?*, originally intended for *Funny Face*, was turned up the following year in *Rosalie*. An ingratiating tune with a well-fitting, questioning lyric, it's a number George loved to sing and play at parties.

After revising *Strike Up the Band* in 1930, the Gershwins turned much of their attention to the operetta-like musical satires *Of Thee I Sing* (1931) and *Let 'Em Eat Cake* (1933)—perhaps their most brilliant score for the musical theater. In 1933 they also furnished songs for three Broadway shows: *Pardon My English* (1929), *Girl Crazy* (1930), and the short-lived *Pardon My English* (1933). The haunting ballad *Isn't It a Pity?* stems from this unsuccessful, little-known show that Ira later described as "a headache from start to finish."

From 1933 until the end of 1935, most of George's energies were devoted to his opera *Porgy and Bess*. In the summer of 1934, George and Ira left New York for Hollywood to work in motion pictures. The brothers had been there briefly, five years earlier, writing songs for the film *Delicious* (1931), now, many believed that after *Porgy and Bess* George was capable of writing only for highbrows, as himself expresses it. Yet his three best songs composed in the last year of his life, contain some of his most successful songs.

*They All Laughed*, from *Shall We Dance* (1937), illustrates a favorite notion of George's, that in a two-part song the second part should sometimes be entirely different from the first. Here, even the tag ("Ha, ha, ha! Who's got the last laugh now?") is given a different ending in each of the choruses. This was while they were creating the score for *Shall We Dance* that Vincente Minnelli contacted George with an idea for a New York, and a Straussian take-off the brothers had toyed with in the spring but left unfinished. Minnelli asked them to complete the song for a revue he was producing. *By Strauss*, a wonderful parody of the Viennese waltz, was introduced in *The Show Is On* (1936). Wynn Murray used this music, which, ironically, wasn't in that for its first verse, in his film *An American in Paris* (1951).

*Nice Work If You Can Get It* had been started in New York before the brothers left for California. It was sung by Fred Astaire and a trio of girl singers in *A Damsel in Distress* (1937). Picked up by dance bands everywhere, the piece took on a jagged, choppy quality characteristic that was a far cry from the composer's marking "smoothly."

*If Strauss* shows George's ability to incorporate another composer's style into his own, *Just Another Rhumba* demonstrates his assimilation of a whole country's musical palette. In 1932, he had paid a visit to Cuba and become enamored of its music; the New York performance of his habanera *Cuban Overture* that same year at Lewisohn Stadium featured percussion instruments George had brought back from the island. He again recaptured the mood and color of Cuba in *Isn't It a Pity?* (1933) and *They All Laughed* (1937).

*Love Is Here To Stay* was written for the 1938 film *Goldwyn Follies* (the last George worked on); the song, however, was deleted from the film. Ira's lyric satirizes every known cliché found in the sort of ersatz Spanish number that was all the rage in the late 1930s and early '40s.

The unfailing optimism evident throughout George Gershwin's career shines through even in his last song, *Love Is Here To Stay*. It has a positive, undefeated quality, a serene sense of victory.

— KAY SWIFT & ROBERT KIMBALL

#### BY STRAUSS [30]

Away with the music of Broadway!
To hell with the Irving Berlin!
Out with your Irving Berlin!
To Kern or Cole Porter.

And Gershwin keeps pounding on tin.
How can I live?
When hearing this drivel!
It's only for night clubbing courses.
Oh, give me the Im'o'tan'
Waltz that is Viennesy—
And, so tell the band that
If they want a hand
The waltz must be Strauss's.

Ya, ya, ya—
Give me Oom-pah-pah!

When I want a melody
Lilting through the house,
Then I want a melody—
BY STRAUSS!

It laughs, it sings! The world is in rhyme,
Swinging to three-quarter time.
Let the Dambe flow along,
And "The Fledermaus" give me song—
Keep the wine and give me song—
BY STRAUSS!

By Jo, by ling,
BY STRAUSS is the thing!
So I say to Ha-cha-cha:
Herraus!
Just give me an Oom-pah-pah
BY STRAUSS.

There's a saying old
Says that love is blind.

Haven't found him yet;
He's the big affair

    There's a somebody I'm longing to see:
