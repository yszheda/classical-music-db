## 内页 - 详细注释 (Page 7)

7 In the summer of 1909 I got to know Dmitri Smirnov... equipped by nature to become the true rival of Yershov, Leonid Sobinov and Davydov... he had a powerful lyric tenor of great range of a slightly rough timbre... As well as a good voice and the ability to use it, Smirnov possessed an attractive appearance and diction. Tall and well-built, he spoke with a beautiful Moscow accent... the more performances of his I saw, the more I came to appreciate his natural talent.

8 Some of my most vivid impressions in those early years in St. Petersburg were associated with Nikolai Figner... quickly got to know well. He appeared to be one of those rare artists who have achieved pre-eminence on the stage not so much as the result of their innate qualities as of an exceptional knowledge of how to make use of these gifts: a knowledge acquired through constant unremitting labour. His was the type of singer who will figure in the whole history of vocal art as Figner. The possessor of an average voice, he became an outstanding performer through his unceasing efforts, displaying a truly phenomenal technique in public as well as the most demanding musicians through his musicianship, subtle taste and sheer wealth of detail.

9 Litvinne was virtually unrivalled for her combination of natural gifts and trained sound... not hearing in any way you forget all other excellent voices: you had never heard anything like it! Like Litvinne's, for fullness of the sound itself, for the enchanting timbre, for the softness and the richness of its range. Never before had I heard so smooth a transition from the (so the soprano heights, the firmness of the mezzo-soprano and the contralto depths... In each role Litvinne's voice seemed made for that particular role: in every sound was the music of the particular role and the words were always perfectly heard, while at the same time every word was clear and somehow transparent. This made it seem very easy... but in fact it was always the result of colossal labour.

10 During the break I asked myself: "Mira Kornevna, you must have sung 'this' on many occasions, as you're working on the role now, which detail must know?" "First, believe me just work all my life. Secondly, from time to time Petrovsky and I look at what we did a long time ago, so that this does not happen. Thirdly," she said, "I'm only envious that you have the facilities; our colleagues don't have them."

When I began I didn't know how either. Or rather they were there but I didn't know how to look for them. My advice to you is to go and find them.

11 Male voices like Baklanov's with a two and a half octave range are rare. The timbre of his voice was similar to Tartakov's, beefy and full-blooded like Griegof Progoro's. He had an excellent cantilena. His diction and musicianship was of a high standard. In short he was a talented and versatile artist - all his roles were carefully and interestingly rounded.

12 Lipkovskaya was a genuine pearl of a singer who, of the Russians, perhaps was second only to Nezhdanova, and the two singers were so unlike that the one was renowned for her considerable dramatic gifts... The Russian Snow Maiden, in her holy simplicity and the Queen of Night's brilliant coloratura were absolutely opposite roles and they were both performed by Lipkovskaya with equal success.

13 Gryzunov was a fine Onegin, approaching the best exponents that I have seen and at times surpassing them. His calm and clear-cut musical phrasing, the superb portrayal of the elegant dandy and the well-prepared ironic movement. When he sang as Barnaba (La Gioconda) I was very pleasantly surprised about his acting, as the coarse brutality emanated from this most villainous of all operatic villains that it was difficult to avoid comparing it to that portrayed by Chaliapin himself as Onegin. The depths of villainy were evident in every phrase. Gryzunov sang the role of the pirate chief in the finale with a special inspiration. There were moments I have heard in this role, and with more vivid contrasts of phrasing. He was a skilled actor and knew how to make his eye look cunning.

14 The tenor Damaev... had a magnificent voice... with free top notes... having been a soldier, which is what transforms his voice quite early on: started heavy roles; being a star to his own temperament, towards the second half of a big opera, he would sometimes fade out... I have had much less success and a little more luck than many singers of that period, but with his natural gifts he could have become a great singer but obviously it was beyond his powers not to force.

15 The ease and lack of constraint in Nezhdanova's singing seemed to exceed even a bird's. No bird's songs were as free and warmth in Nezhdanova's singing and the most beautiful nightingale, pouring forth its trills dies away and catches the attention of nature around. Even the most musical nightingale was alien to Nezhdanova and because of this vocal simplicity, Nezhdanova's vocal creations were so effective.

## 内页 - 详细注释 (Page 8)

16 Yershov had different timbres as wide-ranging as his emotions and therefore his rendering of Jolys Lohengrin, Hermann or Prophetov was superior to Moszhukhin's. "Golitsyn" was sad and malicious; Khlopakov's Song (in Pushchenko's The Eagle's Rebellion) was full of unctuous pompousness, striking; Sigismund and Siegfried's cries were of joyous victory and rapturous triumph; Kutema's howls in The Invisible City of Kitezh were full of suffering; the last utterance of Herod in Salome - "Kill that woman" - was so horrified despair that alarmed his audience; Herman's despairing Heilger was the voice of an obsessed person, full of ambiguity; Siegmund's duet with Sieglinde... was moving and radiantly inspired.

17 Sobinov was a celebrity almost from the very start and the gentle, caressing timbre of his voice attracted all of Moscow. He had a remarkable talent for the stage, a wonderful, beautifully developed intellect, rare in an opera singer, all combined to make him a first-class exponent of a wide repertoire which included Lensky, the Prince in Rusalka, Nadir, Alfredo and Lyubava.

18 Kastorsky's success, not only in Russia but literally throughout the whole of Europe was enormous. The Italians rated the sonority as superior to even that for Chaliapin. It was a bass of unusual power; it seemed that nothing could shake it, nothing to move it up and down; the top was slightly dull. His career on stage lasted for almost forty-five years: for another twenty he sang on the radio.

19 There was always a trace of sadness in the rendering of Tartakov's singing, which slightly lessened the effect of bravura passages and arias and reduced their brilliance. It also gave his singing an especial lyricism and intimacy in Eugene Onegin and The Demon; a sadness in the rendering of Lohengrin, Prince Igor, Mazeppa and others. One felt that he was particularly attractive in the songs of Tchaikovsky, Rimsky, Schubert, Schumann and other masters of the Lied. The finish and polish of his singing, his warmth of manner and his wonderfully elegiac tone was as though a close friend had been sitting with you in a gradually darkening room and with feeling had related some recent passion, disappointment or hope, raised then to music.

20 Vitting's voice was beautiful and quite large. The mezzo-carafterre qualities of the sound, the rich middle and free top notes allowed him such diverse roles as Vladimir (Prince Igor) the tenor soloist, the Italian Guest Guest, Gounod's Romeo (both) and Raoul. Having studied singing in Italy with Del Fante, Vitting brought to the Russian theatre the best qualities of the Italian school...

21 For many years Labinsky was the major lyric tenor after Sobinov and Smirnov. He had a solid technique and appeared, himself rather silent, never on the top, with singing, with off, light. His middle and falsetto which was cunningly blended into the voice, were very good. A careful listener, he observed nuances of musical style, he was a concert performer of a reasonably high standard thanks to his innate musicality.

22 It is impossible to find another voice either in history or in the reminiscences of those who went to the Opera of Moscow at that time, which was sung with a particular note at his stage experience was voice that of any other member of the company. Without education, having appeared on the operatic stage only at 30, possessing no external graces, sounding high, almost a low baritone... he worked hard on his recital material and had a good sense of style. His main vocal qualities were obvious: attention to detail and, as correctly noted by the press, good taste.

23 Alexander Moszhukhin, the younger brother of the film star Ivan Moszhukhin, had come to the Theatre of Musical Drama in 1908. Even after only one month there the director of his stage experience was voice that of any other member of the company. Without education, having appeared on the operatic stage only at 30, possessing no external graces, sounding high, almost a low baritone... he worked hard on his recital material and had a good sense of style. His main vocal qualities were obvious: attention to detail and, as correctly noted by the press, good taste.

24 "When you sing the part of the Demon well and your voice is settled in it..." (Medvedev) once said to me. "When you have the whole role the Devil only then must demonstrate his ability - the flexibility of the voice all the time; it doesn't come of its own accord and is easily lost."
