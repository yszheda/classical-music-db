## Liner Notes by François Hudry

The recordings on this album both come from a point of transition in the history of recording, marking as it does the end of the 'shellac (78rpm) era' and the birth of the long-playing record. Ernest Ansermet, like many great conductors, had recorded orchestral music without interruption. However, from time to time, Decca asked him to make recordings with renowned soloists. Such is the case on this recording of the two concertos. Ellen Ballon who was described by Arthur Rubinstein as 'the greatest pianistic genius I have ever met.' She was born in Montreal into a family of Jewish Lithuanian migrants. A child prodigy, she gave her first concert at age five, before completing her studies at the Conservatoire in her birthplace. In 1906 her parents settled in New York, where she gave her first concert with the Symphony Orchestra. She studied with the great Josef Hofmann, then after the war with Vilhelm Backhaus. In 1934, she settled in London and during the course of her career, she performed three times at the White House; before three American Presidents: Taft, Roosevelt and Eisenhower. Physically robust and with a growing reputation, she commissioned a concerto from the Brazilian composer Heitor Villa-Lobos and recorded it in Geneva - on the same day as Ravel's concerto for the left hand which appears on this disc.

The Chopin's F minor concerto, recorded in 1950 at Kingsway Hall in London, dates from the Romantic period of 1830. It is a very much chamber music for piano with orchestral accompaniment, Ballon and Ansermet bring to this work a Romantic sensitivity to the music, creating a truly powerful listening experience. The narrative and dramatic aspects of this performance are truly fascinating.

Ravel's Concerto for the Left Hand was recorded for 78rpm on 23 June 1949, at the Victoria Hall in Geneva, just a few months after five famous recordings of the long playing record. Four years later, the same performers re-recorded the same work for a 33rpm performance, combining G. appraising as a of the composer's G-major Concerto. The French pianist Jacqueline Blancard was born in 1908 in the suburbs of Paris in Gennes. She made excellent recordings of music by Brahms and Schumann (notably the Arabesque, Op.18), before she still alive music and on several occasions played duets with the composer. The left-hand Concerto became one of her signature pieces, at a time when few pianists included it in their repertoire. On 10 November 1937, a few weeks before the composer's death, Blancard, replacing the French pianist Jacques Février, gave the work its Swiss premiere in Geneva, under the baton of Ansermet. The following year she made its first recording for Polydor under direction of Charles Munch. Ansermet produced three recordings of the concerto in the space of six years - 1938, 1949 and 1953 in collaboration with numerous times in concert throughout the world.

After the 1937 concert in Geneva, the critic Albert Paychère commented: 'She showed remarkable power, authority and finesse.' Behind the pianist there lies a musician, confident and flexible. Her success was as great as that of the work itself.

As a conductor, philosopher, mathematician and composer, Ernest Ansermet occupied an exceptional place in the world of music. He also was the co-founder in 1918 of La dans le conscience humaine (Editions Laffont) was often been criticized for his conservatism and his stiff and precise conducting style. One of the greatest conductors of the twentieth century. At the forefront of modernity since 1910, he presented the world with the modern music of composers who were among his friends: Debussy, Ravel, Roussel and Stravinsky primarily, but also Honegger, Frank Martin, Britten, Hindemith, Bartók, Martinů and Schoenberg. The latter was the only one he admired and with whom he had a warm rapport, but not to Schoenberg, whose music was not his cup of tea. For Ansermet, beauty existed no eternal truth beyond the realms of tonal or polytonal and oriental music.

Ansermet conducted the greatest orchestras all over the world, producing and interpreting an impressive array of new works - a list too long to reproduce here. But there are some which remain particularly notable: L'Histoire du soldat; Le Chant du Rossignol, Pulcinella, Renard, Les Noces, Mavra, the Capriccio for piano and orchestra, the Symphony of Psalms and the Mass, Faîte (the Three-Cornered Hat), Boris Godunov, Stravinsky's Petrushka, Igor Stravinsky, Igor de camera, Sauguet (La Valse d'après Glinka and la poème de l'amour et de la mort), Britten (The Rape of Lucretia), War Requiem, Copland Symphony No 1), Walton (Sinfonia Concertante, Viola Concerto) and works and premières of music by Frank Martin (fourteen) and Honegger (seven).

But it would be wrong to position this extraordinary personality solely in the context of music of his time. Ansermet widely conducted what has been called the 'great' repertoire. For example, his clarity and precision, his invigorating approach to Beethoven is remarkable, perfectly valid in the present day. For the Brahms symphonies, he expertly melds their German world with Latin spirit, so dear to the composer's heart. And that is to say nothing of his 'dear Russians' and of the exceptional forays into the romantic worlds of Berlioz, Schumann, Mendelssohn or Sibelius – witness, for example, his amazing performances of the Finnish composer's Second and Fourth Symphonies and Tapiola.

The equilibrium, precision and stylistic beauty, the keen awareness of 'tempo giusto' and the warmth of his performances are vital elements of Ansermet's approach, and it is for these qualities that his art continues to be so meaningful to us today.

*François Hudry*
