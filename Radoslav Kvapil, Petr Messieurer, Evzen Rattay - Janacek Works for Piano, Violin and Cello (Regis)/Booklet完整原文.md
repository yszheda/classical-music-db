# Inner Page 1

**Leoš Janáček** was born in Hukvaldy, a village on the Silesian-Polish border, in 1854. Besides running the village school, his father was also the church organist and choirmaster. As a boy he was often called upon to assist alongside his numerous siblings at services in neighbouring villages and in 1865 he was sent to Brno to board at the St Augustin Abbey and continued his secondary education there. Following his father's death in 1866 Janáček came under the benign influence of Pavel Křížkovský, director of music at the Abbey and an ardent Moravian Nationalalist, who nurtured Janáček's performing talents and instilled an interest in plainchant and polyphony, in addition to contemporary Czech and German choral music. It was hoped that Janáček would follow his father into teaching and between 1869 and 1874 he attended the Brno Teacher Training College. In late 1874, encouraged by Křížkovský, he enrolled at the Prague Organ School where he remained until July 1875 and then returned to the Teacher Training College where he completed his qualification as a music teacher.

The earliest work on this CD is *Romance*, the fourth and only survivor of a group of seven composed between 26 October and 16 November 1879 during Janáček's first term as a student at the Leipzig Conservatory. Between 29 January and 22 February 1880 he wrote the *Tema con variazione* also known as *Zdenka's Variations* as a present for Zdenka Schulz or Schulzová (1865-1938) whose father was Principal of the Brno Teacher Training College. Zdenka's father had kept a close fatherly eye over Janáček's future career and it was he who had secured him a place at the Prague Organ School in 1875; he later directed Janáček towards Leipzig. In 1877 Zdenka began taking lessons from Janáček soon becoming the inspiration of much of his early work including *Romance*, about which Janáček wrote 'I have expressed my joy, my Zdenka, my happiness'.

Another student piece *Dumka* dates from 1880. A dumka is an elegiac and melancholic Slav ballad originating from the Ukraine and was much used by Dvořák in his piano and chamber works. Dvořák tended to contrast the elegiac with a faster section. Whilst Janáček's also makes use of contrasting tempi the opening con moto passage can hardly be said to be cheerful and the overriding impression is that of great melancholy. Perhaps he was missing his Zdenka! These student works are somewhat indebted to the German romantic school (as a student in Leipzig it would be surprising if they weren't). However in Zdenka's Variations one can appreciate a foretaste of Janáček's later style, particularly the use of short contrasting phrases.

Janáček declared in 1916 that 'Moravia alone is enough to give me all necessary inspiration, so rich are her sources' and the *Three Moravian Dances* demonstrate this love for Moravian folksong.

Danaj, composed in April 1892 but discovered in 1948, makes use of a song in which a young woman bemoans her separation from her lover. Originating from neighbouring Slovakian gypsy music, this was re-used later in the first act of his opera *Jenůfa*. Both Čeladenský and Pilky were published in 1905 and were probably composed also in the 1890's. They also use existing folk material, the former being a clown's dance whilst the latter commemorates the collecting of firewood for the winter "Saws".

*Presto* (originally titled *Allegro*) heard here is of unknown date; Radoslav Kvapil, an acknowledged expert on the music and life of Janáček, has speculated that it dates from about 1910 and has further suggested that it might be have been intended for inclusion in *Podháďka* (A Fairy Tale). The composer did not specify the instrumentation, but musicologists are in general agreement it was intended for cello and piano. Although *Podháďka* was inspired by Zukovskij's *The Fairy Tale of Czar Berendej* Janáček did not intend it as programme music. Instead, the three movements composed in 1910 capture the spirit of Berendej's daring exploits, aided by his lover whose father rules over the Underworld. With its constant change of tempo and melody *Podháďka* represents a further key development in the very gradual maturing of Janáček's style.

At the time of the composition of *V mlhách* (In the Mist) in the winter of 1912/13 Janáček was almost 60 years old but had yet to establish himself as the leading composer in Prague. It was this insecurity, coupled with his tragic domestic life following the loss of both of his children ('one long struggle between resignation and newly felt pain') that, according to his biographer Jaroslav Vogel, led to the composition of this work. The premiere was given by Marie Dvořákova in Brno in January 1914. Included in this collection is Kvapil's premiere recording of Janáček's original, shorter conception of the finale. For those wishing to delve into Janáček's composition methodology the short supplement makes fascinating listening; the melodic material is naturally common to both pieces. However the composer introduced a more reflective passage midway through the revised final movement, thus breaking up the mood of what was initially an incessantly forceful finale. Kvapil speculates that Janáček either destroyed or left incomplete roughly two-thirds of his original work and views this first draft of the *Presto* movement as a typical example of the 'not genial' aspect of some of Janáček's music. One is left to wonder what made the composer change to a more reflective mode.

Janáček began work on the *Violin Sonata* in 1912 shortly before the outbreak of World War One, composing the *Balada* movement first. This was in fact his 3rd Violin sonata, but the two previous ones, written in Leipzig and Vienna, before the end of the previous century, were never played and were lost. Of the work originally planned it is thought that the *Balada* movement was placed third and the *Adagio* opened the work. In 1915 the final movement was a *Tempo di marcia* which no longer exists but which is believed to have made use of material from the *Adagio*. Aside from the more tender *Balada* movement, much of the work is set against the violence of the war; considerable tension and a sense of desperation is apparent in the other three movements (even the *scherzo* lacks a playful mood). The composer wrote that he could 'just about hear the sounds of the steel clashing in (his) troubled head' and the piano tremolo passage from the *Adagio* was marked by Janáček as representing the Russian army's (welcome, for him as 'The great Slavonic nation') advance into Moravia on 26 September 1914. Janáček twice revised his *Violin Sonata* before allowing publication and the premiere to take place in Brno in 1922. The following year it was played at the second ISCM festival in Salzburg.

**Radoslav Kvapil**, who was born in 1934 in Brno, is considered by many to be the finest exponent of Czech piano music, a reputation gained through a lifetime's research (much of which is in print) and a considerable discography, much of which is available on Regis. In addition to a busy international performing career in the major concert halls Kvapil has been a Professor at the Prague Conservatory (1963-73) and the University of South Bohemia (1973-76). Since 1977 he has directed numerous masterclasses in the UK, Europe, and USA and has chaired many piano competitions. He has been Chairman of the International Dvořák Society.

Both **Petr Messieurer** and **Evzen Rattay** were fellow founders of the world famous Talich Quartet, formed in 1964 whilst they were students at the Prague Conservatory. During their time with the Talich Quartet (named after the renowned conductor Vaclav Talich) they recorded critically acclaimed cycles of Beethoven and Mozart quartets for the Calliope label, as well as both Janáček's *String Quartets*. Messieurer and Rattay both left the Talich Quartet in the late 1990s to pursue freelance playing and teaching.
