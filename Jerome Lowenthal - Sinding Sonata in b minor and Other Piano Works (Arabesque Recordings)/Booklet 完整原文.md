## 内页 1 (HIPPO_20221227_0003.jpg)

*"An echo from the sea that dashes against the rocks,"* wrote a contemporary about the music of Christian Sinding. Ears such as his heard *'the storm, the thunder of the waves and the daring of the Vikings'* in these evocative, once-popular works by Norway's most important Romantic composer after Edvard Grieg.

Younger by 13 years than his illustrious predecessor, Sinding made his mark internationally at 28. In 1884, with a staggeringly fiendish violin concerto. Thereafter his musical output was prolific: the Piano Concerto in 1893, the First Symphony in 1898, the *Rondo Infinito* for orchestra in 1897, the opera *Der heilige Mountain* in 1914. Eventually Sinding composed a host of songs (more than 250) and piano pieces (more than 200). His was a long, productive and successful life, which ended in 1941—one month short of Sinding's 85th birthday.

Luckily born to a well-to-do family, Christian Sinding became a painter and the other Stephen, a sculptor. Christian trained in his youth to be a violinist but gradually turned to composition. He studied at the famed Leipzig Conservatory. In 1874, Grieg had built a reputation for himself, but Sinding threw away during his early years under the watchful eyes of Carl Reinecke. Reinecke's Brahmsian tendencies were, however, to be excellent preparation for the composition of the kind of work that would emerge from Sinding's pen. Sinding studied with Grieg's pupil, Hans Sitt. Sinding's study of Chopin, Brahms and Schumann (all composers of considerable stature) gave him a broad foundation for his own compositional endeavors.

A year after his graduation, Sinding penned his first Piano Sonata, a work he never performed in public again. Although that early work is now lost, Jerome Lowenthal, we do know only once more. In 1909, at a mature age of 53. The product of that effort, heard here, was published with a dedication to the German pianist Rudolph Ganz (who, a year earlier that he, had accepted the dedication of Ravel's *Gaspard de la Nuit*).

A luxuriantly romantic, late Romantic score featuring lush, arching melodies, cascading arpeggios, sweeping scales, and chromatic harmonies. The three movements of the Sonata in B Minor will delight connoisseurs of extroverted keyboard music. The first movement, *Allegro non troppo*, is a profusely contrapuntal display of the keyboard. The second, *Andante*, is a lyrical outpouring, an early work of Sinding. Sinding saves his affirmation of the tonic for 30 bars, then lets it in a blaze of major tonality, supported by full-bodied chords in both hands. Throughout there is a constant dialogue between the hands, with the melody passing from one to the other. The finale, *Vivace*, is a brilliant display of keyboard virtuosity, with rapid scales, arpeggios, and octaves. Here, a spiraling melody is expressed mostly gliding, here and there. Its coda, with its fiery momentum, is as virtuosic and thunderous as its virtuosity as anything by Liszt.

## 内页 2 (HIPPO_20221227_0004.jpg)

**Jerome Lowenthal** made his debut at age 13 in his native Philadelphia with the Philadelphia Orchestra. He studied with William Kapell and Edward Steuermann at the Juilliard School and with Alfred Cortot in Paris. For several years he lived in Jerusalem where he was concert lecturer at the Jerusalem Academy of Music and a regular performer with the Israel Philharmonic. Mr. Lowenthal returned to the United States in 1961, and in 1963 he performed the Bartók Piano Concerto No. 2 under the direction of Joseph Krips with the New York Philharmonic. He has since appeared with most of the major American and European orchestras and has given joint recitals with Nathan Milstein, Itzhak Perlman, and his wife, pianist Ronit Amir.

Mr. Lowenthal recently recorded the *Zanegory Piano Quintet* (Z6530) for Arabesque.

## 内页 3 (HIPPO_20221227_0004.jpg 左侧)

Inevitably, some critics found themselves awash in Sinding's swirls of tone and melody. They deplored the older movements of the Sonata, *'swimming fantasies'* and *'bombastic'*. Others found the invention *'live and spontaneous'*, lauding its *'vigor and effectiveness'*. Thanks to the passage of three-quarters of a century and the present recording, we can assess the work anew.

That holds true for the ten shorter pieces as well. Each appeared along with the other pieces of its opus individually, and post the new manuscript to his publishers. Later, every one came out in other collections and arrangements. Sinding's own favorite among the shorter pieces—*Melodie* (1906) attracted admirers for its simple utterance; genial flow and modest technical demands. *Capriccio* (1909) pleased as an excellent contrast. *Pomposo* (1894) and *Caprice* (1906) were short, lively delights, with echoes of Wagner and Brahms respectively. The tiny *Serenade* (1896) over the gem is, in a lyrical effusion floating effortlessly in twos and threes over constant, quiet arpeggiation. Sinding's sprightly *Irrlicht* (1906) will bring a smile to the ear of anyone capable of quick flicks of the wrist. *Alla marcia* (1895) belonged to the composer's rack of brilliant military pieces, which featured rhythmic and almost orchestral sonorities. The *Marche grotesque* (1896) once enjoyed a huge following. *Capriccio* (1909) offers a virtuoso exercise in double trills and a jaunty rhythmic pattern alternating between the two hands. If proved to be a miniature concert etude, the *Rustles of Spring* (1896) shows Sinding to the world at his humble best, with the most remarkable characteristic of all: a piece which is neither too difficult nor too easy, proved to be so popular that it made the composer's name a household word throughout Europe and America, and, sad to say, has gone out of the works together and, over time, may have contributed to the devaluation of its reputation.

When the opportunity of a miniature forever a composer's best and largest works into real oblivion, the responsibility is the composer's. Woe betide the composer whose reputation rests on the *Rustles of Spring* by Sinding—two unfortunate results occur: performers, scholars and the press ignore both the rest and the composer's reputation in gradation. It is Sinding's good fortune that this recording will help to restore some of the balance needed in placing his works against the backcloth of music history for a timely reappraisal and much sheer pleasure.
