## Age of Bel Canto

Rossini was the leading Italian opera composer of the early 19th century. Though he lived until 1868, he retired from the lyric stage some 40 years earlier without ever really explaining why. Among the leading theories is that he simply felt his style of composing was no longer what audiences wanted. Certainly he felt out of sympathy with then-novel methods of vocal production, such as singing from the chest rather than the head (he once irritably invited a visiting young tenor to leave his high C on the hatrack), and he is alleged to have lamented in his later years "Alas for us, we have lost our bel canto," i.e., "beautiful singing." Rossini is said to have defined the term as denoting a voice of natural beauty that is even throughout its range: effortless delivery of florid music thanks to diligent training and a deep comprehension of style acquired through years of immersion in the genre.

Whether Rossini really said all this or not, and it remains unconfirmed, the little phrase soon took on a life of its own and began to mean just about anything anyone wanted to attribute to it. For some it was the entire vocal legacy of Italy in the 18th and early 19th centuries. For others it was just the operas of Rossini, Bellini and Donizetti. In the early 20th century German musicologists invented a new lineage for bel canto, claiming it was a lyrical style that emerged in the second quarter of the 17th century, but in fact there is no record of anyone using the term until after Rossini, more than 200 years later.

Despite all the confusion and mystification, the designation remains popular for one simple reason: everybody knows what it is when they hear it. It is something very close to Rossini's alleged definition, and it exists almost exclusively in the Italian school (which technically includes Handel and Mozart) from the Baroque era up until Verdi—in whose operas, admittedly, there are plenty of moments that can be considered bel canto despite the introduction of a new and more strenuous style. By the time of Puccini and the verismo school, however, it was clearly a thing of the past.

One must ask, if Rossini decried the death of bel canto in 1858, what can we expect from recordings of it made, as in this album, from 1907 to 1953? In fact, this period was the heyday of the styles of Verdi and Puccini, and it was not until the efforts of Maria Callas in about 1953 and onward that a serious effort was made to return to the principles Rossini put forward. We do not then expect authenticity as such, but in compensation we receive something still enormously worthwhile: some of the greatest voices ever put on disc, voices often of miraculous beauty and seemingly superhuman endurance, voices perhaps as great or greater than those before or after, voices which could sing the telephone directory and make it sound sublime.

If Enrico Caruso and Nellie Melba were not, precisely speaking, exemplars of bel canto singing, there is not therefore to be despised. Their singing was "beautiful singing," even if it was produced in a way different from their forebears in the times of Rossini or Bellini, and their spectacular success with this music demonstrates both their overwhelming charisma and the adaptability of their material.

These were singers who had the star appeal of rock singers in their day. Some are still remembered, if unwittingly, by the general public, e.g. Nellie Melba, immortalized by the toast and the peach dessert named after her, and Luisa Tetrazzini by the chicken. In Russia one can buy, of all unlikely things, a brand of cigarettes named after the great bass Chaliapin—and there was once a cigar named after Marcella Sembrich! The name of Caruso has lived on simply because he was the biggest star in the history of vocal music, and his name is confidently bandied about by people who have never heard an opera in their lives.

Among the others are names familiar to every serious opera lover, but to no one else. All of them are singing music of the great age of bel canto, in ways we can only think Rossini himself would have approved if he had been able to hear the results. If this is your first introduction to these singers, we trust you will hear, even if sometimes through a thin veil of sonic senescence, moments of vocalism so beautiful and so technically astonishing that you will be intrigued to hear from them again, and to learn more about their lives and achievements. This is the immortality they deserve.

**Bill Parker**
