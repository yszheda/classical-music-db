The analog recording source for these compact discs was made before noise-reduction methods such as Dolby were available. In the digital remastering, some effort to minimize the inherent tape hiss was made; radical methods have not been used in order to preserve the full-frequency content of the original recordings. ## Liner Notes / 内页说明

The dramatic symphony, *Roméo et Juliette*, op. 17, with chorus, solosists and a prologue in choral recitative, after Shakespeare's tragedy, was written by the poet and playwright, Émile Deschamps. It was written by Berlioz in 1838, owing to a generous gift from Paganini, which enabled him to put his financial worries behind him. This "sublime work of art", the very expression used by the composer, was given its first performance under Berlioz on the 24th November 1839, conducted by Berlioz himself, who performed it again on the 1st and on the 14th December, 1839. This brilliant work, naturally dedicated to Paganini, and published in 1847 after a few final revisions, is "neither a concert opera, nor a cantata, but a symphony with chorus", as is emphasised in the preface to the score. The dramatic scenes in the Garden and in the Cemetery, the conversation between the two lovers, Romeo's passionate outbursts, the duets expressing love and despair are not sung, because, according to Berlioz, the "very nobility of that love" calls for free recourse to musical imagination and to "instrumental language, a richer language which is more varied, more flexible and, by its very vagueness, incomparably more powerful in so supreme a crisis" (Preface).

This "tremendous work" was an immediate success and startled the entire European musical world, including Wagner, with its radical newness. Berlioz wrote in his Mémoires (Paris, Flammarion, 1881, p. 267) that *Roméo et Juliette* "presents great performance difficulties of every sort, inherent in the form and style. To give a good performance of the piece, it is essential to have first-rate artists - conductor, orchestral players and soloists".

Charles Munch was probably this century's finest Berlioz conductor and would have been numbered among those first-rate artists. Other eminent conductors have given skilled and gifted performances of the music by the composer of *La Damnation de Faust*, but only Charles Munch knew how to emulate Berlioz's visionary romanticism, his flamboyant subjectivity, his dazzling flashes of inspiration so well. Charles Munch championed Berlioz throughout the world, and was one of the first to strive for his return to favour, in the United States and in Europe, by ensuring his presence in the recorded repertoire and in his concert programmes. Charles Munch identified body and soul with the truest spirit of Berlioz's music: especially in the great dramatic frescoes or the highly-charged passionate works. The *Requiem*, op. 5, that "Grande Messe des Morts" in which, according to Berlioz (Mémoires p.582), the listener is "crushed by a terrible tear"; *La Damnation de Faust*, op. 24, "Dramatic legends" of a fiery passion and inspiration inflicted by the madness of "Pandaemonium"; *Roméo et Juliette*, op. 17, which has an incandescent, breathless power; the Overtures and the *Roman Carnival*, which contain an incredible abundance of rhythm and colour; *Harold in Italy*, op. 16, with its orgy of the bandits; the *Symphonie fantastique*, op. 14, which Charles Munch could imbue, both on record and in concert, with a unique explosive violence, an understanding of the unprecedented orchestral writing; these are the works recorded for RCA with the Boston Symphony Orchestra between 1953-1961.

Charles Munch was drawn to the myth of the Lovers from Verona, a myth which - like Tristan and Isolde - has given rise to the myth of impossible love, of love in death. This fascination is displayed by his magnificent recordings of Tchaikovsky's *Fantasia Ouverture Romeo and Juliet* and Prokofiev's Suites from his ballet. *Romeo and Juliet*, he recorded the Berlioz *Roméo et Juliette* on two occasions with the Boston Symphony Orchestra: in 1953 in mono, and in 1961 in stereo. The second recording, with Rosalind Elias, Cesare Valletti and Giorgio Tozzi as soloists, is the more famous because it was brought out several times on LP. It was interesting, therefore, to remaster Charles Munch's first interpretation on CD, in which he demonstrates perfectly the qualities which make him unforgettable in his treatment of Berlioz: the deeply moving intensity of his lyricism in the "Scène d'amour", the subterranean momentum of his rhythmic beat in the "Grande fête chez Capulet", his magical sensitivity in the "Scherzo de la reine Mab", "the surge of delirious joy", the fearful anger and the utmost precision of tone-colour in "Roméo au Tombeau des Capulets".

"To play my compositions well," wrote Berlioz, "the performers and above all their conductor, must feel the same way I do." An unspoken precision linked to a compelling liveliness, an ordered passion, a dreamlike sensitivity, a passionate mode of expression, internal fire, rhythmic impetus and the unexpected are all essential. These predominant qualities in Berlioz's music are displayed in full measure by Charles Munch. If Charles Munch was, more than any other, the exciled instigator of these "romantic storms" to quote one of the phrases from his book *Je suis chef d'orchestre* (Paris, Editions du Conquistador, 1954), he was also a poet full of refined enthusiasm and commanding an unusual subtlety of sound, as demonstrated by two other great Berlioz recordings for RCA: *L'Enfance du Christ*, Op. 25 (1956) and, in particular, *Les Nuits d'été*, op. 7, with Victoria de los Angeles (1955).

*Les Nuits d'été* op. 7 is a setting of the poems of Théophile Gautier, and is a cycle of six songs. "Villanelle", "Le Spectre de la Rose", "Sur les lagunes", "Absence", "Au cimetière", "L'île inconnue". Although this song cycle was originally composed for voice and piano around 1840, and published in this version in 1841, it was definitively orchestrated in 1856. The work is extraordinarily beautiful for its melodic poise and its inexpressible fascination with romantic love which is captivating and eternally rejected in an inaccessible place somewhere else, an unknown island, the land of imaginary love. Charles Munch, with Victoria de los Angeles, who also recorded Debussy's *La Damoiselle élue* for RCA, have achieved a historic recording here. This work emanates an exquisite charm and an intoxicating sensuality, and also the fragrance of restrained emotion which proves that Munch could be a great accompanist.

#### 2. PROLOGUE

**CHORUS**
As in Shakespeare's first Prologue, the chorus tells of the "two households" in "fair Verona" and their "ancient grudge"; and of the Prince's decree and the ball at the Capulets'. Continuing, it relates how Romeo wanders about the Capulets' palace, drawn by his love for Juliet; how he finds Juliet in her balcony "confiding her love to the night," and how he reveals himself.

### PART TWO / DEUXIEME PARTIE

#### 1. ROMEO ALONE - SADNESS (ROMÉO SEUL - TRISTESSE)

(Orchestra)

The movement opens with a pianissimo phrase for violins, which, developed into increasingly fervid expression, seems to reflect the contemplation of the melancholy lover who has strayed into the hostile territory of the Capulet palace.

**Distant sounds of music and dancing - Great festivities in Capulet's palace**

Dancing rhythms become the background of his thoughts.
The tempo becomes allegro and the ballroom strains more insistent. The isolated figure of Romeo intermittently holds the attention, the music of festivity recurring and bringing the close.

#### 2. STARLIGHT NIGHT (NUIT SEREINE)

Capulet's garden, silent and deserted
The young Capulets leaving the hall pass by singing

The movement opens with an allegretto (pianissimo) in which the voices of passing revelers sing snatches of song.

**CHORUS**
Oh! Capulets — bonsoir, bonsoir!
Cavaliers, au revoir!
Ah! Quelle nuit, quel festin!
Bal divin!
Que de folles paroles!
Belles Véronaises
Sous les grandes fenêtres,
Allez rêver de bal d'amour
Allez, rêver d'amour
Jusqu'au jour!
Tra la, la la la, la la la la la!...
Tra la la, la la la la...

**CHANT ET SCHERZETTO**

**CHORUS**
Ho! Capulets, good-night all!
Gentlemen, farewell!
Ah! what a night, and what
A glorious ball!
What mad, enchanting talk!
Oh, girls of Verona,
Can't you go now and dream of love
Under the eavesomes till down
Go dream of love
Till dawn!
Tra la, la la la la...

#### 3. LOVE SCENE (SCÈNE D'AMOUR)

(Orchestra)

An adagio begins with the muted strings: expressive single voices of the violas, horns and cellos stand out in music of increasing ardor and richness. A melodic passage from a solo cello suggests the voice of Romeo, although the movement is developed in purely musical fashion. It dies away at last and ends with a pianissimo chord.

"If you ask me which of my works I prefer," wrote Berlioz in 1858, "my answer is that among almost all my compositions I feel the most affection for the love scene in 'Roméo and Juliet'."

#### 4. QUEEN MAB SCHERZO (SCHERZO: LA REINE MAB OU LA FÉE DES SONGES)

(Orchestra)

The Scherzo, prestissimo, is pianissimo almost throughout. The place of a Trio is taken by an allegretto section wherein "Queen Mab in her miniscoot car," wrote Berlioz to his friend Hum, "attended by the buzzing insects of a summer's night and laughing at full gallop by her tiny horses, fully displayed to the Brunswick public her lovely drapery and her thousand caprices. But you will understand my anxiety on this subject; for you, the poet of fairies and elves, the 'own brother' of those graceful and malicious little creatures, know only too well with what slender thread they veil or gaze in women; and how tame must be the sky beneath which they may coalesce into sport freely in this pale starlight."

## LES NUITS D'ÉTÉ, Op. 7

### Six poèmes de Théophile Gautier

### VILLANELLE

**FRENCH**
Quand viendra la saison nouvelle,
Quand auront disparu les froids,
Tous les deux nous irons, ma belle,
Pour cueillir le muguet aux bois;
Sous nos pieds égrenant les perles
Que l'on voit au matin trembler,
Nous irons écouter les merles
Siffler.

Le printemps est venu, ma belle,
C'est le mois des amants béni;
Et l'oiseau, satinant son aile,
Dit ses vers au rebord du nid;
Oh, viens donc sur ce banc de mousse
Pour parler de nos beaux amours,
Et dis-moi de ta voix si douce:
Toujours!

Loin, bien loin, égarant nos courses,
Faisons fuir le lapin caché,
Et le daim au miroir des sources
Admirant son grand bois penché;
Puis chez nous, tout rieurs, tout aises,
En paniers enlaçant nos doigts,
Revenons, rapportant des fraises
Des bois.

**ENGLISH**
When the season changes
And the cold weather has gone,
My love, we will go together
To pick lily-of-the-valley in the woods.
Our feet scattering the pearls
That glisten at dawn in the dew,
We will go and hear
The blackbirds sing.

The spring has come, my love,
It is the blessed season for lovers;
And the bird, preening its feathers,
Sings songs from the edge of its nest.
Oh come and sit on this mossy bank
And talk of our happy love,
And say to me in your soft voice:
For ever!

Far, far away, our footsteps wandering,
We'll startle the rabbit from its lair,
And the deer watching its great antlers
Mirrored in the stream;
Then back home in complete happiness and contentment,
Our fingers entwined, return
Carrying baskets of wild strawberries.

### LE SPECTRE DE LA ROSE

**FRENCH**
Soulève ta paupière close
Qu'effleure un songe virginal;
Je suis le spectre d'une rose
Que tu portais hier au bal.
Tu me pris encore emperlée
Des pleurs d'argent de l'arrosoir,
Et parmi la fête étoilée
Tu me promenas tout le soir.

O toi, qui de ma mort fus cause,
Sans que tu puisses le chasser,
Toutes les nuits mon spectre rose
À ton chevet viendra danser;
Mais ne crains rien, je ne réclame
Ni messe ni De Profundis;
Ce léger parfum est mon âme
Et j'arrive du paradis.

Mon destin fut digne d'envie,
Et pour avoir un sort si beau,
Plus d'un aurait donné sa vie;
Car sur ton sein j'ai mon tombeau,
Et sur l'albâtre où je repose
Un poète avec un baiser
Écrivit: «Ci-gît une rose
Que tous les rois vont jalouser.»

**ENGLISH**
Lift up your eyelids
That glow with a maiden dream.
I am the ghost of a rose
You took the ball last night at the ball.
You took me still moist
With the silver tears of the watering can,
And amidst the sparkling scene
You walked me with you all evening.

Oh you who was cause of my death:
Without your being able to escape it
Night after night my fragrant ghost
Will come to dance at your pillow.
But do not be afraid, I ask
No mass or De Profundis;
This faint perfume is my soul
And it is from paradise that I come.

My destiny was one to be coveted;
And for so bright a lot
Many a man would give his life,
For on your breast is my tomb,
And on the marble whiteness where I rest
A poet wrote with a kiss:
"Here lies a rose
That all the kings will envy."

### SUR LES LAGUNES (LAMENTO)

**FRENCH**
Ma belle amie est morte.
Je pleurerai toujours;
Sous la tombe elle emporte
Mon âme et mes amours.
Dans le ciel sans m'attendre
Elle s'en retourna.
L'ange qui l'emmena
Ne voulut pas me prendre.
Que mon sort est amer!
Ah, sans amour s'en aller sur la mer!

La blanche créature
Est couchée au cercueil.
Comme dans la nature
Tout me paraît en deuil!
La colombe oubliée
Pleure et songe à l'absent;
Mon âme pleure et sent
Qu'elle est dépareillée.
Que mon sort est amer!
Ah, sans amour s'en aller sur la mer!

Sur moi la nuit immense
S'étend comme un linceul.
Je chante ma romance
Que le ciel entend seul.
Ah, comme elle était belle,
Et comme je l'aimais!
Je n'aimerai jamais
Une femme autant qu'elle.
Que mon sort est amer!
Ah, sans amour s'en aller sur la mer!

**ENGLISH**
My fair one is dead.
I will weep always.
She has taken with her into the tomb
My whole being and all my love.
To heaven she did not wait
But returned to heaven.
The angel that drew her back
Would not take me with her.
How bitter is my fate.
Ah, without love to depart on the sea!

Her white body
Sleeps in the grave;
Everything in nature
Seems to me to be in mourning.
The grieving dove weeps
And dreams of her love;
My soul weeps and feels
That it is orphaned.
How bitter is my fate.
Ah, without love to depart on the sea!

The night spreads over me
Like a love-torn shroud.
I sing my song,
A song enchanting and full of doom,
Which affects you unpleasantly
And which one would like to listen to for ever.
One would think the awakened soul
Wept under the earth
In tune with the song.
And from grief at being forgotten
Complained in a soft murmur
Like the moaning of a dove.

### ABSENCE

**FRENCH**
Reviens, reviens, ma bien-aimée!
Comme une fleur loin du soleil
La fleur de ma vie est fermée
Loin de ton sourire vermeil.

Entre nos cœurs quelle distance!
Tant d'espace entre nos baisers!
Ô sort amer! Ô dure absence!
Ô grands désirs inapaisés!

Reviens, reviens, etc.

**ENGLISH**
Come back, come back my beloved,
Like a flower away from the sun
The flower of my life is closed up
Away from your warm smile.

What a distance lies between our hearts;
So great a gulf between our kisses;
What bitter fate, oh cruel absence,
Mighty desires unsatisfied!

Come back, etc.

### AU CIMETIÈRE (CLAIR DE LUNE)

**FRENCH**
Connaissez-vous la blanche tombe
Où flotte avec un pin planté
L'ombre d'un if?
Sur l'urne une colombe
Triste et seule au soleil couchant,
Chante son chant:

Un air maladive et tendu,
À la fois charmant et fatal,
Qui vous fait mal
Et qu'on voudrait toujours entendre;
Un air comme en soupire aux cieux
L'ange amoureux.

On dirait que l'âme éveillée
Pleure sous terre à l'unisson
Et de la voix de l'absente
Se plaint dans un roucoulement
Bien doucement.

**ENGLISH**
Do you know the white gravestone
Which floats upon it a yew-tree
The shade of a yew-tree?
On the urn a solitary white dove
Sings her sad song
As the sun goes down

A sickly sweet air
At once enchanting and full of doom,
Which affects you unpleasantly
And which one would like to listen to for ever.
A sigh issued as if to heaven
By a love-torn angel.

One would think the awakened soul
Wept under the earth
In tune with the song.
And from grief at being forgotten
Complained in a soft murmur
Like the moaning of a dove.

### L'ÎLE INCONNUE

**FRENCH**
Dites, la jeune belle,
Où voulez-vous aller?
La voile enfle son aile,
La brise va souffler.

L'aviron est d'ivoire,
Le pavillon de moire,
Le gouvernail d'or fin.
J'ai pour lest une orange,
Pour voile une aile d'ange,
Pour mousse un séraphin.

Dites, la jeune belle,
Où voulez-vous aller?
La voile enfle son aile,
La brise va souffler.

Est-ce dans la Baltique?
Dans la mer Pacifique?
Dans l'île de Java?
Ou bien est-ce en Norvège,
Cueillir la fleur de neige,
Ou la fleur d'Angsoka?

Dites, la jeune belle,
Où voulez-vous aller?
Menez-moi, dit la belle,
À la rive fidèle
Où l'on aime toujours!
Cette rive, ma chère,
On ne la connaît guère
Au pays des amours.

Où voulez-vous aller?
La brise va souffler.

**ENGLISH**
Tell me, young beauty,
Where do you want to go?
The sail is setting out,
The breeze is getting up.

The oar is ivory,
The flag of silk,
The helm of fine gold.
For ballast I have an orange,
For sail, an angel's wing,
For ship's boy a seraph.

Tell me, young beauty,
Where do you want to go?
The sails are set,
The breeze is getting up.

Is it to the Baltic?
To the Pacific Ocean?
To the island of Java?
Or is it to Norway,
To pick the snowflowers,
Or the flowers of Angsoka?

Tell me, young beauty,
Where do you want to go?
Take me, she replies,
To the faithful shore
Where love lasts for ever.
That shore, my dear,
Is little known
In the country of love.

Where do you want to go?
The breeze is getting up.
