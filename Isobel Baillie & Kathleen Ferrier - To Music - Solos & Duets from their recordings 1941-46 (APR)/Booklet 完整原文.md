## Programme Notes (Pages 4-7)

If the coming together on record of Isobel Baillie and Kathleen Ferrier is the result of a regrettable incident (to be explained later) the published duet discs document for posterity the artistic union of two of Britain's greatest and most popular singers. That the recorded partnership proved such a success was due in no small way to the fact that the two enjoyed a warm personal friendship and a sympathetic professional relationship. The soprano, dubbed 'The British Tetrazzini', dominated Kathleen Ferrier's early steps in her cruelly brief singing career. Isobel Baillie was, to all intents and purposes, her 'musical mother'.

Kathleen Ferrier entered the musical world via the piano and it was as an accompanist that she proved an invaluable asset during the late-1930s and early-1940s to her then friend, Nan Chadwick, a flourishing Northern vocal teacher. Coincidentally, Nan Chadwick and Isobel Baillie had been close since moving to Italy in the mid-1920s as pupils of Guglielmo Somma and it is therefore no surprise to discover that Isobel Baillie first became mention of Kathleen Ferrier's name via Nan Chadwick... However, it was not until Ferrier had summoned enough confidence to desert the piano for singing that the 'IB' and 'KF' of this programme first met. The occasion was a December 1941 'Messiah' at London's Aeolian Hall conducted by then the leader of the Halle Orchestra, Alfred Barker. The vastly experienced Isobel Baillie—at that time she already had been singing the oratorio for some thirty years—must have been an inspiring support to the then she explained the situation to Isobel Baillie and asked if they would enable her to fulfil her contract as a series of duet recordings. The impasse was consequently broken in September 1945, after which Ferrier moved to Decca where she remained until her first real 'Messiah'. 'Well, dear,' came the instant reply, 'you had better start counting for it won't be your last!'

If fate was to decree that Ferrier would enjoy a professional singing career of little more than a decade—and much of that dogged by the cancer which was ultimately to take her life—the Baillie/Ferrier paths crossed regularly. Most often, of course, they met as Moira's London appearance (in May 1943 at Westminster Abbey) and the crucial first 'Mozart Mass' at the Festival Hall. As Ferrier's career rapidly assumed international dimensions it was inevitable that they saw less of each other, although it was to be a poignant symmetry to their partnership when they stood side-by-side for that last performance of 'The London Messiah' in 1952 at the Royal Albert Hall).

This programme, partly a reflection of the once-fashionable 'celebrity recital', is compiled from recordings made by both singers during the period 1941-45. At this time Isobel Baillie, born in Hawick, Scotland in 1895, was at the pinnacle of her illustrious career. She had served a long apprenticeship in and around the Manchester area before making her debut (with the Hallé Orchestra and Hamilton Harty) in 1921. Within a few years of her London debut in 1926, her position as the principal oratorio soprano of her generation established. She never officially retired and continued to appear before the public until 1983, the year of her death.

The chosen selections are but a small part of an extensive discography which encompasses the years 1924 to 1974. (She is one of the very few singers—and surely the only soprano—to have commenced recording in the acoustic [pre-electric] period and go on to record in stereo.) These particular titles were recorded during brief stays in London. (Her American series of this period are crowded with appearances of throughout the length and breadth of the British Isles and overseas, an indication not only of their exceptional popularity but also of her astonishing work schedule.)

The Kathleen Ferrier titles capture the singer at the very start of her meteoric career. Born in Higher Walton, Lancashire in 1912, she first faced the studio microphone as a singer in 1944: she was thirty-two and had made her London debut only the previous year. They comprise four unpublished test recordings for HMV and a handful of unpublished Columbia test discs. The former (Brahms, Elgar & Gluck) were recorded in June 1944 and lay forgotten until 1978 when they were included on a short-lived HMV Treasury LP, Great British. Mezzo-soprano and Contralto, was a solitary exception, this being as a result of contractual obligations with Bruno Walter and the Vienna Philharmoniker.) In the event, only four of the six Baillie/Ferrier duets were published: the masters of two traditional items—'O'ere't than in the cauld blast' and 'Iverr ta to me'—were destroyed soon after being recorded.

Finally, mention must be made of Gerald Moore (1899-1987) who accompanies the entire programme. From the 1930s until his retirement in 1967 there was, in the public eye, no rival to Moore's exceptionally versatile work in the recording studio, predominantly for HMV and Columbia, remains unrivalled in quality and quantity.

Hearing the two artists' recordings alongside each other reveals how the microphones reacted so differently to the two voices. Where it seemed to bask in the velvety warmth of Ferrier's voice it often appeared nonplussed by the intense purity of Isobel Baillie's voice.
