## 内页 - 法文说明

### Les Saisons

L'histoire de cette œuvre n'est pas courte de remarquable. Tchaïkovsky composa le piano cycle some 145 years ago and the enthusiasm it garners worldwide has not abated since. Les pièces sont popular parmi les musiciens professionnels, les amateurs et les enfants.

Ce n'est pas Tchaïkovsky himself who had the idea for the cycle. The suggestion came from Nikolay Bernard, the editor of the women's magazine *Nouvelist*, published in Saint Petersburg. The magazine appeared monthly and 1876. For each piece, Bernard suggested both a title and a short epigraph of Russian poetry. Tchaïkovsky readily agreed to all his proposals. Only after the last issue of the year was published in December did the editor publish the twelve pieces together, in a separate issue entitled *The Seasons*. Thus the first edition of Opus 37a was born. It was an instant success. One of the first countries outside Russia where it was published was France. In fact, one day Tchaïkovsky chanced upon the French edition in the window of a Saint Petersburg music shop.

Although numerous pianists have played several of its pieces, the entire cycle has rarely been recorded. *The Seasons* is indissociable from Konstantin Igumnov (1873-1948), an outstanding pianist and renowned pedagogue at the Moscow Conservatory. Heinrich Neuhaus, an equally famous pianist and teacher of that same conservatory, Tchaïkovsky's music was accessible, it could not be considered easy. It requires not only strength and gentleness, but also sensitivity and refinement. According to Neuhaus, no one was better than Igumnov at rendering such a wide palette of sounds, with a forbright, sincere intonation, played with indescribable charm.

Konstantin Igumnov was probably the first pianist to play the entire oeuvre of *The Seasons*. Once we have acquired an overview of the whole, it becomes clear that each piece is a part of a single narrative with a particular dramatic development. The images of nature and the scenes of Russian life are arranged to fit an allegory of human life. Igumnov saw this work as a "burning confession of the human soul." The poetry of time passing, the melancholy of the Russian spirit, sincerity, intimacy and elegance all contribute to the very essence of this work.

### Janvier: Au coin du feu

*A little corner of peaceful bliss*
*The night dressed in twilight,*
*The little fire is dying in the fireplace,*
*And the candle has burned out.*

**Alexander Pushkin**

The first piece, *By the Hearth*, sets the scene for the poetical atmosphere of *The Seasons*. The epigraph, taken from Pushkin's *Drooms*, immediately establishes the character of the piece, conjuring up a snowy winter evening. During the Russian winter, days are extremely short, and so people in the past enjoyed long evenings at the fireside. The burning flames would awaken memories and fantasies. Igumnov tells us about his childhood in the small town of Lebedyan in the Lipetsk region. In his home there was a fireplace, and as children they would play in the firelight. He sat on the floor, letting their imaginations run free. Dreaming is at the heart of this piece. In one of his letters Tchaïkovsky describes a melancholy feeling: it is the nostalgia which seizes over us when at evening we sit indoors alone, weary of work, while the book we have picked up for relaxation slips unheeded from our finger. A long procession of old memories goes by. How sad to think how much is already past and gone! And yet these recollections of youth are sweet. We regret the past, although we have neither courage nor desire to start a new life. We are rather weary of existence. We would fain rest awhile and look back, recalling many things. There were moments when young blood pulsed warm through our veins and life gave us all we asked. There were also moments of sorrow, irreparable loss. All this has receded so far into the past. How sad, yet sweet to lose ourselves therein!

### Février: Le Carnaval

*At the lively Mardi Gras*
*soon a large feast will overflow.*

**Pavel Vyazemsky**

*The Carnival* or *Mardi Gras* (Maslenitsa) stands in stark contrast to the preceding piece. In a letter to his editor, Tchaïkovsky wrote that he composed the two pieces in one sitting.

In this piece, the frosty air of the Russian winter seems to come alive in the sparkle of the music. This multi-coloured tableau by a consummate master of opera and ballet is imbued with the atmosphere of traditional festivities. Maslenitsa is a festival that dates back to pagan times, a celebration of winter. Traditionally, *scarecrows* would be burned and bonfires lit. When Christianity was introduced, the celebration would begin seven weeks before Easter, when butter and oil (moloko) were still permitted. The festival takes its name from these foods. In Russian Orthodox tradition, pancakes were made and festivities would last over a week. Igumnov lists the essential elements of the piece: carefree fun, festivities, performing bears and costumes. There is no doubt that Tchaïkovsky had considerable influence on Stravinsky's ballets. In Petrouchka and *Maslenitsa*, the Shrovetide fair is one of the key scenes featuring a dancing bear.

The *Carnival* is a hymn to life and to the very joy of existence. Such joy was fundamental to Tchaïkovsky, who said that despite life's trials, he loved it passionately.

### Mars: Chant de l'Alouette

*The field shimmering with flowers,*
*The stars twirling in the heavens,*
*The song of the lark fills the blue abyss.*

**Apollon Maykov**

This piece, merely two pages in length, is the shortest in the cycle and the first devoted to spring. Traces of snow linger in the fields, but nature is gradually reawakening after its long slumber. Sadows and melancholy give way to hope and desire. When months and seasons change, we may well reflect on the ephemeral nature of life and the youthful years we are gradually leaving behind. Such thoughts contribute to the elegiac nature of the piece. The tone signature, the sweeping accompaniment and the gradual fading of the image that vanishes like an illusion are all evocative of a mournful lullaby.

The main theme starts like a birdsong and leads into a melody as beautiful as it is enchanting. The central section takes us straight to the heart of the joyous spring, stirring to the audible twittering of a lark. The music gains speed, with its distinct rhythms twisting in the reprise, the elegiac character returns, but its fleeting melody only lasts through the last notes, repeated three times, before they disappear – a farewell lost in the utter silence of nature.

### Avril: Le Perce-neige

*The blue, pure snowdrop-flower,*
*And near it the last snowdrops,*
*The last tears over past griefs,*
*And first dreams of another happiness.*

**Apollon Maykov**

The only original manuscript of this cycle that has not survived is that of *April*. Snowdrop is one of the most popular pieces of the work. A gentle spring makes its presence felt through the ternary rhythm of the waltz, so beloved of Tchaïkovsky. Igumnov described this particularly subtle, poetic musical tableau as announcing spring. Here, notice the wild torrents, joyful birdsong or bright sunshine of Rachmaninov's Spring Waters (Voie Vesna). With Tchaïkovsky, all is muffled and understated. Snowdrop, the first flower to emerge shyly when the snow begins to melt, symbolizes the awakening of nature and a return to life. The piece represents the fragile, shifting Russian landscape of April. A long, melodic phrase, woven from brief, high-plying motifs, gives lightness and enthusiasm that beautifully conveys the exaltation of the first promises of quiet happiness. Despite its sparseness, the piece is a poem in itself, as humble as it is spellbinding.

### Mai: Les Nuits blanches

*What a night! What bliss is all about!*
*I thank my native north country!*
*From the kingdom of ice and snow,*
*How fresh and clear May flies in!*

**Afanasy Fet**

*May* is an extension of the theme of spring. The atmosphere, with its joyful calm yet understated sadness, has a delightfully ambivalent quality, typical of much of Tchaïkovsky's work. Here, perhaps like nowhere else in the cycle, the poetic epigraph is especially apt to the musical content.

*May* is notable because it alludes to the "white nights" emblematic of the city of Saint Petersburg, where Tchaïkovsky lived and died. The white nights, when the sun barely sets, have inspired many literary works, including Dostoyevsky's famous eponymous short story. Writing of this piano piece, Igumnov talks of its languor and voluptuousness. Even more, it invites us to contemplate the beauty of nature.

Like most of the pieces of this cycle, this miniature is written in ternary form (also called Lied form), including a central section and a repetition of the first section. Although the andante tempo indicated by the composer in the central and last parts suggests some movement, the underlying elegy encourages us to relish the enchanting romantic atmosphere of the central Allegro giocoso. "Giocoso" should be understood here as "with enthusiasm" or "with joy" rather than truly "amusing" or "playful." The apogee of this part is suggestive of infinity – a horizon revealed.

The piece ends in the serenity of a peaceful landscape under a white night of May.

### Juin: Barcarolle

*Let us go to the shore;*
*There the waves will kiss our legs,*
*With mysterious languor,*
*The stars will shine down on us.*

**Aleksey Pleshcheyev**

Unlike the nocturnes such as the White Nights and the *Barcarolle*, the *Reaper's Song* depicts a day of work in the open. Yet it is significant that half of the pieces in the cycle are lyrical. Tchaïkovsky portrays Russian landscapes and takes inspiration from his environment. January, March, June, September and October can all be conceived as introspective, poetical canvases. The other group of miniatures has more objectivity, sketching scenes of daily life at the time. Here, the focus is on ordinary people, their daily labours, their pastimes and their joys. The Reaper's Song is one of these. Both the name and the epigraph describe it aptly. It is a piece about the plenitude of life and the joys of work in the community.

A new artistic objective calls for new means of expression. In the *Barcarolle* and *Autumn Song*, the melody plays out like romances, while in *Reaper's Song*, the melody is more like a Russian folksong sung by a choir that resounds in the fields on a delightful July afternoon. Igumnov told his students that here, the timbre was primordial and everything should sound out.

There is a surprising parallel to this work: two hundred years earlier, François Couperin wrote *The Reapers* using similar techniques to portray an identical scene! The rhythmic counterpoint and the pulsation that beat through the two works reproduces the swaying of the peasants as they swing their scythes.

The central part is a type of instrumental intermezzo: the dynamic increasing and transforming it into a veritable hymn. In the third section, both the bass and the substance of the theme change to take on lyricism and poetry, culminating in a barely audible pianissimo, evocative of the reapers making their way through the fields into the distance. The best of their bending and swaying continues for a while even though the singing has already faded. The last chord blows out like a gust of hot air so typical of July.

### Juillet: Chant du Faucheur

*Move the shoulders, shake the arms!*
*And the noon wind breathes in the face!*

**Aleksey Koltsov**

This is another portrayal of peasant life – a bountiful harvest – with an epigraph taken from a poem by Aleksey Koltsov.

*The Harvest* is truly a virtuosic, symphonic scherzo, another testimony to Tchaïkovsky's mastery of orchestral colouration. In fact, the manuscript is entitled *Scherzo*. Its complex pianistic texture demands a high degree of technical and virtuosity. In the original manuscript, the central section is introduced by a dolce cantabile. The juxtaposition edition, however, approved by Tchaïkovsky, indicates tranquillo. The music in this episode contrasts starkly with the others: it might well be a summer nocturne, one of respite after a day's labour. This central section is one of the finest examples of Tchaïkovsky's unique lyricism. With the reprise, a new day of work gets off to an enthusiastic start.

### Août: La Moisson

*Are mounted on their horses;*
*In early dawn the horrors are jumping.*

**Alexandre Pushkin**

With the very first notes, we are drawn into the heart of a brightly coloured, golden autumn. Yet again, Tchaïkovsky's passionate love of nature and life shines through clearly. Some music critics linked this music to the words of Gorki:

Yes, at such times one desires to taste life to the full, and so to live that the ancient rocks shall smile, and the sea's white horses prance the higher, as one's mouth acclaims the earth in such a paean that, intoxicated with the laudation, it shall unfold its riches with added bountifulness and display more and more manifest beauty under the spur of the love expressed by one of it's creatures, expressed by a human being who feels for the earth what he would feel for a woman, and yearns to fertilize the same to ever-increasing splendour.

Tchaïkovsky succeeds in representing each and every aspect of the hunt in music, as if he were giving us a literal illustration of Pushkin's poetic epigraph. Yet again, we see the great symphony writer at work in these lines. It is easy to imagine an ensemble of wind instruments: the nature of the march in the first and last parts could even suggest a military brass band.

The central episode is a type of short orchestral intermezzo that displays incomparable skill and grace, giving the scene a new, almost fantastical colour altogether. Perhaps this is when the hunt actually begins. The listener imagines the pursuit through the forest, the coming and going of the hunters. We can make out the horses, hear the hunting horns. The dynamic energy that accumulates over a lengthy period bursts out in a frenzy that announces the triumphant return of the first theme.

Over the course of the pieces, the underlying meaning of the cycle becomes clear: appearing first as a tentative dream that unfurls into broad daylight, it is revealed as an ode to life. This is encapsulated in *The Hunt*. Time passing inexorably and the inevitability of death were Tchaïkovsky's most profound preoccupations. It is probably no coincidence that this seemingly modest piece finds a resounding echo in his grandiose Fourth Symphony, whose theme is destiny. It is also notable that this ode to life is followed by a profound, desperate lament, the *Autumn Song*. The dramatic climax of *The Seasons* lies in the contrast between these two pieces, and the postlude of September within the cycle can be thought of in terms of the "golden number".

### Septembre: La Chasse

*C'est l'heure!*
*Les trompes sonnent!*
*The pack is straining at the leash, the huntsmen on their steeds.*

**Alexandre Pouchkine**

Les premières notes nous plongent à elles seules au cœur des couleurs vives de l'automne dont « Une fine de plus » Le grand amour du compositeur pour la nature et la vie est clairement représenté. Certains critiques associent cette musique à ces mots de Gorki: « J'ai terriblement envie de vivre, vivre au point de faire rire les vieilles pierres et les cerises blanches des chevaux blancs de la mer toujours plus haut! » J'ai envie de chanter des louanges à la terre pour qu'enivrée de flatteries, elle soit encore plus généreuse avec ses trésors, elle se montre encore plus belle, attisée par l'amour de l'une de ses créations – l'homme – qui aime la terre comme une femme et qui est animé par l'envie de la féconder d'une beauté nouvelle. »

Comme s'il illustrait littéralement l'épigraphe poétique de Pouchkine, Tchaïkovski parvient à représenter en musique chaque aspect de la chasse. C'est encore un grand symphoniste que l'on devine derrière ces lignes. On imagine parfaitement un ensemble d'instruments à vent – le caractère de la marche de l'exposition et de la reprise suggère même une fanfare militaire. »

La partie centrale est une sorte de court intermezzo orchestral et fait preuve d'une maîtrise et d'une grâce inimitables. La scène prend alors une nouvelle teinte – quasi fantastique – et certains suggèrent que c'est à ce moment que la véritable chasse débute. Dans cette scène, on imagine des poursuites à travers la forêt, l'action qui s'éloigne et se rapproche ; on devine des chevaux, des cornes de chasse... Toute cette énergie dynamique longuement accumulée est libérée dans un paroxysme qui annonce le retour triomphal du premier thème.

### Octobre: Chant d'Automne

*C'est l'automne, notre pauvre jardin s'effeuille,*
*Les feuilles jaunes volent dans le vent... »*

**Alexis Tolstoï**

Cette pièce – l'une des plus célèbres et des plus aimées de Tchaïkovski – peut sans aucun doute être classée parmi les chefs-d'œuvre. Elle est tout à fait remarquable par l'émotion qu'elle suscite. Pour représenter l'automne, le compositeur n'a recouru qu'aux caractéristiques les plus simples et les plus pures du chant ou de la romance qui, contrastant vivement avec l'atmosphère des autres pièces, crée cette dissonance indispensable à Tchaïkovsky pour créer « la dissonance (écrit A. dans un article de l'Art du compositeur) est une immense force de la musique ; sans cela, elle serait condamnée à ne représenter que des blafards éternuements. Son cœur est obligé de représenter nos passions et nos tourments qui est prépondérante pour nous. » Dans cette pièce, Tchaïkovski nous livre ce chant de cette saison qui lui est si chère et qui, d'ailleurs, ne le plonge pas uniquement dans la mélancolie. Dans une lettre, il écrit : « Chaque jour, j'entreprends une longue marche à la recherche d'un coin confortable en forêt et je profite inlassablement de cet air saisonnier gorgé du parfum des feuilles mortes, du silence et du charme offerts par ces paysages d'automne... »

Cette pièce est sans nul doute l'une des plus précieuses miniatures de tout le répertoire pianistique.

### Novembre: Troïka

*Ne contemplons pas la route tristement,*
*Ne te hâte point de suivre la troïka,*
*La mélancolie dans ton cœur s'apaise,*
*Impulse-t-elle le silence à jamais.*

**Nikolaï Nekrassov**

Après la triste Chant d'automne, Troïka, écrite dans la tonalité lumineuse de mi majeur, redevient l'espoir. Il semble que ce traîneau rusé tiré par un trio de chevaux aux grelots si typiques, galope sur une route déjà enneigée. Le tintement des grelots éveille l'espoir, éloigne notre hêtres de la souffrance et l'emmène vers une nouvelle vie, de nouvelles joies, mais peut-être aussi de nouvelles peines... Et bien que la pièce soit écrite en majeur, elle comporte de légères nuances en mineur, comme si l'ombre de la pièce précédente n'avait pas totalement disparu... D'une manière ou d'une autre, on devine cette volonté d'étouffer la mélancolie lancinante et de se perdre dans la gaieté du son des grelots sur cette route qui semble infinie. Cette atmosphère est très représentative de la mentalité russe dans toute la tradition, ce qui est inhérent à la musique russe – particulièrement dans celles de Tchaïkovski et de Rachmaninov. Dans une certaine mesure, la troïka est un puissant symbole de tout ce qu'est la Russie. Tous les sentiments et les émotions s'expriment dans cet extraordinaire thème principal qui est une chanson folklorique de cocher (pesnya yamshchika).

Comme le lecteur et l'auditeur l'ont probablement remarqué, nous citons souvent dans ces commentaires Konstantin Igumnov, un des plus brillants et émouvants interprètes de la musique de Tchaïkovski. Mais la pièce Troïka est étroitement liée au nom d'un autre musicien russe : Sergueï Rachmaninov. Vouant une admiration à Tchaïkovski, Rachmaninov, encore enfant, jouait les Saisons avec ses camarades de classe à Tchaïkovski lui-même, qui ne manquait pas de les féliciter. Ces moments d'exception avaient lieu dans la maison de Nikolaï Zverev, premier professeur de piano de Rachmaninov au Conservatoire de Moscou. Après avoir quitté à tout jamais la Russie après la révolution de 1917, Rachmaninov n'enregistrera qu'une seule et unique pièce des Saisons – Troïka. Cette empreinte du génie de Rachmaninov n'est pas seulement l'une des plus extraordinaires interprétations de cette pièce mais elle est probablement devenue une allégorie de Rachmaninov lui-même.

Voici ce que le compositeur Dmitri Chostakovitch écrit dans un article consacré au centième anniversaire de Rachmaninov : « Après l'incroyable héritage discographique que nous a laissé Rachmaninov, il y a une œuvre que l'on ne peut s'empêcher de citer sans être bouleversé. Une œuvre qui n'est pas de lui. Je fais allusion à une pièce des Saisons de Tchaïkovski : Troïka. Rachmaninov l'incarne totalement comme s'il l'avait lui-même écrite. Et si nous ne savions rien de sa vie ou de ce qu'il éprouvait hors de nos frontières, Troïka est criante de vérité. Rachmaninov y concentre toute la nostalgie qu'il éprouve pour son pays, toute sa terrible solitude malgré sa renommée à travers le monde, ses succès, ses innombrables tournées. Pour Rachmaninov, Troïka évoque tous les souvenirs du pays et ce passé irrévocable. »

La partie centrale respire l'enthousiasme, la gaieté et évoque la danse populaire. C'est ici qu'apparaît l'image du traîneau, des grelots et le son des sabots qui finiront par disparaître avant le chant lui-même ; et ce n'est que grâce au vent que nous parviendront les échos de plus en plus faibles de cette troïka qui s'éloigne...

### Décembre: Noël

*La veille de Noël, des jeunes filles lisaient l'avenir ;*
*Elles retirèrent leurs souliers et les jetèrent dehors.*

**Vassili Joukovski**

Si janvier fait figure de prologue, Décembre est l'épilogue du cycle. Ces deux pièces sont liées comme par un fil secret, nous plongeant dans l'atmosphère chaleureuse et le confort d'une maison de maître. Noël est l'une des pièces les plus poétiques du cycle. Heinrich Neuhaus a dit à propos de la 31ème Sonate de Beethoven : « Le bémol majeur est la tonalité de la pudeur. » C'est dans cette tonalité que cette dernière pièce est écrite. Cette conclusion donne la parole à la poésie et à l'espérance. « Nous sommes arrivés à la dernière pièce des Scènes d'enfants de Schumann, Le Poète parle. Le cycle annuel de la nature arrive à sa fin, et l'inévitable nous dit, en se retournant sur tout le parcours, que personne n'est en mesure d'arrêter le cours des choses, dans lequel toutes les peines, les tourments et les souffrances humaines finissent par se perdre... »

### Deux Nocturnes

Parmi la centaine de compositions pour piano écrites par Tchaïkovski, on ne trouve que deux Nocturnes : celui en fa majeur, écrit en 1872 et faisant partie de l'opus 10 aux côtés de son célèbre Humoresque, et le Nocturne en do dièse mineur, composé l'année suivante, et figurant parmi les six pièces de l'opus 19. Ces Nocturnes sont deux extraordinaires témoignages du prodigieux lyrisme de Tchaïkovski.

Si le premier Nocturne en fa majeur est une sorte d'épigraphe et installe une atmosphère poétique en accord avec le thème de l'œuvre ; le second, en do dièse mineur – véritable « misgridise » – prolonge l'impression de récit qui vient s'achever. Ils peuvent être perçus comme des prières, des prières du matin et du soir – un hommage à l'Album pour enfants de Tchaïkovski s'ouvrant et s'achevant respectivement par Prière du matin et à l'Eglise.

Dans celui composé dans la tonalité chaude et pastorale de fa majeur, on trouve deux images très différentes : une voix qui improvise librement accompagnée sobrement d'une guitare ; et un chœur étouffé comme s'il provenait d'une église – cette impression est rendue en superposant des accords diatoniques sur une basse obstinato. Les passages de certains Nocturnes de Chopin dont on retrouve le même procédé et évoquent, eux aussi, des chants religieux.

Le thème choral est répété deux fois dans des tonalités différentes : il nous est proposé la première fois au travers d'une dynamique intense, qui conduit au retour de cette voix soliste, agrémentée, cette fois, d'un subtil motif contrapuntique. Après une courte cadence, le chœur revient de nouveau, dans la tonalité principale et de manière plus apaisée. Il s'élève de plus en plus haut jusqu'à ce qu'il se perde dans les cieux.

Le second Nocturne, en do dièse mineur, se compose en triptyque, comme la plupart des pièces des Saisons.

La simplicité et la sincérité absolue de cette musique, l'extraordinaire beauté de sa mélodie, sa texture délicate et gracieuse ont fait de ce Nocturne l'une des pièces les plus populaires de Tchaïkovski. Il a fait l'objet de nombreux arrangements pour tous les instruments et est très souvent interprété au violoncelle.

L'intimité des Nocturnes, leur brièveté et leur simplicité, nous plongent dans l'atmosphère musicale d'un foyer de l'époque où jouer de la musique était très apprécié par la noblesse russe. La plupart des miniatures de Tchaïkovski étaient destinées à être jouées dans des salons. La plupart des pièces des Saisons sont façonnées par la même simplicité, l'intimité et la chaleur humaine. Le choix de ces deux Nocturnes et leur disposition respective autour des Saisons paraît particulièrement judicieuse et organique.

## English Liner Notes

### The Seasons

The story of this work is nothing short of remarkable. Tchaikovsky composed the piano cycle some 145 years ago and the enthusiasm it garners worldwide has not abated since. The pieces are popular among professional musicians, amateurs and children alike.

It was not Tchaikovsky himself who had the idea for the cycle. The suggestion came from Nikolay Bernard, the editor of the women's magazine *Nouvelist*, published in Saint Petersburg. The magazine appeared monthly and for each issue in 1876. For each piece, Bernard suggested both a title and a short epigraph of Russian poetry. Tchaikovsky readily agreed to all his proposals. Only after the last issue of the year was published in December did the editor publish the twelve pieces together, in a separate issue entitled *The Seasons*. Thus the first edition of Opus 37a was born. It was an instant success. One of the first countries outside Russia where it was published was France. In fact, one day Tchaikovsky chanced upon the French edition in the window of a Saint Petersburg music shop.

Although numerous pianists have played several of its pieces, the entire cycle has rarely been recorded. *The Seasons* is indissociable from Konstantin Igumnov (1873-1948), an outstanding pianist and renowned pedagogue at the Moscow Conservatory. Heinrich Neuhaus, an equally famous pianist and teacher of that same conservatory, said of Tchaikovsky's music was accessible, it could not be considered easy. It requires not only strength and gentleness, but also sensitivity and refinement. According to Neuhaus, no one was better than Igumnov at rendering such a wide palette of sounds, with a forbright, sincere intonation, played with indescribable charm.

Konstantin Igumnov was probably the first pianist to play the entire oeuvre of *The Seasons*. Once we have acquired an overview of the whole, it becomes clear that each piece is a part of a single narrative with a particular dramatic development. The images of nature and the scenes of Russian life are arranged to fit an allegory of human life. Igumnov saw this work as a "burning confession of the human soul." The poetry of time passing, the melancholy of the Russian spirit, sincerity, intimacy and elegance all contribute to the very essence of this work.

### January: By the Hearth

*A little corner of peaceful bliss*
*The night dressed in twilight,*
*The little fire is dying in the fireplace,*
*And the candle has burned out.*

**— Alexander Pushkin**

The first piece, *By the Hearth*, sets the scene for the poetical atmosphere of *The Seasons*. The epigraph, taken from Pushkin's *Drooms*, immediately establishes the character of the piece, conjuring up a snowy winter evening. During the Russian winter, days are extremely short, and so people in the past enjoyed long evenings at the fireside. The burning flames would awaken memories and fantasies. Igumnov tells us about his childhood in the small town of Lebedyan in the Lipetsk region. In his home there was a fireplace, and as children they would play in the firelight. He sat on the floor, letting their imaginations run free. Dreaming is at the heart of this piece. In one of his letters Tchaikovsky describes a melancholy feeling: it is the nostalgia which seizes over us when at evening we sit indoors alone, weary of work, while the book we have picked up for relaxation slips unheeded from our finger. A long procession of old memories goes by. How sad to think how much is already past and gone! And yet these recollections of youth are sweet. We regret the past, although we have neither courage nor desire to start a new life. We are rather weary of existence. We would fain rest awhile and look back, recalling many things. There were moments when young blood pulsed warm through our veins and life gave us all we asked. There were also moments of sorrow, irreparable loss. All this has receded so far into the past. How sad, yet sweet to lose ourselves therein!

### February: The Carnival

*At the lively Mardi Gras*
*soon a large feast will overflow.*

**— Pavel Vyazemsky**

*The Carnival* or *Mardi Gras* (Maslenitsa) stands in stark contrast to the preceding piece. In a letter to his editor, Tchaikovsky wrote that he composed the two pieces in one sitting.

In this piece, the frosty air of the Russian winter seems to come alive in the sparkle of the music. This multi-coloured tableau by a consummate master of opera and ballet is imbued with the atmosphere of traditional festivities. Maslenitsa is a festival that dates back to pagan times, a celebration of winter. Traditionally, *scarecrows* would be burned and bonfires lit. When Christianity was introduced, the celebration would begin seven weeks before Easter, when butter and oil (moloko) were still permitted. The festival takes its name from these foods. In Russian Orthodox tradition, pancakes were made and festivities would last over a week. Igumnov lists the essential elements of the piece: carefree fun, festivities, performing bears and costumes. There is no doubt that Tchaikovsky had considerable influence on Stravinsky's ballets. In *Petrouchka* and *Maslenitsa*, the Shrovetide fair is one of the key scenes featuring a dancing bear.

The *Carnival* is a hymn to life and to the very joy of existence. Such joy was fundamental to Tchaikovsky, who said that despite life's trials, he loved it passionately.

### March: Song of the Lark

*The field shimmering with flowers,*
*The stars twirling in the heavens,*
*The song of the lark fills the blue abyss.*

**— Apollon Maykov**

This piece, merely two pages in length, is the shortest in the cycle and the first devoted to spring. Traces of snow linger in the fields, but nature is gradually reawakening after its long slumber. Sadness and melancholy give way to hope and desire. When months and seasons change, we may well reflect on the ephemeral nature of life and the youthful years we are gradually leaving behind. Such thoughts contribute to the elegiac nature of the piece. The tone signature, the sweeping accompaniment and the gradual fading of the image that vanishes like an illusion are all evocative of a mournful lullaby.

The main theme starts like a birdsong and leads into a melody as beautiful as it is enchanting. The central section takes us straight to the heart of the joyous spring, stirring to the audible twittering of a lark. The music gains speed, with its distinct rhythms twisting in the reprise, the elegiac character returns, but its fleeting melody only lasts through the last notes, repeated three times, before they disappear – a farewell lost in the utter silence of nature.

### April: Snowdrop

*The blue, pure snowdrop-flower,*
*And near it the last snowdrops,*
*The last tears over past griefs,*
*And first dreams of another happiness.*

**— Apollon Maykov**

The only original manuscript of this cycle that has not survived is that of *April*. *Snowdrop* is one of the most popular pieces of the work. A gentle spring makes its presence felt through the ternary rhythm of the waltz, so beloved of Tchaikovsky. Igumnov described this particularly subtle, poetic musical tableau as announcing spring. Here, notice the wild torrents, joyful birdsong or bright sunshine of Rachmaninov's *Spring Waters* (Vesniye Vody). With Tchaikovsky, all is muffled and understated. *Snowdrop*, the first flower to emerge shyly when the snow begins to melt, symbolizes the awakening of nature and a return to life. The piece represents the fragile, shifting Russian landscape of April. A long, melodic phrase, woven from brief, high-plying motifs, gives lightness and enthusiasm that beautifully conveys the exaltation of the first promises of quiet happiness. Despite its sparseness, the piece is a poem in itself, as humble as it is spellbinding.

### May: White Nights

*What a night! What bliss is all about!*
*I thank my native north country!*
*From the kingdom of ice and snow,*
*How fresh and clear May flies in!*

**— Afanasy Fet**

*May* is an extension of the theme of spring. The atmosphere, with its joyful calm yet understated sadness, has a delightfully ambivalent quality, typical of much of Tchaikovsky's work. Here, perhaps like nowhere else in the cycle, the poetic epigraph is especially apt to the musical content.

*May* is notable because it alludes to the "white nights" emblematic of the city of Saint Petersburg, where Tchaikovsky lived and died. The white nights, when the sun barely sets, have inspired many literary works, including Dostoyevsky's famous eponymous short story. Writing of this piano piece, Igumnov talks of its languor and voluptuousness. Even more, it invites us to contemplate the beauty of nature.

Like most of the pieces of this cycle, this miniature is written in ternary form (also called Lied form), including a central section and a repetition of the first section. Although the andante tempo indicated by the composer in the central and last parts suggests some movement, the underlying elegy encourages us to relish the enchanting romantic atmosphere of the central Allegro giocoso. "Giocoso" should be understood here as "with enthusiasm" or "with joy" rather than truly "amusing" or "playful." The apogee of this part is suggestive of infinity – a horizon revealed.

The piece ends in the serenity of a peaceful landscape under a white night of May.

### June: Barcarolle

*Let us go to the shore;*
*There the waves will kiss our legs,*
*With mysterious languor,*
*The stars will shine down on us.*

**— Aleksey Pleshcheyev**

Unlike the nocturnes such as the *White Nights* and the *Barcarolle*, the *Reaper's Song* depicts a day of work in the open. Yet it is significant that half of the pieces in the cycle are lyrical. Tchaikovsky portrays Russian landscapes and takes inspiration from his environment. *January*, *March*, *June*, *September* and *October* can all be conceived as introspective, poetical canvases. The other group of miniatures has more objectivity, sketching scenes of daily life at the time. Here, the focus is on ordinary people, their daily labours, their pastimes and their joys. The *Reaper's Song* is one of these. Both the name and the epigraph describe it aptly. It is a piece about the plenitude of life and the joys of work in the community.

A new artistic objective calls for new means of expression. In the *Barcarolle* and *Autumn Song*, the melody plays out like romances, while in *Reaper's Song*, the melody is more like a Russian folksong sung by a choir that resounds in the fields on a delightful July afternoon. Igumnov told his students that here, the timbre was primordial and everything should sound out.

There is a surprising parallel to this work: two hundred years earlier, François Couperin wrote *The Reapers* using similar techniques to portray an identical scene! The rhythmic counterpoint and the pulsation that beat through the two works reproduces the swaying of the peasants as they swing their scythes.

The central part is a type of instrumental intermezzo: the dynamic increasing and transforming it into a veritable hymn. In the third section, both the bass and the substance of the theme change to take on lyricism and poetry, culminating in a barely audible pianissimo, evocative of the reapers making their way through the fields into the distance. The best of their bending and swaying continues for a while even though the singing has already faded. The last chord blows out like a gust of hot air so typical of July.

### July: Reaper's Song

*Move the shoulders, shake the arms!*
*And the noon wind breathes in the face!*

**— Aleksey Koltsov**

This is another portrayal of peasant life – a bountiful harvest – with an epigraph taken from a poem by Aleksey Koltsov.

*The Harvest* is truly a virtuosic, symphonic scherzo, another testimony to Tchaikovsky's mastery of orchestral colouration. In fact, the manuscript is entitled *Scherzo*. Its complex pianistic texture demands a high degree of technical and virtuosity. In the original manuscript, the central section is introduced by a *dolce cantabile*. The juxtaposition edition, however, approved by Tchaikovsky, indicates *tranquillo*. The music in this episode contrasts starkly with the others: it might well be a summer nocturne, one of respite after a day's labour. This central section is one of the finest examples of Tchaikovsky's unique lyricism. With the reprise, a new day of work gets off to an enthusiastic start.

### August: The Harvest

*Are mounted on their horses;*
*In early dawn the horrors are jumping.*

**— Alexandre Pushkin**

With the very first notes, we are drawn into the heart of a brightly coloured, golden autumn. Yet again, Tchaikovsky's passionate love of nature and life shines through clearly. Some music critics linked this music to the words of Gorki:

> Yes, at such times one desires to taste life to the full, and so to live that the ancient rocks shall smile, and the sea's white horses prance the higher, as one's mouth acclaims the earth in such a paean that, intoxicated with the laudation, it shall unfold its riches with added bountifulness and display more and more manifest beauty under the spur of the love expressed by one of it's creatures, expressed by a human being who feels for the earth what he would feel for a woman, and yearns to fertilize the same to ever-increasing splendour.

Tchaikovsky succeeds in representing each and every aspect of the hunt in music, as if he were giving us a literal illustration of Pushkin's poetic epigraph. Yet again, we see the great symphony writer at work in these lines. It is easy to imagine an ensemble of wind instruments: the nature of the march in the first and last parts could even suggest a military brass band.

The central episode is a type of short orchestral intermezzo that displays incomparable skill and grace, giving the scene a new, almost fantastical colour altogether. Perhaps this is when the hunt actually begins. The listener imagines the pursuit through the forest, the coming and going of the hunters. We can make out the horses, hear the hunting horns. The dynamic energy that accumulates over a lengthy period bursts out in a frenzy that announces the triumphant return of the first theme.

Over the course of the pieces, the underlying meaning of the cycle becomes clear: appearing first as a tentative dream that unfurls into broad daylight, it is revealed as an ode to life. This is encapsulated in *The Hunt*. Time passing inexorably and the inevitability of death were Tchaikovsky's most profound preoccupations. It is probably no coincidence that this seemingly modest piece finds a resounding echo in his grandiose Fourth Symphony, whose theme is destiny. It is also notable that this ode to life is followed by a profound, desperate lament, the *Autumn Song*. The dramatic climax of *The Seasons* lies in the contrast between these two pieces, and the postlude of September within the cycle can be thought of in terms of the "golden number".

### September: The Hunt

*It is time! The horns are sounding!*
*The hunters in their hunting dress*

**— Alexander Pushkin**

The first notes alone plunge us into the heart of the vivid colours of autumn. The composer's great love for nature and life is clearly represented. Some critics associate this music with these words of Gorki: "I have a terrible desire to live, to live to the point of making the old stones laugh and the white horses of the sea prance ever higher! I have a desire to sing praises to the earth so that, intoxicated with flattery, it will be even more generous with its treasures, it will show itself even more beautiful, spurred on by the love of one of its creations – man – who loves the earth like a woman and is animated by the desire to fertilize it with a new beauty."

As if literally illustrating Pushkin's poetic epigraph, Tchaikovsky manages to represent in music every aspect of the hunt. Once again, we see the great symphonist behind these lines. One can perfectly imagine an ensemble of wind instruments – the character of the march in the exposition and reprise even suggests a military fanfare.

The central part is a sort of short orchestral intermezzo that displays incomparable skill and grace. The scene then takes on a new hue – almost fantastical – and some suggest that this is when the true hunt begins. In this scene, one imagines pursuits through the forest, action that moves away and draws nearer; one makes out horses, hunting horns... All this long-accumulated dynamic energy is released in a paroxysm that announces the triumphant return of the first theme.

### October: Autumn Song

*Autumn, our poor garden is shedding its leaves,*
*The yellow leaves are flying in the wind...*

**— Aleksey Tolstoy**

This piece – one of Tchaikovsky's most famous and most popular pieces and may be considered one of his masterpieces. Requitingly emotional, it represents autumn through the simplest and purest traits of song and romance. Yet it is so expressive that the listener is moved to the core. October is not a depiction of a golden autumn with glowing colours like that of *The Hunt*. Rather, it takes its inspiration from an autumn that is reaching its end.

This *Barcarolle* is most certainly one of the best-known and most appealing pieces of *The Seasons*. It is one of those pieces that touches the hearts of countless human souls, encouraging us to connect with our innermost feelings. The poetry and fragrances of summer nights are conveyed with boundless tenderness. After reading the epigraph, with its mention of the mysterious sadness of the stars, simply let yourself be carried away as you listen to the murmurs of celestial desolation.

Of course, the barcarolle was originally a song sung by Venetian gondoliers. The form has inspired countless musical works, both for instruments and for the voice. Tchaikovsky's composition is the barcarolle of the Venice of the North, as Saint Petersburg is known. Describing this piece, Igumnov remarks how evocative it is of a small Russian town's summer evening: rolling waves and twinkling stars. This song seems to emerge from a dream, or perhaps from a charming romance. To depict the water in the music, Tchaikovsky uses the technique of arpeggiation.

The vertible barcarolle appears in the central section, in a major key, conveying the distinctive swaying of the boat and its reflections on the water. It gradually unfolds until it reaches an apex worthy of an operatic scene, only to break up in an outpouring of tragic chords characteristic of the symphonic genius of the composer and his profound sense of tragedy.

The piece concludes in a song about the beauty of the world and the happiness it contains despite the difficulty of human existence. It resonates with resilience.

### November: On the Troika

*In your loneliness do not look at the road,*
*And do not rush out after the troika,*
*Suppress at once and forever*
*The fear of longing in your heart.*

**— Nikolay Nekrassov**

After the sadness of *Autumn Song*, the *Troika*, in the luminous E-major key, represents hope. It is as if a sleigh drawn by three galloping horses, bells a-tinkling, takes us onwards towards a new life. The road may well be covered in snow already, but there will be new joys... perhaps, too, new heartaches. Although the piece is written in a major key, touches of minor keys are discernible, like remnants of the previous piece. Whatever the case may be, we perceive the composer's intention of muffled the haunting melancholy to abandon himself to the joy of the bells jingling along the seemingly endless road. The atmosphere is representative of the Russian temperament reflected throughout the literature, poetry and music of the country, in particular the music of Tchaikovsky and Rachmaninov. The *Troika* here is not a strong symbol of all that is Russian. The sentiments and emotions are expressed in the wonderful main theme, a coachman's folksong (*pesnya yamshchika*).

We have frequently cited Konstantin Igumnov here. His moving interpretations of Tchaikovsky's compositions are among the most brilliant ever made. But on the *Troika* is inextricably linked with another Russian musician, no other than Sergei Rachmaninov. He venerated Tchaikovsky and as a child played the *The Seasons* to Tchaikovsky himself along with his classmates, earning great praise from the composer. These recitals took place in the home of Nikolai Zverev, Rachmaninov's first piano teacher at the Moscow Conservatory. After Rachmaninov left Russia for good after the 1917 Revolution, he recorded only a single piece of *The Seasons* from the *Troika*. It is an exceptionally brilliant recording, one of the finest ever, and seems to have become an allegory of Rachmaninov's life.

Writing on the occasion of Rachmaninov's centenary, Shostakovich marvelled at his wonderful recorded heritage. This short piece would not fall to move any listener profoundly, although it was not one of his compositions. Rachmaninov's playing, in Shostakovich's opinion, embodied the work as if he had written it himself. Although he knew nothing of the life Rachmaninov lead, or what he felt once he left Russia. On the *Troika*, said Shostakovich, is the interpreter that we can hear, echoing all his nostalgia for his country, his extreme solitude despite the fame he enjoyed worldwide, his success, and his countless tours. For Rachmaninov, *On the Troika* conjured up all his memories of his homeland and a past he had lost forever.

The central section breathes enthusiasm, gaiety and evokes the folk dance. It is here that the image of the sleigh, the bells and the sound of hooves that fade away before the song itself. Only thanks to the wind we hear the increasingly distant echoes of the troika as it disappears...

### December: Christmas Tide

*Once upon a Christmas night*
*The girls were telling fortunes;*
*Taking their slippers off their feet*
*And throwing them out of the gate.*

**— Vasily Zhukovsky**

If January is the prologue to the cycle and December, the epilogue. These two pieces are like kinsmen spirits, drawing us into the warmth and comfort of a large house. *Christmas Tide* is one of the most poetic pieces of the cycle. Writing of Beethoven's Sonata No. 31, Heinrich Neuhaus said that A-flat major is the key of modesty. It is the key Tchaikovsky used for this piece.

The conclusion, where the poet is given the floor, is in our view similar to Schumann's last piece of *Scenes from Childhood*, *The Poet Speaks*. Nature's yearly cycle is coming to an end. The author, looking back on the path his life has taken, seems to be telling us that no one can stop the course of the world. All heartbreaks, all torments and all human suffering ultimately disappear. Something fragile and elusive makes itself felt. The cycle, rather than offering a conclusion, is left unresolved.

"Disonance," he wrote in an article entitled *The Art of Composing (Kompozitorskoe Masterstvo)*, "is a vast strength in music. Without it, it would be condemned to representing nothing but perpetual beatitude. But what is most important for us is its capacity to represent pain and torment. It is one of his letters, he wrote: "Every day, I take a long walk in search of a comfortable spot in the forest. I never tire of enjoying the air of the season, so redolent of dead leaves, silence, and the delights that the autumn scenes offer up to our eyes."

This piece is without doubt one of the most precious miniatures of the entire piano repertoire.

### Two Nocturnes

Among Tchaikovsky's one hundred or so piano compositions, there are only two Nocturnes: the Nocturne in F Major, Opus 10, composed in 1872, part of his Opus 10 that included the famous *Humoresque*, and the Nocturne in C-sharp Minor, written the following year, one of the six pieces of Opus 19. These two Nocturnes are outstanding testimony to Tchaikovsky's prodigious lyricism.

The first Nocturne in F Major could be considered an epigraph, setting the poetic atmosphere fitting to the beginning of *The Seasons*. The second, in C-sharp minor, a previous little treat or moodpiece, prolongs the impression of the narrative that has just finished. The Nocturnes can also be seen as morning and evening prayers – a nod to his own *Children's Album* that opens with the *Morning Prayer* and closes with *The Church*.

In the F Major Nocturne, with its warm, pastoral key, there are two very different images: a voice that improvises freely to the sober accompaniment of a guitar; and a choir, muffled as though emerging from a church. The impression is rendered by superimposing diatonic chords on a basso ostinato. Some of Chopin's Nocturnes use the same procedure and are also evocative of religious chants. The choral theme is repeated twice in different keys, the first time in an intense dynamic that takes us back to the solo voice, embellished now with a subtle contrapuntal motif. After a short cadence, the choir sounds out again in the principal key, more appeased now, rising ever higher until it loses itself in the skies.

The second Nocturne, in C-sharp minor, is a musical triptych, like most of the pieces of *The Seasons*. The sheer simplicity and sincerity of this music, the extraordinary beauty of its melody, and its gracious texture have made it one of Tchaikovsky's most popular pieces. It has been arranged for all other instruments and is often played in its cello version.

These intimate Nocturnes, brief and simple, take us into the musical atmosphere of a noble home of the time, where playing an instrument was a popular activity. Most of Tchaikovsky's miniatures were intended to be played in such salons, and most of the pieces of *The Seasons* were conceived with the same simplicity and personal warmth. The choice of these two Nocturnes, one before and one after *The Seasons*, speaks for itself in this album.

## Vladimir Tropp Biography

Vladimir Tropp was born in Moscow in 1939. He studied at the celebrated Gnessin Music School from an early age. His teachers included the distinguished Russian Moisey Fegins and Theodor Gutman, both pupils of Heinrich Neuhaus. While still a student, Tropp began giving recitals and performing with orchestras. He was awarded the prestigious Rimsky-Korsakov scholarship.

In 1963 Tropp graduated with distinction from the Gnessin State Musical Institute, since named the Gnessin Russian Academy of Music. He continued his graduate studies while teaching there. Today he is known as one of the leading professors of this major institution of professional music education. In 1997 he was appointed chair of the Academy's piano department. For the last ten years he has also taught at the R.I. Tchaikovsky Moscow State Conservatory. It is his teaching Professor Tropp strives to maintain the finest traditions of the Russian school of piano playing. Several of his students have won major international competitions.

In 1970 Tropp was a laureate of the International Enesco Piano Competition in Bucharest. Since then, he has constantly performed and held master classes in Russia and many other countries: the Netherlands, Italy, Japan, Finland, the USA, Germany, the UK, Ireland, France, Czechoslovakia, Portugal, Canada, Costa Rica, Taiwan and Southern Korea. Romantic music comprises the major part of his repertoire. His performances of works by Rachmaninov, Scriabin and Medtner are widely acclaimed.

The celebrated pianist Lazar Berman wrote that Tropp "is a singular and subtle artist. Sincerity, an outstanding sense of poetry, improvisatory freedom, rhythmic flexibility, a highly coloured pianistic sound – these are the essential traits of the powerful artistic persona of Vladimir Tropp."

Tropp has made a series of radio, LP and CD recordings. From 1995 onwards the Devon Company (Japan) produced ten CDs of works by Schumann, Chopin, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Rachmaninov, Scriabin and Medtner. In 1998, one of his series of three CDs entitled *Russian Miniatures* was awarded the prestigious Record Academy prize for best classical recording of the year.

Tropp is a musician with wide-ranging interests. He has published work on the performing arts of Russia and other countries, as well as articles on the recordings of outstanding performers, and made a series of radio broadcasts. Most prominent among Professor Tropp's activities has been the study of Sergei Rachmaninov's creative work. He has examined all extant archives of the composer in the United States, Russia, Switzerland and Costa Rica, publishing articles and writing and directing television films about him.

Tropp is vice-president of the Rachmaninov Society and a board member of the Scriabin Society in Russia. He is a member of the Teodor Leschetizki and Scriabin Societies in the USA and the Glenn Gould Society in Canada. He has played solo and chamber music at music festivals in Kulmo (Finland), Portogruaro (Italy) and elsewhere. He regularly serves on juries of international and national piano competitions, including the F. Lamond International Competition in Glasgow, the International Piano Competition in Hamamatsu (Japan) and the Rachmaninov International Piano Competition in Moscow.
