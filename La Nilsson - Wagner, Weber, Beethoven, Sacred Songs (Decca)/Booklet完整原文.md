## 专辑介绍
"Comfortable shoes." According to Swedish soprano Birgit Nilsson, having them was one of the chief requirements for singing the role of Isolde. Nobody knew better than Nilsson that there was a great deal more to singing Isolde than comfortable shoes, but such a comment was typical of her practical, down-to-earth attitude. After all, here was a woman who was literally a farmer's daughter, and who, later in life, would summon her six cats to meals with a ringing high C.

Her first official appearance on the operatic stage was in Stockholm, in the role of Agathe in Der Freischütz. This was in October 1946, and, with only five days notice, she was substituting for a colleague. As the performance neared, she was so overcome with nerves she briefly considered jumping off a bridge. Of course she did not, and the performance went off, if not without a hitch, then at least without any major incidents. The newspapers praised her voice, her intelligence, and her bearing. She was on her way. The following spring, she made her first recording: an aria from an opera by Franz Berwald. Again, she was replacing another singer, who was indisposed. Her next recording, also a 78 rpm disc, was 'Dich teure Halle', Elisabeth's greeting to the Hall of Song from Wagner's Tannhäuser.

While a student at the Royal Academy of Music in Stockholm, Nilsson had dreamed of singing the role of Elisabeth, and her opportunity to do so on stage came in 1953, not long after her recovery from a bout with tuberculosis. Nilsson was dissatisfied with her interpretation, however. "It was not until several years later," she wrote in her autobiography, "when I sang the seductive Venus and the pure Elisabeth in the same performance, that I succeeded in capturing the innermost meaning of Elisabeth." Later that year, she sang her first Isolde – the role with which she came to be identified, apart from Brünnhilde and Turandot.

She also added Beethoven's Fidelio to her repertoire around this time. Nilsson was a devoted wife – she married the veterinarian Bertil Niklasson in 1948, and they remained married until her death. In other words, she had a basis for understanding the role of Leonore, whose love for her unjustly imprisoned husband is so great that she disguises herself as the young man Fidelio and risks her life to save him. Ah, perfido!, Beethoven's Mozartean concert aria portraying the various emotions of a woman who has been abandoned by her lover, was part of her training at the Royal Academy of Music.

Birgit Nilsson's voice is instantly recognizable. To paraphrase the title of a song by Sibelius that was in her repertoire, it is like a diamond on the March snow. John Ardoin recalls how easily her voice "pierced the uppermost reaches of the Met like a laser beam," and how it "always seemed bigger and more tinted than it actually was". Something of this penetrating power can be heard in Elsa's aria from Lohengrin, or in Agathe's aria: rather than a wave of sound, Nilsson focuses it into a point, and so even her quiet singing always finds its mark. She actually preferred singing in large, deep theaters, claiming that it was more tiring to sing in small ones.

Of course Nilsson had voice teachers when she was young, but for the most part, she did not have happy experiences with them, and it probably would not be unfair to say that what made her singing unique was mostly self-taught. In her autobiography, she wrote, "There must always be an air column that carries the tone into the mask, like a ping-pong ball bouncing on top of a fountain. (The 'mask' is a term singers use to describe the sensation of singing from the face or the sinuses directly behind it.) When Nilsson sang loudly, it didn't sound forced, thanks in large part to her innate understanding of how to use her muscles to support the tone. This ability is displayed particularly well in sustained passages, such as in much of the latter part of 'Abscheulicher!', or in the climax of Reiza's aria from Oberon.

Nilsson was comfortable in recording studios. She wrote, 'The camaraderie that grows out of making recordings affords one a lifetime of memories.' She was fortunate to have recorded for Decca during the 1950s and 60s, when the label's pioneering use of technology meant that operas could be captured on LP with greater realism than ever before. She admitted, though, that she missed the inspiration given by a live audience, and preferred live recordings. At the same time, she disliked 'pirate' recordings, calling them 'the bane of the music industry', and she was dismayed when, after her retirement, she was presented with a 27-page list of unsanctioned recordings bearing her name.

Her professional relationship with Rudolf Bing, general manager of the Metropolitan Opera between 1950 and 1972, was sometimes contentious. Usually, the arguments concerned money. Nilsson's famous sense of humor was at its best here. When discussing her income tax return, Nilsson was asked if she had any dependents. 'Just one,' she is said to have replied, 'Rudolf Bing'. For his part, when Bing was asked if Nilsson was difficult, he supposedly answered, 'Not at all. You put enough money in and a glorious sound comes out.' Clearly affection was intermixed with their squabbles. When Bing retired from the Met in 1972, he requested that Nilsson close a gala evening in his honour with her performance of the final scene from Salome. 'As added excitement,' he told her, 'you can bring my head in on the silver platter.'

Nilsson's colleague Astrid Varnay also could attest to Nilsson's sense of humour. Once they were performing together in Elektra, with Nilsson in the title role and Varnay as the haunted Klytämnestra. One day, while Varnay was walking to the theatre, behind her on the sidewalk she heard "an abrasively cranky child whining incessantly, 'Mommy, mommy…' When I finally decided I could ignore this no more and turned around in exasperation to beg the parent to pay a little attention to the yammering kid, I realised that the 'brat' was my friend Birgit."

There is no truth in the story that tenor Franco Corelli, after losing a battle with Nilsson to see who could hold a high C longer, actually bit the soprano during a Met production of Turandot. Nilsson claims that Bing tried to put him up to it, however, so she sent Bing a telegram: "MUST CANCEL NEXT PERFORMANCE STOP SERIOUS BITE INJURY STOP BIRGIT."

The LP recorded with Sir Edward Downes in April 1963 receives its first integral release on CD here, with the original order of arias retained. Beethoven's Ah, perfido! was recorded a month later with the same forces and was issued only as a 45rpm record. Here it receives its first release on CD.

The last four selections on this CD remind us of Nilsson's simple beginnings. She was born in 1918, the only child of a farming family in Sweden's Skåne region, in the southernmost part of the country. Her vocal potential became known early on. 'At my christening I allegedly drowned out both the pastor and the organ,' she remarked in her autobiography, and she sang before she could walk. Later, when a doting farmhand bought her a toy piano, she accompanied herself to her heart's content! No matter where her fame took her – Stockholm, Bayreuth, Vienna, New York, Milan – Västra Karup in Skåne remained Nilsson's true home. She often returned there, to visit and to sing in the church, and that is where she retired in 1984. A decade after her retirement, a reporter from The New York Times visited her there, and she told him, "I've always tried to remember what my mother used to tell me: stay close to the earth. Then when you fall down, it won't hurt so much.' It was in Skåne that Birgit Nilsson died on Christmas Day, 2005. Bertil, whom she called her 'number one admirer', survived her by two years.

Raymond Tuttle
