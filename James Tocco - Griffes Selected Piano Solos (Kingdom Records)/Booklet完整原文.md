### Charles Tomlinson Griffes (1884-1920)

by Gordon Peters

It is hard to think of another American composer who packed so much achievement into so short a life as Charles Tomlinson Griffes. Born in Elmira, New York in September of 1884, he died in New York City in April of 1920, at the age of thirty-five, a victim of the influenza epidemic that swept the world that year. His creative life spanned not much more than fifteen years, yet he left us a body of work that grows in stature with each passing decade.

Griffes began his musical studies at an early age, and from 1903 to 1907 he studied in Berlin at the Hochschule für Musik, with Wilhelm Klatte and Engelbert Humperdinck. On his return to the United States he settled in New York, and for the next twelve years taught piano at the Hackley School for Boys in Tarrytown, New York, a position that allowed him considerable time for composition. His early works are strongly influenced by French Impressionism, and it is this style that we associate with the best known of his works, those included on this recording. In his last years he moved toward a more modernistic, almost expressionistic style, and he was beginning to experiment with polytonality and atonality when his life was cut short.

The musical landscape of early twentieth-century America was dominated by two opposing camps: the conservatives led by Horatio Parker, and the radicals led by John Philip Sousa? No, wait—actually, it was the New England School of composers (Parker, Chadwick, Foote) that emphasized the German tradition, versus the more experimental Americans like Ives, Griffes, Ruggles, Copland (later), and Carl Ruggles. Griffes was definitely in the latter camp, and his reputation has grown steadily while many of his more conservative contemporaries have faded from view.

The popularity of Griffes' music today rests largely on the four pieces from *Roman Sketches*, completed in 1916. Originally, they were intended as illustrations for a book of poems by Adeline Harriet Howe called *Sketches of Rome*. Griffes quickly outgrew that program, however, and reworked them into the four independent nature pieces we know today. "The White Peacock," the first of the set, is probably his best-known work, with its lush impressionistic sonorities and flowing lines that perfectly evoke its title. "Night of Fog" is appropriately atmospheric, harmonically ambiguous, very different in mood from its predecessor. "The Fountain of Acqua Paola" fairly bubbles with energy—one can almost see the water sparkling in the sun. "Clouds" completes the set with a quiet, mysterious stillness. It is easy to understand why these pieces have remained so popular—they are exquisitely crafted, full of color and atmosphere, and they sit perfectly in the piano repertoire.

## Page 2 (scans/HIPPO_20241215_0002.jpg) - Liner Notes continued

*Three Tone Pictures*, completed in 1912, comes from an earlier period, but it is very similar in its impressionistic language. "The Lake at Evening," like "The White Peacock," is full of Impressionist color, calm and atmospheric. "Night" is darker, more mysterious, and "The Dance of the Dryads" brings the set to a brilliant, energetic close, with a glittering piano texture that looks forward to the virtuoso writing we find later in *Roman Sketches*.

The three *Fantasy Pieces*, Barcarolle, Notturno Elegiaco, and Scherzo (De Profundis), date from 1912-14, and show Griffes' style steadily maturing and becoming more adventurous harmonically. The Barcarolle is a beautiful example of his gift for tranquil melody with shimmering accompaniment. The Notturno is an elegiac piece, deeply felt, very atmospheric. The Scherzo is more energetic, almost aggressive, and it shows Griffes moving towards a more modernist style.

The Piano Sonata was Griffes' last major work, completed in 1919, just a few months before his death. It is a very different work from the impressionistic pieces we have heard so far. The language is much more abstract, harmonically more adventurous, with passages that verge on atonality, and it has a contrapuntal complexity that is greater than anything he had written before. It is in three movements: an opening Allegretto con moto, a tranquil Lento sostenuto, and a closing Presto that is brilliant and dramatic, bringing the work (and Griffes' creative life) to a powerful close. It is now recognized as one of the first masterpieces of American modernist piano writing, and it deserves its place alongside the better-known impressionistic works.

James Tocco is one of the most admired American pianists of his generation. He has long been identified with American music, and he has done a great deal to promote the cause of American composers both here and abroad. We are delighted that he has turned his attention to Griffes, and we think this recording shows off both the composer and the pianist at their best.

## Page 3 (scans/HIPPO_20241215_0003.jpg) - Biography of James Tocco

### James Tocco

James Tocco has been described by *The New York Times* as "a bold, searching pianist with a graceful technique." His versatile musicianship has won him acclaim on four continents as recitalist, soloist with orchestra, chamber musician and teacher. His many recordings for a variety of labels have focused on the central repertoire, with special emphasis on the music of American composers.

A native of Detroit, Michigan, he studied with Clyde Adler there, and later with Rudolf Ganz in Chicago. He completed his studies with Hans Richter-Haaser in London and William Kapell at the Juilliard School. He won first prize in the ARD International Music Competition in Munich, and launched his career with debuts in Berlin, Munich, and London. He has since appeared with many of the world's leading orchestras, including the Berlin Philharmonic, the Munich Philharmonic, the BBC Philharmonic, the Detroit Symphony, the Cleveland Orchestra, the Philadelphia Orchestra, and the Los Angeles Philharmonic.

As a recitalist he has appeared in many of the world's major cities, including New York, Washington, Chicago, Los Angeles, London, Paris, Berlin, Munich, Milan, Rome, Tokyo, and Sydney. He has given many premiere performances, including the premiere of Samuel Barber's *Ballade*, which was written for him. He has been a prolific recording artist, with many releases on labels including Angel, Pro Arte, and now Kingdom.

Mr. Tocco is currently Distinguished Professor of Music at the City University of New York Graduate Center, and he is also the former president of the Beethoven Society of America. He has given master classes all over the world, and his students have won many prizes in international competitions.

For more information about James Tocco: www.jamestocco.com
