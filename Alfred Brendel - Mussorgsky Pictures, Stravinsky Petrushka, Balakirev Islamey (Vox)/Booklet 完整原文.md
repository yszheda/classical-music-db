## Modeste Petrovitch Moussorgsky (1839-1881)
### Pictures at an Exhibition

Moussorgsky is now regarded as the most genuinely talented of the Five, a group part of a life he had almost forsworn. But in his creative recess he had returned to them, for the group he was known as a somewhat foppish young officer in the Preobrazhensky Regiment of Guards; Borodin describes him as an elegant piano-playing dilettante. He dabbled at composition, often drinking late into the night, his eyes always red and bleary. But he had a certain form with Balakirev. On a visit to Moscow, however, a significant change in his state of mind seems to have been brought about. There he was to have undergone a sort of rebirth; I have been brought near to very close Russian. For the time being there was no change in his music; that the critic Stasov, closely connected with the Five, had to agree with Musorgskian obdickten that Mussorgsky remained almost an idiot. But in 1865 he happened to fall in with a group of young Russian intellectuals whose attitude and profession of arms and music had made a sobering impact on him. There seems somehow to have awakened him to the real truth of his genius. After several false starts he produced Boris Godunov, initiating a stream of dramatically original works culminating in the magnificent Song of Destruction, which appeared in 1871. The following year he wrote a letter about the appearance of the opposite corner he was now too radical for even their tastes; Stassov alone had brought him work such as the Pictures at an Exhibition was the most significant and original upon which he wrote: Mussorgsky's music I wholeheartedly send to the Devil is the cheapest, the richest, the grandest and most original that music had ever seen. But Mussorgsky's devotion to drink, and when he died from it in 1881 Pictures at an Exhibition stood also his last consequential work.

The piece is dedicated to Stassov his only constantly loyal admirer. It is an impression of a retrospective showing of architectural drawings, watercolours and stage-designs by Moussorgsky's friend Viktor Hartmann, who had died the previous year. The music moves from room to room, pictures several times in the course of the piece, separating most of the various tableaux. It may be taken as representing an observer walking through the gallery. The succeeding movements are as follows: Gnomus, a drawing of a weird gnome; The Old Castle, before which stands a troubadour; Children Quarrelling after Their Games; Bydlo, a picture of a great-wheeled Polish cart, drawn by oxen; Ballet of the Chicks in Their Shells; Two Polish Jews, Haunted and Unhaunted; Limoges, The Market; One Rich, the Other Poor; a dialogue between pomposity and beggary; Limoges, The Marketplace; Catacombae, a representation of haggling women; Catacombae, which Moussorgsky subtitled cum mortuis in lingua mortua, or with the dead in a dead language; writing on the original manuscript of a dramatic encounter with the dead Hartmann; The Hut on Fowl's Legs, in which was supposed to dwell the evil witch Baba-Yaga, of Russian fairy-lore; and the final Great Gate of Kiev, a massive conclusion in the style of a chorale.

Nothing like Pictures at an Exhibition had ever been heard before; in every branch of music concerned, instrumental techniques, harmonic and melodic rules, precepts of form. In its impact to the whole it is similar only to the work of Debussy, who was just beginning to experiment with impressionism. But Pictures at an Exhibition has had a permanent place in the piano repertoire. It is one of the most frequently performed of all piano works. It has inspired countless orchestrations; it has influenced the development of Russian music and, more recently, of jazz. The piece has been arranged for orchestra by Stokowski, Ormandy, and others. The most famous orchestration is by Ravel, which has become a staple of the concert hall.

### Petrouchka Suite

## Mily Alexeyevitch Balakirev (1837-1910)
### Islamey - Oriental Fantasy

The composer of this brilliant keyboard fantasy has been celebrated more in the history books than in the modern concert hall. And yet a great portion of the music regularly heard today bears the stamp of his powerful influence.

Although Mikhail Glinka was the actual founder of Russian nationalism in the last century, it was Balakirev who established that school as a permanent force in the musical life of his country. He bore his appearance most Russian music was scarcely more than a dim reflection of the current Italian style then in demand in the churches and at the Court. Balakirev was the mentor of a group of slightly younger composers known as the Five which, besides himself included Rimsky-Korsakov, Moussorgsky, Borodin and César Cui. As a group they devoted their careers to the creation of indigenous Russian music; individually they produced some of the most durable popular music of the modern symphonic and operatic repertory. Through Stravinsky, a pupil of Rimsky-Korsakov, their influence extends to music being written at the present time. Balakirev himself was the most Russian of all the members of the Five; as the group became known, Balakirev thus emerges as a principal force in the shaping of modern musical life.

Islamey was composed between August 21 and September 27, 1869. This was a trying year for Balakirev; at the time he was director of an important series of concerts given by the Russian Musical Society of St. Petersburg, a post which he had held for two years. His forthright advocacy of the cause of nationalism had incurred the patronage of the Society the Grand Duchess Helena Pavlovna, and after many months of intrigue, that lady succeeded in having him ousted from his position. Since he had no other regular income, Balakirev was forced to take a post in the Czar's Civil Service, and for some time afterward found little time for composition. These circumstances brought on a severe nervous depression which, according to all accounts, nearly cost Balakirev his reason.

Little trace of such unhappy developments can be found in the music of Islamey. It is an expansive virtuoso piece, presenting three vividly contrasting impressions of oriental folk. Its difficulties are prodigious: Balakirev himself was unable to perform it in his own satisfaction, and he was regarded as an excellent pianist. In this recording, Islamey is revealed as a vehicle for the most brilliant pianistic exploitation nearly every possibility of the instrument.
