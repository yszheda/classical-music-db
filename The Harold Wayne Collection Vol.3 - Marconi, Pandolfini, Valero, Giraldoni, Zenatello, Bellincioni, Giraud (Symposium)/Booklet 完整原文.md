## 内页说明

### THE HAROLD WAYNE COLLECTION

Dr Harold Wayne started collecting vocal records in the 1960s. It was a time at which many collectors considered that, with the demand for rare specimens rising, it was already too late to aspire seriously to build a representative collection of moderately uncommon, let alone what P.G. Hurst has termed 'the pinnacles of the collector's ambition'.

The Wayne collection is the result of an enthusiasm which developed into determination to be as honest as possible - ultimately into an attempt to gather records by correspondence and by telephone, personally and by proxy, by means systematic or serendipitous, and having refreshingly, he secured Europe, the Americas, and the Antipodes. The result is a collection which, for its scope, its completeness, its state of preservation, and for the importance of some of the singers of whom it holds specimens which are thought to be unique, is probably unparalleled.

Symposium Records now presents a further selection of important records from the Wayne collection. It comprises a representative and substantial collection of G&T's which were never intended by the manufacturers to have been issued commercially. Every item on it is a major rarity. Many of these items are reissued, and for as far as known, for the first time.

This Compact Disc presents recordings of singers of major importance who recorded in the years before 1910. The records have been chosen for their musical and historical value. All are of extreme rarity and most have been unavailable in any form for eighty years or more. Great care has been taken to transfer the voices as faithfully as possible even to the point of leaving untouched incidences of mild mechanical noise than one would wish. Similar care has been exercised in transferring the records from speed, no more so than since the Compact Disc medium normally offers no possibility of variation.

Track numbers appear in square brackets

### Dr. Wayne writes:

To Michael Henstock's notes I am happy to add some comments, based on my own privileged experience, on the relative rarity of the original discs which I have been privileged to re-record with Symposium Records.

**Marconi:** The six titles on Marconi are more properly reserved for the notes which accompany the compact disc devoted to this tenor (Symposium 1069).

**Pandolfini:** Her records are only accessible to the most assiduous of collectors. My copy was owned by the well-known London collector and dealer the late Ronald Phillips. Among them was the "Chanson de Florian" recorded by her at Rome for Fonotipia in 1906. I passed it to the late Anthony Westbrook. The Chanson de Florian was recorded at Rome for Fonotipia in 1906 and was released on 6th July (October 13th 1906) when I travelled with the late Roberto Bauer along the Via Aurelia to visit Dr. Monticco. Roberto kept three very scarce Ancona 1904 Red G&T's and the Pandolfini, which he gave to me as a present.

**Valero:** I have found two excellent copies of Valero's "Dormi pure" and one each of his two "Cavalleria" records. These are all black G&T's made in Milan in 1904. I have no idea where they were found or how they came to be in my collection.

**Giraldoni:** These six records are very rare. The Aida is perhaps the least uncommon followed by the Don Carlos is real beauty! and the Demone undoubtedly the most elusive of the six.

**Zenatello:** I agree wholeheartedly with Michael Henstock that the G&Ts are musically unknown. Poor old Zenatello!

**Bellincioni:** The Fedora is one of the group of records at very slow speeds - many unsigned probably because of this. The Mefistofele was recorded at very slow speeds and I am still undecided whether to speed it up or slow it down.

**Giraud:** The Andrea Chénier is unique as far as I know, recorded on an island in the erstwhile estate of the late Earl of Harewood, and now, sadly, lost. It was broadcast every week on the single radio station which served the island but recordings of great singers now come from tapes!

### Fiorello Giraud (1868 or 1871-1928)

**Black G&T, Milan, April 1904**

52046 Andrea Chénier Come un bel dì di maggio [27]
(Matrix 2188h)

Fiorello Giraud, son of the tenor Lodovico Giraud (1846-1882), studied at the Conservatory of his native Parma. The fact that his debut, in Lohengrin at Vercelli, took place in 1881 suggests that of the alternative birth dates, 1868 rather than 1871 is correct. He was acclaimed for the correctness of his schooling, his finesse, and his splendid high notes, and his progress was rapid. In 1882 came his moment of enduring fame when, with Stabile, Maurel, Paddi, and Ancona he sang in the world premiere of Leoncavallo's Pagliacci. Subsequently he sang at Barcelona, Lisbon, Santiago (Chile), Trieste (world premiere of Tommasini's Medea), and Palermo. At La Scala he sang Pellas in the Italian premiere of Debussy's opera. The Giraud title included here was still unknown when that Bauer and his co-workers compiled their catalogue. It displays the dark, virile tenor voice, with enough vibrato to give it character, of a man whom, we might have thought, was still young enough to manage the more usual high B flat, rather than A flat, in the final bars.

The remaining seven known Giraud G&T records will appear on a forthcoming compact disc (The Harold Wayne Collection, Vol. IV, Symposium Records 1073).

July 1989
Michael R. Henstock

Eugenio Giraldoni, was another singer to spring from an illustrious musical background. His father was renowned baritone Leone Giraldoni (1826-1897) and his mother Carolina Ferni was a leading soprano who subsequently became his duet partner in 1875. As a pupil of the celebrated violinist Virgilio Panfili. After study with his father the young Eugenio made his debut at Lucca in 1885, as Amonasro, and in the years which followed his career developed rapidly throughout Italy and Buenos Aires he created the baritone role in the first Ariesina performance of Franchetti's Cristoforo Colombo, and on May 1896 the part of Zaccaria in Giordano's Il Voto, but posterity will certainly best remember him for his creation of Figaro di Basso, in the world premiere of Puccini's Tosca. The two records from Don Carlos and La Traviata included Andrea Chénier, Madama Butterfly, Luisa Miller, Otello, Rigoletto, La Traviata, Un Ballo in Maschera, La Gioconda, I Medici, and I Pagliacci. He sang in French. The Meistersinger (I Maestri Cantori), Lohengrin, and Tannhäuser. Rosenkavalier, Salome, La Fanciulla del West, and other operas by German and Wagner's artistic intelligence. He impressed as much as he was a great Verdian.

All of Giraldoni's recordings were made during the years 1902 or 1904 are exceptionally scarce. Though the Notturno from Cristoforo Colombo (accompanied by harp and cello) bears his name on the record label. The Aida is historically important it is a Rubinstein's Demon - less sinister and insinuating than Battistini's characterization - that was the impression of the authority of his stage performances. We cannot agree with the description (Scutti) of his recording of the Death of Rodrigo from Don Carlos as being in a monotonous mezzo

### Giovanni Zenatello (1876-1949)

**Black G&T, Milan, February 1903**

52702 Andrea Chénier Un dì, all'azzurro spazio [18]
(Matrix 3119-Rn)

52703 Faust Salve! Dimora casta e pura [19]
(Matrix 3120-Rn)

52712 La Traviata Un dì di felicità [21]
(Matrix 3140-Rn)

52713 I Maestri Cantori Nel verso al piè [22]
(Matrix 3141-Rn)

Giovanni Zenatello studied with Zannoni and Moretti. He began his vocal career as a baritone, making his debut at Verona as Amonasro. A fine, wide-range, both artistically and geographically - and highly successful. His repertoire embraced Aida, Un Ballo in Maschera, La Bohème, Carmen, Faust, Germanico, La Gioconda, Lucia di Lammermoor, Manon, Pagliacci, Rigoletto, La Traviata, and Il Trovatore. In 1904 he made his first records for Fonotipia. His repertoire also included Gloria (Cilea) and Siberia (Giordano). But it was as Verdi's Otello that he made his name, having made his debut in the part at Verona in 1906.

Zenatello's records for Columbia, Fonotipia, Edison, and Victor are moderately accessible. Virtually unknown, however, are the group of black G&T's made in Milan in 1903. In these records we hear (despite some speed variations in the originals) the very youthful Zenatello, the voice being described as a voice of trumpet-like quality, exactly suited to the eager, youthful exuberance of Andrea Chénier's opening aria. I cannot convey the intensity of sound he reaches with innocent ease the high G of the climax of 'Salve! Dimora casta e pura.'

**Red G&T, Milan 1903 and 1907***

052056 La Gioconda: Cielo e mar [1]
(Matrix Con 722) (De Angelis, pf)

052065 Non guardarmi così: Non guardarmi così (Paloni) [2]
(Matrix Con 725WGC)

(Matrix Con 730) (Bella cantiam l'amore Salabini, pf)

052187 La Geisha: O mia Mimosa (Jones) [4]
(Matrix 1415r)* (Bella cantiam l'amore S. Abajo, pf)

052673 In questa sera: In questa sera (Denza) [5]
(Matrix 11178b)*

Among the greatest historical figures enchanted by the gramophone the name of Francesco ('Checco') Marconi ranks highly, for here we have a tenor who learned his craft in Italy and sang with his place of birth - Gayarre, Castena, di Stagno, and Tamagno - whose names were then household names. After studies in Milan, he made some recordings in 1903, the year after Tamagno retired, as the Pagliano of Florence, where he sang Mefistofele in 1876; he appeared at the Teatro Carlo Felice in 1878, and made his debut in 1880. In his career he sang in Milan, Rome, St. Petersburg, Buenos Aires, London, Naples, Barcelona and Paris in Russia, appearing for the first time in the new opera house in 1912. His repertoire expanded to embrace I Puritani, Lucia di Lammermoor, La Sonnambula, and Gli Ugonotti, as well as Carmen, Aida, Otello, and La Gioconda. With several other of his great tenor contemporaries he was a notable Lohengrin. On these works he brought the beauty of a tenor voice which, by common consent, was one of the finest of his time and which J.H. Duval described 'as a voice of silver, forte, for it displays many dynamic nuances and also the splendidly finished trill which was with the capabilities of so many male singers a century ago.'

### Gemma Bellincioni (1864-1950)

**Red G&T, Milan 1903**

053014 Fedora: O grandi occhi lucenti [23]
(Matrix 570R)

053017 Mefistofele L'altra notte [24]
(Matrix 572R)

053018 Cavalleria Rusticana: Voi lo sapete [25]
(Matrix 574R)

053019 La Traviata: Ah! fors'è lui [26]
(Matrix 577R)

S. Cottone, pf.

Of all the sopranos who identified themselves so completely with the new repertory [verismo], none was more famous in her life-time, or more important historically than Gemma Bellincioni. So runs Scott's introduction (The Record of Singing. To 1914, p. 147) to his discussion of this singer, and we can only concur in his summary of one of the greatest sopranos who ever lived.

Born in Monza on August 17th or 19th 1864, Bellincioni's background was musical; her father was a bandmaster in the Royal Italian Army, and she studied at the Milan Conservatory with Barbara Swellinck. Her dramatic gifts made an early appearance, when she played Hamlet in a private performance at the age of twelve. She began by imitating the moves and gestures of the ballerinas when she was on holiday with her family at Salsomaggiore in 1880, but it was decided that her talents would better benefit from study. Records (Record Collector, Vol. XV, Nos. 9-10, 1966; quoted by Scott), she first appeared in public in Il Sogno d'un valzer di Ducbeschi by Dall'Orefice (Naples, May 1879). Schmidt specifies, however, that the work was Dalloste Tutu e la maschera di Dalcroze, and was not an opera but a concertato. By the terms of her marriage contract, which was signed on 16th June 1880, Roberto Stagno was her husband. She made sixteen appearances in all in L'Amico Fritz Mascagni. In Il Barbiere di Siviglia, Linda di Chamounix, and in Lakmé (Amneris in Aida, Santuzza in Cavalleria rusticana, Norina, also sang in L'Africana (Ines), Il Profeta (Berta), Roberto il Diavolo (Alice), and in Lucia (Lucia). A visit to Spain in 1885 and then Russia with Stagno's company brought appearances there with luminaries of the stature of Gayarre, and also her first performance of Traviata, which they repeated at the Teatro Comunale of Bologna in October 1885. Following the spring season of 1886 at La Scala, Milan Roberto il Diavolo she joined the Ferrari company for her first tour to South America. The troupe also included the Sicilian tenor Roberto Stagno, whose companion they did not marry) she thereafter remained until his death in 1897. Together they triumphed in the world premiere of Cavalleria Rusticana at Rome in May 1890; some of the drama lingers in the several photographs of them as Santuzza and Turiddu in a Scènes from that first performance of Giordano's Mala Vita. Vizi, in a persistent lie that did much to popularize Mascagni's work; she created the role of Fédora at the Teatro San Carlo. Schmidt Gordon wrote for them the principal parts in Fédora, but Stagno's death left Bellincioni to create Fedora without her partner at the Teatro San Carlo in 1888, acclaimed by audiences all over Europe in roles as diverse as Carmen and Elsa, and Norma and Salome, gradually replacing lyric parts by the dramatic. She created roles in numerous other operas by composers famous or obscure. In addition to the roles sung elsewhere she sometimes appeared to make guest in parts such as Figaro in Il Barbiere. Such was her long life until 1911, when she retired from the stage and devoted herself to teaching. Her most celebrated pupils included her daughter Italia Bellincioni, who taught singing in Rome; later, she taught various aspects of musical and dramatic art in various European cities.

Her four red G&T records (all included here) of 1903 and her ten Pathé records of a year later, have only recently become known. The Pathé's appeared in the late 20th century. In the former, when she was only about forty years old, we can readily perceive the dark soprano voice, vibrant, if a trifle over the vibrato evident. The closing bars of the Mefistofele piece - in which she dispels the fantasy that singers with vibrato cannot trill - remind us that she had once been a coloratura soprano. But the voice, which was never large, is now used in a dramatic fashion and the upper notes are frequently forced. The pathos and tenderness she infuses her Verdi so much admired her Violetta; for all the vocal wear and unevenness there are yet flashes of those fine feelings, her treatment of the phrase 'Ah quell'accent ... ch'è palpito dell'universo.' In the fragments of his 'Si, fui trafitta' one senses an almost physical reaction to the tragedy, as one does with the 1890s.

These records all too true that the aura of Gemma Bellincioni came essentially through a temperament which the early gramophone was unable to capture, but they provide a fascinating glimpse of a singer able to persuade us of her strong magnetism. Would that there had been more of them!

### Angelica Pandolfini (1871-1959)

**Red G&T, Milan, 1903**

53333 Chanson de Florian (Goddard) [6]
(Matrix Con 364)

53340 Adriana Lecouvreur: Sortita di Adriana [7]
(Matrix Con 371)

053037 Le Nozze di Figaro: Deh vieni non tardar [8]
(Matrix Con 718)

S. Cottone, pf.

Angelica Pandolfini was a daughter of Francesco Pandolfini (1836-1916), the Amonasro of the first performance 1872 of Aida in Italy, and sister of the operatic tenor and singing teacher Pompeo Pandolfini. After studies with Mariani, first she made her debut at the Teatro Comunale, Modena, in February 1884, in Gounod's Faust, the following August at the Royal Liceu, Barcelona where she appeared as Santuzza, Nedda, and Puccini's Manon. Returning to Italy, she appeared at the Lirico in Milan, in which she was to become more celebrated as the creator. Cleite Ferrani, whom - according to no less an authority than Rodolfo Celletti - she had surpassed 'as the voice of Santuzza'. Shown in the first La Scala performance of La Bohème. Her subsequent repertoire embraced Margherita, Suor Angelica, La Traviata, Falstaff, Mascagni's (Iris, Iris), Iris, Tosca, Fedora, Le Maschere, Otel, Madama Butterfly, Mefistofele, Lohengrin, and Figaro in Le Nozze di Figaro, and in 1902, in the company of Caruso and De Luca, she created the part of Adriana in the world premiere of Cilea's Adriana Lecouvreur. In 1906 she retired and taught, first in Rome and then in Naples.

Among record collectors the name of Pandolfini lives today principally through her series of Fonotipia - unknown to most of those who knew her during her lifetime and desirability of her red G&T records, dating from 1903. It is a rarity such that the vast majority of every known copy has now been destroyed. Few collectors can hope to see one of her records, still less own one. But, while many highly-prized recordings may escape attention by a discerning public, few could possibly have, early in the century, a considerable sum for a dubious souvenir of some aging diva, no modern collector would pay. It is strange, but there were many who were attractive on purely vocal grounds. Rumour has it that, for some unknown reason, she became dissatisfied with them and had both matrices and moulds destroyed. Oddly, the diva seemed to have only vague recollections of her records: her nephew related to the writer that, to her family, she always maintained that they had been made in Lisbon, running at least the possibility of the existence of further titles. Of the titles included here, her singing of the aria in Adriana treatment well illustrates the evenness of her diminuendo and the mellowness of her chest notes.

### Fernando Valero (1854 or 1862-1914)

(Matrix WCC 3922-R) Dormi pure (Scuderi) [9]
(Matrix WCC 3923-R) Siciliana [10]
(Matrix WCC 3924-R) Brindisi [11]
(Matrix WCC 3925-R)

Fernando Valero's training with Vidal, the former teacher of Julian Gayarre is perhaps best known for his recordings. Of the Gayarre. Other sources variously attribute his training to Salazar, to the celebrated tenor Zepilli Tamburini, or to Masini. After an operatic debut in Madrid in 1881 at the Teatro Real of Madrid, as Lorenzo in Aubér's Pro Diavolo. From 1880 to 1881 he was with the principal companies in Spain and Portugal, appearing at La Scala, Milan, making his debut as Gounod's Faust. In 1885 he appeared at La Scala, Imperial Theatres in St. Petersburg, and at the Bolshoi in Moscow and Odessa in St. Petersburg. In 1887 he appeared at the Imperial Opera, where he sang Don José, Nadir, and the Duke of Mantua. In 1890 he appeared in his first Italian opera, Otello, at the Teatro Costanzi in Palermo. In January 1891 he returned to La Scala, to create Turiddu in that company's first performance of Cavalleria rusticana. He also appeared in Paris at the Metropolitan Opera House, to the Santuzza of Emma Eames. After 1887 his career was interrupted by illness. In 1900 he taught singing school in St. Petersburg.

This reissue presents three of Valero's four known records. Though Scuderi's premi part is set, Scuderi himself, in his article, quotes the other three records, this is a charming record which well demonstrates his vocal flexibility. We are fortunate to have two glimpses of his Turiddu, when, especially in the 'Siciliana' (with its customary semitone transposition to E minor, he open-throathed method and exuberant flourishes admirably suggest the passion of the Sicilian peasant.
