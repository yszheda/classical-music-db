## Introduction

Given the relative obscurity of his operas, *Cavalleria Rusticana* apart, it is strange to reflect that for a while Mascagni was generally regarded as the heir to Verdi's throne – the most important Italian composer who could build on the traditions of the past while taking opera forward into the dawning twentieth century. As we now know, it was Puccini who was to occupy that position until his death in 1924, by which time Mascagni was increasingly overlooked. In fact when he died in 1945 he was virtually forgotten outside musical circles. Yet arguably it was he who had paved the way for *Madama Butterfly* and *Turandot*, for without *Iris* those two favourite operas might have been rather different from the works we know today.

The prevailing opinion that Mascagni's stage works subsequent to *Cavalleria* are in comparison somehow lacking is nothing new. He himself was aware of it, and he knew that audiences wanted him repeating a winning formula. To his credit, he refused. While maintaining a firm belief in the importance of melodic accessibility, he was keen to move away from the crude melodrama of *Cavalleria* towards subtler, more refined romanticism, both in his music and in the operatic subjects he set. So whatever the public's reaction to his operas both in his own time and today, we should consider his claim that he wrote finer works than *Cavalleria*. He is not the only composer whose reputation rests unfairly on only one work, and the fact that he never repeated the success of his first opera should not lead us to assume that he was a 'one-hit wonder'.

That said, the unpleasant, even sordid, plot of *Iris* was guaranteed not to endear Mascagni to the Italian public of his day. In 19th century Japan a wealthy nobleman, Osaka, fails to seduce the innocent young dressmaker Iris and persuades Kyoto, a boorish brother keeper, to abduct her. But after her imprisonment Osaka becomes bored with her continuing naivety and he abandons her. When her elderly blind father II Cieco learns of her fate he assumes that she has joined the brothel deliberately in order to prostitute herself. Grief-stricken by his rejection and cursed by him, she kills herself by jumping into a sewer.

Often criticised as unlikely and distasteful material for an opera, the story makes more sense in the context of Mascagni's enduring concern with romance and beauty (he referred to the opera as 'a symbol of immortal art, triumphing over all the filth of the base world...' And lest listeners need reminding of his commitment to beauty of ideals, it is worth noting that he added, 'what graceful contours, what delicacy, what sweetness surround this symbol!'

Not that *Iris* is an unadventurous work. Indeed, in some respects it is remarkably modernistic given Mascagni's enduring concern with romance and beauty (he referred to the opera as 'a very light thing, really delicate'). While the end of the opera might seem overly sentimental – as Iris dies at daybreak after reaching out to the sun, the symbol of life and her only loyal companion, the sewer is suddenly flooded with light and flowers – other aspects of the work are surprisingly forward-looking. In addition to his use of oriental motifs years before Puccini did, let alone *Turandot* more than a quarter of a century later, some of Mascagni's harmonies border on impressionism – at that time a technique not merely innovative but virtually avant-garde.

The familiar balance between voices and orchestral textures was to be retained, but music was to predominate by narrating merely echoing the events on stage. 'Words and music combine to form a unified impression', he explained rather grandiosely, 'the music expressing sublimely the maternal memory that is in the words.' For the new opera he chose his own distinguished career also famously collaborated with Catalani, Giordano and Puccini. Their working relationship was challenging but always friendly, and Mascagni would later describe him as "my advisor and inspiration... In short, he created the drama and was not a simple adapter like so many others."

Mascagni, an internationally esteemed conductor as well as a composer, led the premiere of *Iris* in Rome in November 1898. Perhaps because of the music's relative subtlety and the unusual subject matter of the libretto, the opera was not a success with the critics. But the Rome audiences took *Iris* to their hearts, as did the people of Naples when Mascagni conducted performances there the following March. He also took the opera *Zanetto* to America at the end of 1902, (along with *Cavalleria Rusticana* and described its New York reception as a triumph and adding that it was "the kind of opera they like better in America than in Italy; indeed it made me more popular than *Cavalleria* did... but then the Americans have always gone crazy over Italian art."

*Iris* will certainly come as a relief to those music lovers who resent the way that intrusive applause breaks the dramatic spell of a performance. Mascagni, too, found such interruptions vulgar and, recognising that such a way as to minimize applause between scenes. Despite this, or perhaps even because of it, performances of *Iris* have been recorded commercially, but even today *Cavalleria Rusticana* is his only stage work to retain a place in the mainstream repertoire.

The concert performance of *Iris* on these discs was given in Amsterdam by a predominantly Italian cast and starred Magda Olivero (born 1910), perhaps the best-known *Iris* of her generation. It was one of the many relatively obscure roles that she championed at the beginning of her long career, which began with a Radio Turin production of *Misteri Dolorosi* in December 1932. Her stage debut, as Lauretta in *Gianni Schicchi*, took place the following year, as did after a ten-year break in the 1940s she resumed her career as Adriana Lecouvreur and began to make more appearances outside her native Italy.

After a triumphant US debut in Cherubini's *Medea* in 1967 she became a regular part of American operatic life thanks to her performances with the Opera Theatre of New Jersey. In the early 1970s she often appeared there after Fedora, Adriana Lecouvreur and Tosca in addition to giving performances in Boston, Dallas, New York's Carnegie Hall and Philharmonic Hall, and San Francisco. today *Cavalleria Rusticana* is his only stage work to retain a place in the mainstream repertoire.
