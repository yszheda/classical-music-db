### "The Mirror": An appreciation of Sviatoslav Richter

*By Kevin Bazzana*

More than ten years later, it's still hard to believe he's dead—hard to believe that such a mountain of a musician, who commanded the piano and its literature like few others in the twentieth century, who was a paragon of artistic integrity admired and loved by audiences and colleagues alike, could turn out to be mortal after all. But only the man has left us: Sviatoslav Richter the artist, and collectors of classical recordings at least, has never been more alive, more present on the music scene. He is surely the best-documented pianist of the recording era, represented by hundreds of LPs and CDs released by many labels around the world—and the number only continues to grow.

Richter left a large body of sanctioned live and studio recordings, but even more numerous are the unauthorized releases of concert performances, which span some half a century and contribute much to our understanding of Richter's art. In the years just before his death, in 1997, a handful of labels—BMG/Melodiya, Philips, Praga, Olympia, Stradivarius—released retrospective boxes of CDs that conveniently summarized his achievement, and more such sets have emerged posthumously. So ubiquitous have his recordings become that one is tempted to infer some unspoken industry fiat to the effect that every preserved Richter performance can be offered up to the public. Of course, the resulting body of work is uneven, sonically as well as artistically; Richter himself, in his later years, worried that he was over-recorded, that the glut of recordings only devalued them all. Still, one can only marvel at the consistently superior musicianship displayed throughout this enormous body of work.

---

#### Biography

Sviatoslav Teofilovich Richter was born on March 20, 1915, in Zhitomir, an agricultural and light-industrial city near Kiev, in Ukraine (then under Russian rule). There was music in his background: his grandfather was in the piano business (Zhitomir was renowned for the manufacture of musical instruments), and his parents were both pianists. His father, of German descent, was also an organist and composer, trained in Vienna; his mother (who, he claimed, was a distant relative of Jenny Lind) was of mixed central- and eastern-European ancestry, and apparently had some Tartar blood with which her son strongly identified. When he was still an infant, the family moved to the Black Sea port of Odessa, which possessed a rich cultural life. There, his father worked as a church organist and choirmaster, and taught piano at the local conservatory. (In 1941, he was arrested and shot by the Soviets after the family refused to evacuate Odessa in anticipation of the German invasion; Richter called this the "darkest chapter" of his life.)

Richter recalled that his love of music was awakened in infancy by his father's piano playing, though he took up music in earnest only around age 8. He learned some rudiments from his father, but as a pianist was largely self-taught until his twenties—a remarkable fact, considering his mastery of the instrument. Opera was his first love, and as a child he accumulated a large library of operatic scores; he also made transcriptions of orchestral masterpieces, just for fun. (The music that most occupied him in those days was Wagner's Ring cycle. "Wagner for me is more than music," he said. "His works are wonders of nature.") He was fond of drawing and painting, too, and of writing and performing plays. But he hated school—it bored him.

Early on he wanted to become a conductor and composer, but a private concert in 1931, at which he played the Schumann concerto (both solo and orchestral parts), made him decide to devote himself to the piano. Years later, he explained, with characteristic self-deprecation, why he gave up composing: "Perhaps the best way I can put it is that I see no point in adding to all the bad music in the world." He also recalled that tedious private lessons in theory and composition, in the early 1930s, "robbed me of any desire to write music." As for conducting, he gave up that notion because "I simply have too much to do as a pianist."

Beginning in 1930, the teenage Richter spent two years accompanying operatic excerpts in shows mounted by the local Sailors' Club, then a year with the Philharmonic Society. In 1934, he became a répétiteur at the Odessa Opera, where his skill at sight-reading scores was much admired, and in later life, he was quick to explode "the perpetual myth that I had been a conductor at the Opera! I was a répétiteur, nothing else." Around this time, he also accompanied singers in clubs, retirement homes, and factories, and gave a few solo appearances, in places like barracks and collective farms. He gave his first proper recital in 1934, an all-Chopin program at Odessa's Engineers' Club.

Richter received his first formal instruction in piano in 1937, when he entered the Moscow Conservatory as a pupil of Heinrich Neuhaus, with whom he also boarded, and who became something of a second father to him. He was further nourished by the busy Moscow music scene, and co-founded a Students' Circle that met regularly to study opera, the symphonic literature, and contemporary music. (Throughout his life, he enjoyed having friends over for listening parties.) At this time, he said, he "still had no pianistic technique, for I had always played as an orchestra plays," but under Neuhaus he prospered. His natural pianistic gifts and astonishing memory were immediately noted; Neuhaus, indeed, felt that he had little to teach his pupil: "All music, all the finest music, rests—like the infant in the arms of Raphael's Madonna—within his skill, which reminds one of domes built by Bramante and Michelangelo." Richter, in turn, considered Neuhaus "a miraculous pianist" then at the height of his powers, and said that "in half a year, Neuhaus freed my hands—really liberated me. For that reason he is more than a teacher or a friend to me."

In 1937, Richter met Sergei Prokofiev, and the two encountered each other often after that. In his first important appearance, a much-talked-about 1940 concert at the Moscow Conservatory in which he shared a program with Neuhaus, he played only Prokofiev, including a world premiere. He recalled,
> I was still a student when I gave the first performance in public of Prokofiev's Sixth Sonata, and it was an extraordinary gesture on the part of Neuhaus to have me play it. At the time many musicians said that Neuhaus was out of his mind. Prokofiev himself had first played this sonata on the radio, and that was the actual first performance. But then he found it too difficult, and Neuhaus said to him, "In that case young Richter will play it!" ... After my performance, [Prokofiev] came onto the stage and shook my hand.

He remained an ardent champion of Prokofiev. In 1941, he performed the Fifth Piano Concerto under the composer's baton. In 1943 and 1951, respectively, he gave the premieres of the Seventh and Ninth Sonatas (the latter was dedicated to him). He accompanied the premieres of the Op. 94 flute sonata (in 1943) and the Cello Sonata (in 1950, with Mstislav Rostropovich). In 1952, he made his only public appearance as a conductor (after ten days' coaching from Kiril Kondrashin), leading Rostropovich in the premiere of the *Symphony-Concerto*. His only significant piece of writing was an essay about Prokofiev published shortly after the composer's death in 1953.

Richter's professional career began in 1942, with a recital at the conservatory (he was literally shaking from nerves); the same year, he made the first of many radio broadcasts. In 1943, he undertook his first concert tour, in the Caucasus. In 1944, he graduated from the conservatory (he won its Gold Medal two years later), and in 1945, he won first prize in the All-Union Piano Competition, though he had entered it reluctantly and considered it "a complete waste of my time." (He never entered a competition again: "I loathe them.") The same year, he performed for the first time with the soprano Nina Dorliak (1908-98), whom he had met shortly after entering the conservatory, where she taught singing. She soon became his wife, and remained at his side for the rest of his life, though it was generally known that he was homosexual. (After his death, it emerged that the two were never legally married.) Together, the couple began making recordings in 1948, and performed some two hundred concerts through 1959.

At first, Richter performed only in the Soviet Union, and he appeared on Russian television as early as 1952. Gradually, he added other East Bloc countries to his touring schedule—Czechoslovakia in 1950, Hungary and Poland in 1954. In 1957, he toured four cities in China. The present recording, of a complete recital given in Budapest, dates from February 9, 1958, by which time Richter was touring Eastern Europe regularly. He appeared in seven concerts in Budapest between February 9 and 17: three solo recitals, two lieder recitals with sixty-six per year. But his schedule was never regular. Sometimes he toured almost compulsively; sometimes he took months-long breaks from public life. (He was forever at war with his own constitution, he said: "I'm laziness and passivity personified.") He was a notorious canceller. Ill health was usually the reason cited, and it is true that his health was always delicate, his disposition nervous. From the 1970s, he dealt with high blood pressure and a heart condition, but though his health problems naturally worsened with age he still had some very busy years in later life. In 1986, for instance, the year of the Siberian tour, he was 71 but gave almost 150 concerts.

He often sought out the sympathetic surroundings of festivals: Edinburgh, Lucerne, Moscow, Prague, Salzburg, Schleswig-Holstein, Spoleto, Vienna. Between 1964 and 1975, he appeared frequently at Benjamin Britten's festival in Aldeburgh, and became a great friend and champion of Britten's, as well as a partner in four-hand piano music and concertos. (Britten wrote for him a pair of charming, quirky cadenzas for Mozart's K. 482 piano concerto.) After appearing in 1963 in the intimate setting of the Grange de Meslay, near Tours, France, Richter returned in 1964 to become the guiding inspiration for annual Fêtes Musicales there. In 1981, he launched the annual "December Nights" festival in Moscow, and not only performed but was actively involved with stage productions and art exhibitions.

Though he had his pick of cities and halls, Richter always had a special fondness for small towns and intimate spaces—he cared nothing about image or prestige when choosing a venue. Toward the end of his career, he grew increasingly removed from the mainstream concert business, and averse to long-term planning. He would sometimes arrive in a city, decide spontaneously that he wanted to play, and hire a hall. When he was 77, he risked his health to perform in a ruined theater, exposed to the elements, for students at a music school in Benevento, in southern Italy. Some such recitals were literally unannounced, some given on short notice with minimal publicity; sometimes tiny (and doubtless pleasantly startled) audiences were the result, other times full houses thanks to word of mouth by informed locals. The French violinist and film-maker Bruno Monsaingeon, who conducted extensive interviews with Richter in his last years for his documentary *Richter: The Enigma* and his book *Sviatoslav Richter: Notebooks and Conversations*, reported that in May 1996, though short of money, Richter turned down a fee of a million dollars for two Carnegie Hall recitals, yet at the same time developed a curious desire to give concerts at which he planned—literally—to pass the hat.

He performed publicly for the last time in 1995, shortly after his eightieth birthday. His subsequent years were sad, as he lived in self-imposed isolation around Europe, depressed by illness and the inability to play. As though he knew the end was near, he returned to Moscow—having been away almost three years—just a month before his death, from a heart attack, on August 1, 1997.

---

As his career progressed, Richter looked less and less like a musician who cared about garnering the applause, money, and career mileage that his talent and reputation merited, and more and more like a dedicated and sincere artist who hated the trappings (and trap) of celebrity, with whom he regarded with disgust the self-promotion and public relations to which many of his colleagues succumb, and who put his integrity as a musician before personal and professional considerations. He hated the stage-door crowds and post-concert receptions, the autograph-hounds and interviewers—the whole business of being famous. He was well paid, it is true, but also very generous with his money, which, one senses, did not much matter to him.

His modesty was almost pathological: "You can never be modest enough," he insisted. He was relentlessly self-critical, holding himself to impossible standards; this made him a better artist, he believed, though it also tormented him. A substandard performance could haunt him, throw him into a depression, and he worried that he could not live up to people's expectations of him (posters advertising him as the "world's greatest pianist" filled him with dread). To praise him to his face was merely to provoke his suspicion. In his notebooks and interviews, he pitilessly critiqued his own work, finding fault even with some of his most legendary recordings. A by-product of this modesty was an absence of professional jealousy and a deference toward his peers. He said he banished Chopin's E-minor concerto and Beethoven's "Emperor" from his repertoire because he could never equal Neuhaus's interpretations of them, and, asked why he never performed Bach's *Goldberg Variations*, he replied, "Well, I heard Glenn Gould in Moscow ..."

If one thing was predictable about Richter—about his career, his character, his performances—it was unpredictability. Mystery always surrounded him; you never knew what he might do next. His eccentricities were legendary. He detested the telephone, refused to use it, and forgot his own number. He loved to drive whenever possible, hated flying, even took trains with reluctance. He would disappear for days at a time, to the distress of impresarios, then suddenly reappear, refreshed, as though nothing had happened. He had a reputation

Richter toured Canada in 1964, and the United States in 1965 and 1970, but after that he refused to return to North America. (Recitals in New York, Chicago, and San Francisco were announced, out of the blue, in 1990, but ultimately canceled.) He didn't return to North America for a simple reason: he didn't like it. Even of his triumphant 1960 tour he said, "Initially I found this other hemisphere psychologically and geographically horrible. I was afraid, and did not know what I was afraid of." In 1986, he remarked wryly, "There are three marvelous things in the U.S.A.: the picture galleries, the orchestras, and the cocktails ..." Yet, that same year, he spent three months from July through December touring Siberia by car and train, and after a residency in Japan, touring back across Siberia all over again—in all, close to a hundred concerts in numerous towns, some of which had never before hosted a piano recital. Here was a universally lionized artist who refused lucrative offers to return to Carnegie Hall but who relished the opportunity to perform in Orekhovo-Zuevo, Petropavlovsk, Tchegdymn, Chouchenskoe, Tselinograd ... #### The 1958 Budapest Concert

Richter's reading of Schubert's C-minor sonata, as we have it here, is, even by Richter's standards, an extraordinarily intense and searching performance. It's typical of him that, in this most mercurial of composers, with its wide range of contrasting themes and moods, he finds an underlying unity and sustained tension, a steady forward tread throughout. It's also characteristic that he doesn't rush the first movement—he takes it at one of the slowest tempos on record, and, as in so many of his performances, the spaciousness lets all the counterpoint, all the details of the harmony and keyboard texture register, without sacrifice of momentum. The slow movement, a deeply poetic statement occasionally rent by eruptions of stern octaves, and flat, his Mozart heavy and humorless—his Haydn was more interesting. Certain musical qualities were not native to his arsenal: it is not to Richter that we should look for the salon charm of the smaller Chopin pieces, for the lilt and Schwung of the Viennese waltz, for the jazz and blues of Gershwin, and he admitted that he was less at ease with the galant Bach of the suites than the strict, Olympian Bach of the fugues.)

Richter's reading of Webern's *Variations* offers another fine example of his unwillingness to compromise an interpretation. Just about everyone plays this piece in around five or six minutes, a timing that seems perfectly appropriate, even self-evident, but Richter took more than ten minutes. Another example of his perversity, his eccentricity, his notorious penchant for slow tempos and broad time scales? But then look at the last measure in the score, under which Webern has written, plain as day, "10 min." Perhaps other pianists don't notice Webern's timing, or dismiss it as absurd. Not Richter. As in the Schubert sonata, he did not ignore or rationalize away the composer's instruction and return to the more comfortable ground of a conventional interpretation. Rather, he took the composer at his word, and did what was necessary to make the resulting interpretation work. It says much about Richter as an interpreter that he should make such an eye-opening statement in what was hardly, for him, characteristic repertoire.

The present recording shows off a quality that endeared Richter to colleagues and audiences alike: his courage—a by-product of his integrity. He was not one to shy away from risks, to play safely (The Monsaingeon film includes a clip of him playing Chopin's C-sharp-minor étude, Op. 10 No. 4, with a speed and ferocity that seems impossible—in a private setting, admittedly, but we know he played the piece the same way in public.) The flat-out playing one hears throughout this recording is simply routine Richter—if such playing can ever be called routine. In the furiously galloping finale of Schubert's C-minor sonata, and even more stunningly in Schumann's treacherous Toccata, he hurls himself into the music, playing caution-to-the-winds, at suicidal tempos that he does not bend for the sake of technical expediency. The results are not note-perfect. Indeed, Richter performances were sometimes ornamented with fistfuls of wrong notes—no pianist of comparable reputation got away with so many digital glitches—but like an athlete who plays through pain, he would play through the technical tangles without fear, refusing to sacrifice the integrity of the interpretation—the right tempo, the right dynamic, the right phrasing. It is in this disregard for caution that you see sure evidence of Richter's commitment to the music he plays.

Richter underscores (without exaggerating) the Beethovenian drama, the surge and blaster, of the C-minor sonata, as well as the characteristically Schubertian lyricism, which he renders with much introspection, especially in the slow movement, a deeply poetic statement occasionally rent by eruptions of stern octaves. (Incidentally, one of the few studio recordings that satisfied Richter was the album of this sonata he made in 1972.) Richter was always at home in the music of Schumann, perfectly suited to realizing its moodiness, quirkiness, and mercurial temperament, and the composer's fondness for extremes matched the performer's tendency to push every aspect of performance to its limit. This performance of the *Toccata* comes close to wildness, but at the same time is somehow probing and transparent, thanks to Richter's breathtaking technical command.

The highlight of this concert is the performance of Musorgsky's *Pictures at an Exhibition*, one of Richter's signature pieces. This performance offers basically the same interpretation as the legendary Sofia recording made two weeks later, but quite possibly an even better performance of it, with fewer finger slips and a greater command of every facet of the music (poetry, wit, fire-and-brimstone, the grotesque—this was a work that suggested to Richter "a whole world in its myriad contradictions"). Equally important, the Budapest performance is more closely and sympathetically recorded than the Sofia one (though the audience seems not much healthier). Again, it is the courage of this performance that deserves to be singled out—the huge risks he takes, most of which pay off brilliantly. Unless the record, Richter hated orchestral versions of this piece—indeed, disapproved of transcriptions on principle, unless made by the composer. "Much as I love Ravel's music, I find his orchestral transcription of Musorgsky's *Pictures at an Exhibition* an abomination," he said, "a terrible, decorative travesty of the most profound masterpiece of Russian piano music."

Richter was a quintessential exponent of the Grand Manner in Russian Romantic repertoire, as demonstrated by his first encore in Budapest, a deeply felt reading of Rachmaninov's famous G-sharp-minor prelude. But as his other two encores show, his Debussy was no less potent than his Romantic playing. (He admired many great composers, but, as he said, reserved his passion for just three: Wagner, Chopin, and Debussy.) In Debussy, he was generous with the sustaining pedal without sacrificing his trademark clarity of phrase; his tone is firm and clean-edged, vibrant and strongly projected, his interpretations strongly expressed but also unfussy, and structurally solid. Richter seems to want to classicize Debussy here: he offers poised, articulated readings, vigorously but most carefully played, sweetly expressive but never fawning or kitschy. It is still very beautiful and colourful playing, however, sometimes ravishing, and it weaves a real spell on the listener (especially "Bells through the Leaves"). Richter obviously wanted to reveal the inherent power of Debussy's music, to set it on a large stage, in strong relief and in bold colors. If you prefer your Debussy on the watery side, look elsewhere.
