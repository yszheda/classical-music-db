### HAYDN STRING QUARTETS
### OP. 71 & OP. 74

The three string quartets contained in Haydn's Op. 71 are valued by those who know them. Performers cherish a few of the movements as deeply as anything in Haydn, and feel that while chamber music had been from time to time often before him, and would be often again after him, it often is less generally known than the neighboring Op. 74 group and the neighboring Op. 76 group. Haydn's own publisher Tobias Haslinger wrote to the Op. 71 quartets with a letter in which he said that although these works were his masterpieces: "They are not, of course, entirely not lacking in charm." We have no record of Haydn's reaction to this patronizing statement.

The string quartet part of the opuses has to do, one suspects, with the vagaries of concert programming. The 19th century as we common practice in publish in collections, especially in the string quartet medium, tended to view one set of chamber music, keyboard pieces, etc.) as a unit and to program all the quartets or trios in a set. Bach's partitas and his preludes and fugues were issued in sets, as likewise the sonatas of Beethoven. When a quartet society would make a list of the quartets it would make an exception for Op. 71, and would program a number of quartets from the Enlightenment, background sufficient to explain the fact that in his corresponding collections such as his set of piano trios Op. 70, Haydn would write a preface that his set of trios would be "in general with string quartets (that is, a publisher would issue a collection of keyboard trios with string accompaniment). The quartet was not the same as the thoroughly competent amateur who dominated the market for 18th-century domestic chamber music; he pursued a career of chamber music at home; players, then, would often find themselves in the company of their betters, but more servilely to be played through and picked from, than are publications such as the harpsichord sonatas of Mozart, or the piano trios of Haydn. The quartets that remain as Op. 71 were published separately by Artaria in 1795.

Necessarily, variety and balance are the essentials of a good collection, and a sound overall plan makes the set coherent. String quartets, immediately accessible to the ear, were of far more subtle work, cooler in texture, and in their own way as deeply thought. The three quartets of Op. 74 are, taken as a set, stronger and more unified than Op. 71. This is the first Haydn quartet to be published in London, and it is a work which, as we shall see, Haydn wrote for the quartet to play at his concerts in London. The three quartets of Op. 71 were published separately by Artaria in 1795.

There are many traces of approaching Op. 71 and Op. 74 in Haydn's earlier quartets. But the two sets are far enough advanced in style to have been written after his return from his second trip to England. The Op. 71 quartets are the first to be called by the name of "Quatuors concertants," meaning that all four instruments are treated as equal partners, rather than the first violin with accompaniment. This is a major step forward in the development of the string quartet as a genre.

The two opuses were written during and just after Haydn's second trip to London (1794-95). They were dedicated to the young Hungarian Count Anton Appony. Haydn met Appony in London, and the Count commissioned these quartets for his private concerts. The dedication reads: "To His Excellency Count Anton Appony, Chamberlain to His Imperial and Royal Majesty."

**Quartet in B flat major, Op. 71, No. 1**

This sufficiently cheerful work opens the set and triple solo (first violin) preface the first subject. The preface in B flat major is a movement of great charm and wit. The first subject is a lively tune in the first violin, with accompaniment in the other three instruments. The second subject is in the dominant key (F major), and is more lyrical in character. The development section treats both subjects with Haydn's characteristic inventiveness. The recapitulation brings back both subjects in the tonic key, with some delightful variations.

The slow movement (Adagio) is in E flat major, the subdominant key. It is a movement of great beauty, with a singing melody in the first violin, accompanied by the other three instruments. The middle section modulates to the minor mode, adding a touch of pathos. The movement concludes with a return to the main theme, beautifully varied.

The Menuetto is in B flat major, with a Trio in G minor. The Menuetto is a vigorous dance, with strong accents on the first beat of each bar. The Trio is more lyrical, with a flowing melody in the second violin. The return to the Menuetto is marked by a dynamic contrast.

The finale (Vivace) is in sonata form, with a lively first subject and a more lyrical second subject. The development section is characterized by Haydn's characteristic humor and wit. The movement concludes with a brilliant coda.

**Quartet in D major, Op. 71, No. 2**

The D major quartet opens with a slow introduction (Adagio) that leads to an Allegro movement. The introduction is in D minor, creating a dramatic contrast with the main body of the movement, which is in D major. The first subject is a lively tune, and the second subject is more lyrical. The development section treats both subjects with Haydn's characteristic inventiveness.

The Andante cantabile is in G major, the dominant key. It is a movement of great beauty, with a singing melody that passes between the four instruments. The middle section modulates to the minor mode, adding emotional depth. The movement concludes with a return to the main theme.

The Menuetto is in D major, with a Trio in B minor. The Menuetto is a vigorous dance, while the Trio is more lyrical and contemplative.

The finale (Allegretto) is in rondo form, with a cheerful main theme that returns several times, interspersed with contrasting episodes. The movement concludes with a brilliant coda.

**Quartet in E flat major, Op. 71, No. 3**

The E flat major quartet opens with a Vivace movement in sonata form. The first subject is a lively tune, and the second subject is more lyrical. The development section is characterized by Haydn's characteristic humor and inventiveness.

The Andante con moto is in A flat major, the subdominant key. It is a movement of great beauty, with a singing melody that passes between the four instruments. The middle section adds emotional depth through modulation.

The Menuetto is in E flat major, with a Trio in C minor. The Menuetto is a vigorous dance, while the Trio is more dramatic and intense.

The finale (Vivace) is in sonata form, with a lively first subject and a more lyrical second subject. The movement concludes with a brilliant and humorous coda.

**Quartet in C major, Op. 74, No. 1**

The C major quartet opens with a sustained chord (timpani effect) resolved by an abrupt one-bar preface (solo violin), followed by the first subject (first violin) and the silence (silence is essential to the effect) of the other three instruments. This preface does two things simultaneously and with a stroke of genius. It focuses the listener's attention on the tonic sonority, and by a pre-emptory gesture secures attention. The subject that follows is indeed what the Germans call a "Satzen" a movement in the nature of a proposition. The first subject is a lively tune, and the second subject is in the dominant key (G major).

The Adagio is in F major, the subdominant key. It is a movement of great beauty, with a singing melody in the first violin. The middle section modulates to the minor mode, adding emotional depth.

The Menuetto is in C major, with a Trio in A major. The Menuetto is a vigorous dance, while the Trio is more lyrical and remote.

The finale (Vivace) is in sonata form, with a lively first subject and a more lyrical second subject. The movement is characterized by Haydn's characteristic humor and wit.

**Quartet in F major, Op. 74, No. 2**

The F major quartet opens with a unison preface fit for a symphony. The first subject, closely modeled upon the introduction, retains its exuberance but dispenses with its pre-emptory manner. As in the previous quartet, there are surprises in the matter of key relationships. Thus after the tonic key (F major) has been confirmed in the first statement of the first subject, a prolonged pause ensues. Then suddenly the theme leaps out boldly in an entirely unexpected key (G minor). There are lighter moments by way of contrast, but a certain nervous energy propels the movement along. This is the fourth quartet in the series, and by now Haydn has moved considerable distance from the finely spun intimacies of the first quartet (Op. 71, No. 1). In this work (Op. 74, No. 2) the opening movement is directed with muscular and, at times, with genuinely dramatic force.

The andante that follows is scaled to size and has long been recognized as one of his profoundest creations. It is here, as analogously in his later symphonies, that we find the slow movement emerging as the emotional center of gravity in a large scale design. The Menuetto (F major) is forceful, high spirited and, at moments, angular. By contrast, there is a strangeness in the mild Trio, for it appears softly and suddenly in a distant key (D flat).

The finale (Presto) is a study in chiaroscuro. The first eight measures, and their repetition, are high, scored for just the upper three instruments. The next six measures (for all four) are suddenly low. The movement is enveloped in a brilliant scattering of bright sonorities, but thrice this exuberance is interrupted for a lovely progression of soft, low, velvety string sound moving over a throbbing pedal in the 'cello part. It is characteristic of Haydn that he should choose to make luscious and somewhat mysterious sounds amid such giddy surroundings. Sometimes this is referred to as his "kittenish" manner, but the word is really quite inept for, despite some mythology to the contrary, Haydn is not at all a coy or cute composer. If anything, this movement is reminiscent of that interesting gleam that looks out at us from some of his portraits, and "kittenish" is hardly the word for it.

**Quartet in G Minor, Op. 74, No. 3**

This quartet is easily the most widely performed and the best known in the series of Op. 71 and Op. 74. Commonly, it is referred to as the Rider Quartet, or the Horseman Quartet, the title having been suggested, it would see, by the bumpy and rather rambunctious preface to the first movement, as well as by the bouncing syncopations and the brilliant take-off of the finale. A lesser-known quartet warrants more studied comment, but here the listener encounters an old favorite.

The first subject (allegro con brio) is introduced by the four instruments in unison. The theme is characterized by a rhythmic figure that suggests the galloping of horses. The second subject is more lyrical and provides a contrast to the energetic first subject.

The Largo assai is in E flat major, the relative major of G minor. It is a movement of great depth and emotion, with a singing melody that passes between the four instruments. The middle section modulates to the minor mode, adding intensity.

The Menuetto is in G minor, with a Trio in G major. The Menuetto is a vigorous and dramatic dance, while the Trio is more lyrical and pastoral.

The finale (Allegro con brio) is in sonata form, with a brilliant and energetic first subject and a more lyrical second subject. The movement concludes with a thrilling coda.

### ABOUT THE PERFORMERS

The Griller String Quartet was founded in 1928 when its members were all students at the Royal Academy of Music in London. Trained and advised, through its formative years, by the great quartet teacher and violist, Frederick Griller, they rapidly became leaders in their field. By 1932 they were completely devoted to the quartet medium and late that year were named by Arnold Schoenberg and Arturo Toscanini. They gave the first performance of Schoenberg's Third Quartet in England and worked closely with Bartók on his quartets. They toured extensively throughout Europe, North America, and Australia. The quartet was known for its interpretations of Haydn, Beethoven, and Schubert.

格里勒弦乐四重奏团于 1928 年成立，当时所有成员都是伦敦皇家音乐学院的学生。在他们成长的岁月里，他们接受了伟大的四重奏教师和中提琴家弗雷德里克·格里勒 (Frederick Griller) 的培训和指导，迅速成为该领域的领军人物。到 1932 年，他们完全致力于四重奏领域，同年晚些时候被阿诺德·勋伯格和阿图尔·托斯卡尼尼认可。他们在英格兰首演了勋伯格的第三四重奏，并与巴托克密切合作研究他的四重奏作品。他们在欧洲、北美和澳大利亚广泛巡演。该四重奏团以对海顿、贝多芬和舒伯特作品的诠释而闻名。
