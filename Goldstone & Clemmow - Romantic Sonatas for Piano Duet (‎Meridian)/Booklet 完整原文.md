## ROMANTIC SONATAS FOR PIANO DUET
### Anthony Goldstone and Caroline Clemmow

---

**Ignaz Moscheles (1794-1870)**
Grande Sonate in E flat major, Op. 47

The many compositions of the German-Bohemian Ignaz Moscheles were for a long period rated extremely highly. In his twenties he was hailed as a front-rank virtuoso. Beethoven commissioned him to make a piano reduction of Fidelio and at the end of his life entrusted him a sonata with which he intended that Moscheles should realise his idea for Beethoven's funeral. Particularly admired in Britain, he settled in London in 1826, among other important duties composing the famous Study for the Preservation of the Order of the Hands. He collaborated with Chopin on the latter's cello sonata, and was one of the first to play Schubert in 1845 and 1845 acting as regular conductor of the Philharmonic Society. Moscheles received piano lessons from him in Berlin at the age of fifteen, became a close friend, championed his music for piano duet, and as a young man took part in the first piano professor in his newly formed Leipzig Conservatory.

The highly successful duet style established by Schubert was by mid-nineteenth and was for decades. Moscheles described a command performance before the French royal family in October 1839, with Chopin "playing the bass, a thing he always insists on...[it] was a great success. I had an illumination, and M. Amboise [the Duke d'Orleans]...was so struck...I can assure you it is a clear rendezvous"...so we played it again...I had the illumination, and I was very pleased with myself. I am really very happy." Dedicated to the Archduke Rudolf, it is full of fun but also fine musical content; very difficult and lively; it is made up of four movements and for four hands. This recording is of the original final version, not the later shortened one.

The opening movement launches directly into the dynamic first subject, which unexpectedly turns into a black and white passage in the minor key; the second subject, arriving after a calm pause, is an expressive tune with an almost Schubertian simplicity. The scherzo is a quicksilver dance which rushes from both themes. It is no surprise that the Andantino was enshrined by the Queen, a deliciously nostalgic piece (on "Gipsy Life") which is first heard on the upper piano register of the supreme nonchalance and in the coda the opening motto of another famous symphony makes a third appearance.

The finale, brief, is finely paired with mock solemnity before the perpetuators make their escape as the curtain falls.

The distant key of E major is home for the Adagio, in effect a bel canto aria, and we see why Rosamunde was described as the most "musical" of composers in its poignant beauty. The "middle" section however there is no reprise but hushed chords are rudely interrupted by the finale; leading us back into E flat. The merriment is now irrepressible; laced with marvellous twists and turns, and with a patter song for the second subject. Moscheles' son Felix later wrote how his father and Mendelssohn played together, often improvising "four hands there might be, but only one soul, so it seemed, and they would catch with lightning speed at each other's ideas." One can imagine these mischievous equal contests, and indeed playing this sonata at the age of fifteen with its composer must have helped to mould Mendelssohn's principal pupil as a player and one can certainly note similarities with Mendelssohn's Octet in the same key written soon after.

**Zdenek Fibich (1850-1900)**
Sonata in B flat major, Op. 28

One of Moscheles' last piano students at Leipzig was Zdenek Fibich, who was to become one of the leading lights of the great Czech musical renaissance. While his two colleagues were towards the German romantics that he quite soon realised the importance of his roots, with his works perhaps his most important contribution to the National Theatre in Prague are tinged with Czech colour. Though he wrote operas, symphonies etc., some of his most beautiful compositions were written for the piano, notably the Moods, Impressions and Reminiscences.

Dating from 1880, the lovely duet sonata began a new confident period in Fibich's output. The dedication to Theodor Kirchner may account for the predominantly Germanic manner—the sonata does not suffer in comparison—the whole work is characterised by a homely charm, and there is little sign of the nationalist fervour then sweeping the country. The solo form of the relative minor (the first movement) as early as the second half of the opening bar), it is also the key which brings the work to an end, though the intervening movements wander on a rustic second subject. In a felicitous touch the rising oscillating quavers in the development become the accompaniment to the reprise of the opening melody, this time heard in the tenor register. The second movement has an attractive simple theme with five variations and a coda, while the finale has an infectious sparkle, a quirky transitional theme and a second subject consisting of rising octaves in the bass.

**Hermann Goetz (1840-1876)**
Sonata in G minor, Op. 17

It is a tragedy for us that Hermann Goetz died before his thirty-sixth birthday. After reading mathematics and physics at the university of his home town of Konigsberg in Germany he settled not in his native land, but in Switzerland. In Berlin he studied under Friedrich Kiel (1821-1890). He took up an organ post in Winterthur, Switzerland, when he began his friendship with the Swiss poet and the highest level of chamber music. It was in Switzerland where he began seriously deteriorating, he nevertheless produced a highly successful opera on Shakespeare's Taming of the Shrew, two symphonies, a fine violin concerto, a Mass, and a symphony of which G.B. Shaw wrote in 1893 (sixteen years after Goetz's death): "It is the only symphony by a composer of the first rank that has been written in the last twenty years. Put it on at your next concert and you will see." In comparison to him. Pinch of salt notwithstanding, this puts Goetz in the right league.

Goetz was twenty-five when he completed the duet sonata. The solemn Bach-inspired introduction on a long pedal bass uncannily anticipates the opening of Saint-Saens' second piano concerto in the same key, written a couple of years later. Upward octave leaps, long sequences and imitative counterpoint combine to produce a yearning effect, and the restless first subject of the main movement which follows is a transformation of the motto of the introduction. There is a dark overlay to the sometimes Mendelssohnian rhythmic character of this movement, but the lyrical second subject with its descent-accompaniment provides welcome consolation. The development is a model of how basses falling and rising in steps can create ebb and flow of tension, and just before the end of the movement there is a special moment of suspended animation.

The second movement is a real treasure, a song without words which shows Goetz's personal melodic gifts and inventive textures. The coda is particularly magical, making the funeral march which began the last movement seem even more desolate. The finale proper, though graceful in rhythm, is like the first movement, permeated with darker colours. A brief episodic interlude that may bring some repose, but the agitated counterpoint will not be subdued and a reappearance of the funeral march precedes a turbulent close. Ten years later Goetz was claimed by long-standing tuberculosis and the world lost a fine and individual talent.

## ANTHONY GOLDSTONE and CAROLINE CLEMMOW

Both internationally known artists with multi-faceted careers, the husband and wife team of Goldstone and Clemmow formed their duo in 1984, since when they have given concerts all over Britain as well as abroad. They broadcast regularly, have appeared in many festivals and possess an extensive and enterprising repertoire on one and two pianos: cycles of the complete Mozart and Schubert duets, premieres of Rimsky-Korsakov, Holst, Franck, Herzogenberg, Alkan and so on. Critical acclaim has greeted their recordings, and their refreshing approach to concerts, including informal but informative introductions, creates a warm rapport with audiences. "We must be enormously grateful to them." - The Daily Telegraph: "A quite formidable piano duo" - CD Review.
