## Szymanowski/Janáček: String Quartets
*By Antony Beaumont*

### Facts

Karol Szymanowski originally conceived his *String Quartet No. 1, Op. 37* in four movements, with the scherzo in second place, and the ensuing Andante followed by a fugal finale. Composed in the autumn of 1917 amidst the turmoil of the October Revolution, the work remained unperformed until 1924, when it was published in Vienna and premiered in Warsaw on 10 March by the Warsaw Philharmonic Quartet. Zofia Helman prepared a corrected version of the score for the *Szymanowski Complete Edition* (Cracow/Vienna, 1984). The scherzo-finale (Vivace) is an ingenious study in polytonality, with each instrument playing in a different major key: first violin in A, second violin in F sharp, viola in E flat and cello in C.

The *String Quartet No. 1* by Leoš Janáček, based on Tolstoy's novella *The Kreutzer Sonata*, was actually his second essay in the medium, preceded in the spring of 1880 by a three-movement student work (now lost). This first 'official' quartet was composed in October – November 1923 and dedicated to the Bohemian Quartet, who premiered the work in Prague on 17 October 1924. Josef Suk, second violinist of that ensemble, gave the world premiere on 14 May 1929, and he himself a composer of stature, edited the work for publication (Prague, 1925).

Since then, other editors have attempted to decipher the manuscript with greater precision, removing a substantial layer of performing tradition in the process. The first revised edition, prepared by Otakar Šourek (Prague, 1945), was followed by those of Milan Škampa (Prague, 1973), and Miloš Štědroň with Leoš Faltus (Janáček Critical Edition, Prague, 2000).

Szymanowski composed his *String Quartet No. 2, Op. 56* in 1927 for a competition organised by the Musical Fund Society of Philadelphia. The outcome was disappointing for him, with the first prize awarded jointly to Béla Bartók (*String Quartet No. 3*) and Alfredo Casella (*Serenade* for Five Instruments). Szymanowski, who never found much of a following in the US, returned home empty-handed. Like the first quartet, the second is cast in three movements. This time, however, the promise of a fugue is fulfilled, and the 'finale problem' that had beset him in Op. 37 is solved by welding the adagio and concluding allegro into one seamless entity. ### Facts (German version)

Karol Szymanowski konzipierte sein Erstes Streichquartett op. 37 als viersätziges Werk mit einem Scherzo an zweiter Stelle; nach dem Andante sollte eine Fuge den Abschluß bilden. Das Quartett entstand inmitten der Oktoberrevolution im Herbst 1917 und blieb vorerst unaufgeführt. Im Jahre 1924 erfolgte die Drucklegung in Wien sowie die Uraufführung am 10. März durch das Philharmonische Quartett in Warschau. Im Rahmen der Szymanowski-Gesamtausgabe (Krakow/Wien 1984) sorgte Zofia Helman für eine korrigierte Neuausgabe. Das Scherzo-Finale (Vivace) gibt sich als polytonale Anlage, wobei die 1. Violine durchgehend in A-Dur bewegt, die 2. Violine in Fis-Dur, die Bratsche in Es-Dur und das Cello in C-Dur.

Leoš Janáčeks Erstes Streichquartett, nach Leo Tolstois Erzählung *Die Kreutzersonate*, war eigentlich sein zweites, denn ihm ging ein dreisätziges, im Frühjahr 1880 komponiertes, seitdem verschollenes Studentenwerk voraus. Das endgültig "Erstes Quartett" bezeichnete Spätwerk entstand im Oktober – November 1923 und ist in Prag am 17. Oktober 1924 vom Widmungsträger, dem Böhmischen Quartett, uraufgeführt worden.

### More Facts...

When the sixty-two-year-old Janáček met the twenty-seven-year-old Kamila Stösslová, wife of an antique dealer from Brno, he fell passionately in love with her. That love remained unrequited and unfulfilled, but Stösslová, wise enough to realise that she had involuntarily become the muse to a great composer, she maintained this for more than twenty years: in a letter dated October 1924, he wrote to her of his Quartet No. 1:

> I imagined a woman, miserable, suffering, depicted by the great Russian author Tolstoy in his *Kreutzer Sonata*. Having composed a piano trio in 1909 but subsequently lost or destroyed, here he made a second attempt to transform the message into musical sounds and forms. Milan Škampa, violist of the Smetana Quartet, has observed that the four movements depict the phases of a Greek tragedy: exposition, peripeteia, crisis and catharsis. The 'critical' third movement opens in G flat minor with a canonic allusion to the E major second subject of Beethoven's *Kreutzer Sonata, Op. 47*, several times interrupted in second violin and viola by wild, asynchronous outbursts based on a four-note figure derived from it. Important as the Tolstoy connection must have been to Janáček himself, the work makes its effect without any foreknowledge of its literary origins. For all intents and purposes, the titles of Janáček's two quartets are interchangeable.

The line through history from the French violin virtuoso Rodolphe Kreutzer via Beethoven and Tolstoy to Janáček extends further into our time and indeed onto this disc. In 2002, the Dutch writer Margriet de Moor published her best-selling novel *Kreutzersonate: when passion and disaster*, a dramatic and passionate story set in the Royal Conservatory in The Hague. de Moor first came into contact with members of the Schoenberg Quartet. Her novel draws inspiration from recording this quartet, which the Schoenberg Quartet does here after Milan Škampa's 1973 edition, but also incorporates Škampa's later corrections and additions. For the *Second Quartet*, the preferred edition is the Šourek edition, enhanced once again by Škampa, whose alterations are based upon intensive study of the manuscript sources and years of experience with this repertoire, both as performer and teacher.

## Background: Intimate Letters

Between January and February 1928, Janáček interrupted work on his opera *From the Dead House* to write his second string quartet. It was to be called **'Intimate Letters'** (originally 'Love Letters'), and was inspired by Kamila Stösslová, whom the sixty-two-year-old composer had met the previous summer at Luhačovice Spa. The composer had fallen in love with her, and in a series of daily letters to her over the last years of his life she became his muse. The piece has four movements, and Janáček explained that the first movement recalls their first meeting, the second the growing sense of longing, the third the happiness that came from their shared holidays and the fourth the future after his death.

> I have have made a picture of you here in the notes:
> unglucklich, leidend, geschlagen, elend, wie
> in seiner "Kreutzersonate" gezeichnet hat.

Already in 1909 he had written (indecipherable) a piano trio on the same theme, which was subsequently lost or destroyed. Here he made a second attempt on the same theme.

The interpretation by the Schoenberg Quartet is based on Milan Škampa's edition of 1973, but also incorporates Škampa's later corrections and additions. For the *Second Quartet*, the preferred edition is the Šourek edition, enhanced once again by Škampa, whose alterations are based upon intensive study of the manuscript sources and years of experience with this repertoire, both as performer and teacher.

### The Schoenberg Quartet

the Quartet share a strong interest in the composers of the Second Viennese School — Arnold Schoenberg, Alban Berg, Anton Webern and Alexander Zemlinsky — and the complete works for strings by these composers form the heart of their repertoire. Their programmes range, however, from the music of Debussy and Brahms to that of composers of the present day, highlighting the music of the first decades of the twentieth century. Besides concerts and broadcasts in The Netherlands, the Schoenberg Quartet has given performances in most European countries as well as in Canada and the United States. Its performances for Dutch television of Schoenberg's eight works for strings were broadcast worldwide and the Quartet appears on the Rhombus film *My War Years* about the life of Arnold Schoenberg.

Between 1983 and 1991 the Quartet worked frequently in Boston where it studied with the violist and quartet coach Eugene Lehner, formerly a member of the Kolisch Quartet which in the 1920s and 1930s worked closely with Schoenberg, Webern, and Berg. The Friends of the Arnold Schoenberg Institute in Los Angeles awarded the Schoenberg Quartet Honorary Life Membership in 1989, an honour previously accorded to such artists as Pierre Boulez, Felix Galimir and Eugene Lehner. The Quartet has issued numerous CDs with works by Andriessen, Brahms, Chausson, Dutilleux, Ketting, Pijper, Reger, Roussel, Schulhoff, Shostakovich, van Vlijmen and Vermeulen, among others, and on nine CDs for Chandos has recorded the complete works for strings by Schoenberg, Webern, Berg and Zemlinsky, a unique event in recording history.
