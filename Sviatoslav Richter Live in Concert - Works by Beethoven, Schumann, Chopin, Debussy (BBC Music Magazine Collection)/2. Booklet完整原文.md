## Listening guide

### Master of the keys

*Julian Haylock enjoys a stunning recital by Sviatoslav Richter, in which the Russian pianist's sublime technique and rare versatility are displayed in full*

THIS RECITAL BY Sviatoslav Richter at London's Royal Festival Hall on 11 May 1969 opens with the Op. 22 Sonata by Beethoven – a composer who by all accounts was himself a formidable presence at the keyboard, compensating for a less than transcendental technique with outbursts of temperament and passion that left audiences stunned and pianos in need of serious repair. To hear any of his 32 Piano Sonatas is to experience a vital part of a spiritual journey that occupied him for over a quarter of a century.

Written in 1800, the Op. 22 Sonata is a pianistic culmination of the early-period techniques Beethoven had been honing for over a decade. The opening *Allegro con brio* is a physically exuberant celebration of sonata style, matched by an expansive, meditative *Adagio* (also in sonata form) that anticipates the profound introspection of the middle-period slow movements. The *Menuetto* is thoroughly unconventional both in terms of its melodic shape and rhythmic outlines, setting up a minor key middle section with syncopated held chords sounded against bustling semiquavers in the left hand. The sonata ends with an easy-going *Rondo: Allegretto* which subtly integrates elements of variation form along the way. Tellingly, Beethoven's next sonata (in A flat, Op. 26) opens with a full-scale theme and variations.

"I am affected by everything that goes on in the world,' Schumann revealed in a letter to his future wife Clara Wieck in 1837, 'and I think it all over in my own way – and then I long to express my feelings and find an outlet for them in music.' For a composer capable of producing full-scale masterpieces in a matter of days, the *Etudes symphoniques* absorbed an extraordinary amount of his time and energy. The main problem Schumann set himself was creating a set of variations on a theme (by the adoptive baron father of his ex-fiancée, Ernestine von Fricken) that would combine the genre's formal structures with his penchant for poetic fantasy. On reflection he felt his first draft leaned too far towards the latter. Five variations were therefore jettisoned which were posthumously rescued by Brahms for Breikopf's 1893 complete edition – Sviatoslav Richter plays them in published order as a group here, between 9:03 and 19:30).

It was a more robust set of variations that finally emerged in the first edition of 1837, complete with an exhilarating finale based, in part, on a theme from Heinrich Marschner's then highly popular opera *Der Templar and die Juedin* (The Templar and the Jewess), as suggested by the *Etudes symphoniques's dedicatee, William Sterndale Bennett. Schumann was still unhappy, however, and in 1852 produced a second edition shown short of two variations with a tauter version of the final section. Ten years later a posthumous edition emerged that kept the revised finale but reinstated the excised material. It is this version that forms the basis of most of today's performances.

Schumann devoted himself to the piano for the first decade of his composing career, whereas Chopin gave his entire life to it. Every one of the 250 pieces he composed in a creative lifetime spanning just 30 years featured the piano. His series of 21 nocturnes – Richter here plays the first of three published in 1834 as Op. 15 – gives the impression of being forged in a state of sublime inspiration. Yet, according to pianist-critic Carl Filtsch, 'when it came to writing them down and recapituring an original inspiration in all details, Chopin endured days of nervous strain and almost terrible despair.' 'I am increasingly convinced,' wrote Debussy to his publisher, 'that music is not, in essence, a thing which can be cast into a traditional and fixed form. It is made up of colours and rhythms.' This is the key that unlocks the door to the last decade of his creative life in which he embarked on a process of expressive refinement that saw all inessentials discarded. *Cloches à travers les feuilles* ('Bells through the leaves') from the second book of *Images* (1907) was inspired by a letter from his friend, music critic and scholar Louis Lalo, in which he described the tolling of a passing bell being carried from village to village between Vespers on All Saints' Eve and the Mass for the Dead.

## Artist biography

### SVIATOSLAV RICHTER (piano)

On 20 March 2015 the music world marks the 100th anniversary of the birth of Sviatoslav Richter, one of the greatest and most versatile pianists of the 20th century.

Richter was born in the Ukraine into a musical household: his father was a German pianist and composer who had studied in Vienna. From the age of seven, he began learning piano and composition and in his teens he worked as an accompanist, giving his first recital in Odessa, at the age of 19. In the late 1930s, he entered the Moscow Conservatory. Despite being expelled three times for refusing to take exams, he was reinstated on each occasion on the insistence of his teacher, Heinrich Neuhaus. In 1940 he made his official debut with works including Prokofiev's Sixth Sonata – an ongoing association later resulted in the composer dedicating his Ninth Sonata to him.

After World War II Richter enjoyed a major career both in the Soviet Union and in the West. For many years, until 1989, he toured prolifically, clocking up an average of 70 concerts a year – a wave of classic recordings, many of them live, included works by Liszt, Prokofiev and Schumann.

'His technique allows him an extraordinary freedom...' says *The New Grove Dictionary* about his performance style. 'He draws from the piano a range of tone colours varied, distinctive and recognisable from first utterance to last.' He died in 1997.
