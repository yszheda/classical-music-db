## 内页说明

HE WAS a born singer, with a voice of exceptional beauty and most unusual timbre – difficult to describe the best I can do is to say that it had a peculiarly pearly quality that made it instantly recognisable.
– Dame Isobel Baillie

HEDDLE NASH (1894-1961) was a Londoner who studied in Milan and made his début there in 1924 in The Barber of Seville. The next year saw his first appearance on an English stage, for the Old Vic Company, as the Duke in Rigoletto; that autumn he also sang Don Ottavio, Tamino, Faust, Pinkerton and Alfredo. In one of the busiest and most varied of singing lives he appeared for the British National Opera Company, at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden and at Glyndebourne (1934-38), made countless appearances in oratorio and at concerts, and recorded prolifically – a complete Faust and Messiah, both with Sir Thomas Beecham; his unrivalled interpretation of The Dream of Gerontius was given at the Albert Hall, Nash, as well as many single records of songs and arias, mostly in English. He was one of the sixteen singers for whom Ralph Vaughan Williams conceived his Serenade to Music.

Pride of place in this centenary tribute goes to the complete Act 4 of La Bohème, made in 1935 with Beecham conducting. Nash sang Rodolfo at Covent Garden that year to Dora Labbette's Mimì, and two days before making this recording they appeared together in the opera with Beecham and the Company on tour in Leeds. Labette had just begun to use the nom de plume Lisa Perli, to separate her operatic and concert careers; the disguise was quickly seen through, but the name sounded so authentic that it was a long time before anyone realized that perhaps she had adapted the name of her birthplace, Purley in Surrey.

Nash was Glyndebourne's first Ferrando when Così fan tutte was staged on the second night of the opening season (1934), and he naturally took part in the complete recording made at the end of the following season under Glyndebourne's musical director Fritz Busch: Un'aura amorosa comes from that fine set. Ina Souvez (1908-1992), the Fiordiligi at Glyndebourne, had already made some records for Columbia – mostly of excerpts from musicals – but now the company decided to capitalise on the sensational success of their first Glyndebourne season, and quickly recorded her in Come scoglio; a coupling, however, was more problematic (Per pietà, Fiordiligi's other aria, would have been too long). So Nash, also a Columbia artist, joined her for their duet in Act 2, with Clarence Raybould making sure that the music moved along sufficiently quickly to fit onto one side. Even so, the orchestral postlude had to be cut (as also happens in Don Ottavio's Il mio tesoro). The Columbia disc of Fra gli amplessi had been preferred for this reissue over its rival in the complete set on account of its rarity and its historical significance in pre-dating the Glyndebourne recording.

It was Don Ottavio that Nash made his Royal Opera House début with in 1929. Surprisingly, there are examples of him singing in Italian recorded at a time were never reissued during the LP era. Similarly, neither have the Duke's trio of arias from Rigoletto ever been gathered together before on one disc. They may be sung in English, as are Count Almaviva's serenade and the Serenade from The Barber of Seville, but they all display the great sense of style Nash invariably brought to such music. These qualities, allied to excellent diction and a fine presence on both stage and platform, made Heddle Nash the most sought-after English singer of his time.

The Serenade from The Fair Maid of Perth was perhaps Nash's most popular 78, and for years represented the only music that millions knew from an opera that was (and still is) rarely performed. Lastly comes a delightfully light-hearted performance of the duet towards the end of Act 1 of Die Fledermaus in which Dr Falke persuades his friend Eisenstein that there is time, before he reports to begin a jail sentence, to attend Prince Orlofsky's party. In this Nash is partnered by Dennis Noble (1891-1966) in roles that they both later carried to the stage of Covent Garden.
