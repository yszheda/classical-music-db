## LINER NOTES

### by CHARLES HAYNES

The ever-popular *Old Father Thames* is followed by the delightful *Phil the Fluter's Ball*, in which the singer seems to be in some linguistic trouble, since when the Irish accent deserts him for a moment one can be heard to creep back in! *Waltzing Matilda* requires no comment save perhaps the explanation that the singer's "billy" is a billy-can, a "tucker-bag" a dinner bag and a "jumbuck" a sheep. *Ol' Man River*, the song from Kern's "Show Boat" made famous by Paul Robeson, is a reminder that although Dawson sang as a baritone for much of his life he was originally a bass and retained his lower register all his career. *Waiata Poi* (the meaning of which is apparent when you hear the recording) has its origins in Maori folk music.

This recital concludes with two absolute "irresistibles". First, Newton's sprightly *The Drum-Major* and at the very end we hear a most unusual record, taken from an Edison cylinder of about 1912, although one would hardly guess this from the surprisingly good sound quality. *I am a Roamer* is, equally surprisingly, from a little-known operetta by Mendelssohn which dates from 1829. Peter Dawson here exploits the gravely bottom-end of his bass register in a performance of this hilarious patter-song of such rich humour (not to mention of this improbable diction) that it lingers long in the memory.

**— CHARLES HAYNES**

### PETER DAWSON

Peter Dawson was one of the most popular singers of his time. His straightforward, manly and attractive style of singing made him very acceptable to the audiences of the period of his greatest success – roughly the inter-war years – and if the nature of some of the material to which he selected may in some cases now seem dated, the voice with which he heard those records must still be relished.

Dawson's appeal as a singer was one. In a word, he represented robustness. Love of England, the English countryside, the spirit of adventure, the lure of the ocean, and above all else a cheerful appreciation of the military, and good, old-fashioned sentiment – these were his main topics. Peter Dawson loved his public and his public loved him; he lived comfortably.

Born in Adelaide, Australia, on the 31st January 1882 Peter Dawson, one of eight children, was the son of a Scottish captain who had retired from the sea and established a hardware business. Whether it was from this paternal background that the young Peter drew his subsequent love of nautical songs we do not know, but what we do know is that despite the father having perceived all children a professional musical education he did not disapprove of his youngest son's plans to follow a professional career in that field. But Peter and arrived to become a local church choir, one C. J. Stevens and in 1900, at he was only seventeen, he won first prize for bass solo in a eisteddfod competition. After this Stevens was able to persuade Dawson père to send his son to England for study with the great baritone Sir Charles Santley. This was done and, in due course, the young man soon received a valuable first engagement, a tour of the West of England (and, to the painter, perhaps, to his subsequent musical production) with his new teacher and Madame Albani. His actual début took place at the Guildhall, Plymouth, in 'Glorious Devon'. Despite this success, Dawson on his return to London found that work was slow to come, but by a fortunate chance he was invited by the Edison Bell Company to make a voice test for recording. This proved a most important turning point, for many years he was henceforth to become his principal means of livelihood, a type of activity. For his first record, a piece entitled *Arawata*, he received just £1.00. When he was about to be issued the company's recording manager, an American, suggested that he should change his name, saying "Every negro in America is called Peter." So the earliest cylinders appeared as being by Leonard Dawson. It was not long however before it was realised that Dawson was rapidly making a career for himself in the concert halls and that to his real name. So he became Peter Dawson.

In about 1910, a Russian singing master (sometimes known as van Dixhon) approached him and assured him it would be possible to extend his range considerably he then composed E Flat to D without damaging the quality of the voice. So successful was this scheme that Dawson's range was soon pushed up to the tenor A flat. It is said that Kantor even offered to make him the finest tenor of his age, but that the Australian wisely declined.

Peter Dawson's success on the concert platform (which saw some 6,000 performances) and in the recording studio continued, comparatively until well after the Second World War. In 1947, when he was already seventy years old, he made seventeen records, nearly a year, in a commercial concert tour and broadcast twenty-seven times. In 1955 he made a recording by the new process of "stereophonic" process; he retired the following year and died concluding a recording career that had spanned over fifty years, a feat surpassed only by his fellow baritone George Baker. In 1958 a ten-year tour took him to Sydney, where he died on 26th September 1961.

Peter Dawson was a natural recording artist. Dawn on the natural, power of tone and clarity of diction being essential prerequisites to those early records. As the artist himself was to write: "I always put diction, or if you prefer, enunciation, in the forefront of any rendition. I have from the beginning studied the lyric of a song until I knew it. In this way I have almost never failed – I recorded – no matter whether it was a grand or a classical number". And he added, tellingly, "I did not want any of my numbers to be unintelligible even to me by Sir Charles Santley."

The songs collected here are thoroughly representative of Peter Dawson's recorded repertoire and the themes upon which he so often touched. There are of course also included a number of popular arias, sung in English, and a selection of other songs of no specific appeal other than that they are all jolly good tunes.

We open with the song which is arguably more than any other has come to be regarded as Dawson's "signature tune" *The Floral Dance*, the one piece which has ensured the immortality of its composer, Katie Moss. The quaint old Cornish town referred to is Helston, and the Flora or Furry Dance is performed there annually to this day. The rousing *Glorious Devon* is from the pen of Edward German, of "Merrie England" fame. This music is so well suited to Dawson's voice, and the great baritone sang a fair amount of it. *Drake Goes West* and *Up from Somerset* are both arrangements of specific songs by Sanderson, whose other evergreens include *As I sitt bowand* and *Shipmate o' Mine*. *Up from Somerset* is more of a patriotic utterance than anything else in its offer by country folk to serve their monarch; at the time of its recording in 1914 it must have been considered obligatory, jingoistic fare. If the melody of the refrain seems particularly familiar, it is possibly because the line "we come up from Somerset, where the cider apples grow," was on one quite reasonably appropriated by a cider manufacturer to the jingle for a television commercial.

When the Sergeant-Major's on Parade is arguably the most popular military song in the repertoire. Longstaffe's robust piece is ideally suited to Dawson's manly style, and it was a favourite 'vehicle' of his.

As a young man Peter Dawson sang some roles in comic opera, but soon gave up this sphere of the opera, but soon gave up this sphere of the opera, but soon gave up this sphere. Many know the theme of the opening line, rather pointedly, of the ever popular, ingratiating second subject, here not overdone by Dawson, with what is clearly a twinkle in the eye.

The verses of Rudyard Kipling have inspired several settings, of which *The Smugglers' Song* is many of them. The Smugglers' Song, tackled by at least three singers. Mortimer's may not be the best-known, but it is certainly the most effective. We return to Edward German for *The Yeomen of England*, a hearty patriotic song, the fighting men of Elizabethan times, culled from "Merrie England".

What may broadly be described as "encores" (did Dawson ever really sing anything else?) make up the remainder of this record.
