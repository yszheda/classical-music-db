### The Grand Piano Era Essay

The Grand Piano Era offers seventy-one minutes of glittering piano playing in a diverse programme of repertoire ranging in a dozen entirely original compositions by the pianists themselves. All of these artists were extraordinary musicians, indeed paragons of genius, who transcended the barriers, indeed paragons of genius, of the piano, and life troubadours, roamed the globe with their musical messages. Each of the pianists represented here was an iconoclast and richly fascinating personality who contributed greatly to the prestige of the instrument. The earliest, born in 1860, was Paderewski and the latest, Grainger and Friedman in 1882; thus they form one generation of the golden age of the piano.

#### Ignaz Friedman

Ignaz Friedman was born in Podgorze, near Cracow, where Josef Hofmann had been born some six years earlier. An early teacher, Flora Grzywinska did a great deal for his musical development, and at eight he could transpose his fugues into various keys. After more training in composition and piano at Leipzig, he arrived in Berlin in 1901 with great ambition to become a concert pianist and, with this goal in mind, to study with the great pianist-pedagogue of the piano, Theodor Leschetizky, the teacher of Paderewski and many of the current stars of the piano. At first Leschetizky discouraged him, but, after four gruelling years, he became one of his master's favourites.

Late in 1904, the twenty-two year old Friedman was considered ready for his Vienna debut where he played the Tchaikovsky Concerto No. 1, Liszt's E flat Concerto and the Brahms B minor - a feat unprecedented at the time than now. The concert was a big success, and soon Friedman was playing recitals, concertos, accompanying singers and, with Hofmann, playing Beethoven Trio Sonatas, with Casals, Beethoven Cello Sonatas and his own cello sonata. One year later he said it was a trio "such as has never been before".

Through most of the First World War Friedman lived in Copenhagen. It was in Copenhagen that the late Danish pianist Victor Schjellerup Johan first heard him as a youngster, relating, "It was colossal. Now you'd wonder when I hear his recordings I understand why. He had an indelibly big technique - nobody plays like Friedman."

Friedman made an American tour in 1920 when he recorded a considerable repertoire for the Duo-Art Piano, including two reproductions on this CD. One is an incomparable Don Juan fantasy based on Mozart's opera; a hero it is evident since the composition was too long to be contained on one roll, and could not conveniently be divided into two parts. The cuts are glaring. The second selection is from a set of six Viennese Waltzes. These masterpieces of nostalgic waltz writing on themes from his friend the singer Gärtner.

In 1940 he was invited to tour Australia and left France just before the Germans entered. At a concert in the summer of 1943, he felt coordination problems in an all-Chopin programme which included the 24 Preludes, some Etudes, Polonaises, Mazurkas and the B flat minor and B minor sonatas. The next day his left hand was completely numb, and Friedman, who had never before cancelled a performance, was never again heard on stage. His affliction was diagnosed as a form of rheumatism.

It had been a worldwide career, and in the early 1930s he travelled to Japan. In forty-one years he played 3000 concerts. Friedman died on January 26, 1948. He was one of the most wonderful pianists and an unforgettable soul, a man of great imagination and possessing one of the outstanding techniques in an age of great technicians.

#### Frederic Lamond

Frederic Lamond was born in Glasgow, Scotland on January 28, 1868. With his death on February 21, 1948, followed by that of the Portuguese pianist Jose Vianna Da Motta on June 1, 1948, the last of Liszt's pupils passed away. The final links to Liszt, the greatest inspirational force in the history of piano playing.

By twelve Lamond was organist at his church. His early studies included violin, piano and composition. He left Scotland in 1882 to study at the Raff Conservatory in Frankfurt. Later the boy worked with Hans von Bülow who helped shape his future. Bülow inspired him to the desire to study with Liszt. While still surrounding himself with young talents, the sixteen-year-old Lamond was introduced to the seventeen-year-old Lamond to the Abbé Liszt in Weimar.

He played in Europe, Russia, Mexico and the United States with unequalled success. In 1927 he became Director of the Curtis Institute in Philadelphia, a position he retained until 1938. This year before, he gave a concert at the Metropolitan Opera House celebrating its Fiftieth Anniversary. The first piano recording by the United States. The recording made from that concert has become one of the best known piano recordings ever made. Hofmann retired from concerts in 1946. He worked on an autobiography which unfortunately was left incomplete at his death in 1957.

As a pianist, Hofmann is one of the great figures of the instrument. Rachmaninov never tired of calling him the greatest living pianist, "not only technically but in every way."

#### Josef Hofmann

Josef Hofmann was born in Podgorze, Podoly in 1876. The most astonishing child prodigy in piano history. He was fortunate in having for a teacher his father Casimir who was an excellent pianist. He was then performing throughout Europe. In Berlin the child sold over fifty thousand of his Piano Concerto No. 1. In 1887 he gave his first New York concert.

Meanwhile he played forty completely sold-out cities before he was banned from the stage by the society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. An anonymous donor, later identified as the philanthropist Alfred Corning Clark, gave Casimir $50,000 to take the prodigy home. He studied with the promise that the boy could perfect at the piano until he was eighteen.

In Berlin Hofmann met at the piano with Moszkowski, who taught him for nearly two years. Later he studied with Anton Rubinstein, dazzled by the boy's gifts and told Casimir that his son's playing would do much harm.

#### Ferruccio Busoni

Ferruccio Busoni, born in the small Italian town of Empoli on April 1, 1866, was one of the towering figures of history and a very many-sided musician and thinker whose own musical activities were more widespread than many. His scale of influence in his own time was enormous. His name and advice influenced composers as dissimilar as Sibelius, Varèse, Webern, Grainger, Schoenberg, Hindemith and Kurt Weill. His papers are legion, both in composition and piano. Busoni was open to every musical impact. His book Sketch of a New Aesthetic of Music had a far-reaching influence.

"I should like to catch hold of a corner of the coming art of music and, where possible, see a team in it myself."

Busoni's pianism were vast; he taught a great deal, from Vienna masterclasses in Vienna. Vienna and Berlin, and Boston's New England Conservatory where he pioneered his superb dualisation of Bach's Chaconne in 1897. For a time he was the director of the Bologna Conservatory. It is little known that he also conducted, and early in the century he was producing such new music in Berlin as Debussy's Nocturnes, Stravinsky's Firebird Symphony and Delius' Paris, among others.

His tours as a pianist were endless; a restless seeker for perfection, he was relentlessly self-critical. Not for Busoni an after-recital recognition or finality. Feeling that the best time to practice was after the concert itself he would often tidy up details to be searched ever deeper into the great masterpieces of the piano literature. From the beginning of his career his playing was different from any other. His philosophical Faustian, he thought big, felt big and played bigger. The organ fascinated him - its immensity, its largesse, and though he was infused with the deepest feeling, he would crush any wayward impulse of expression.

His Chopin was frequently condemned for being too monumental, lacking charm. As in Paris recital after playing the Ballades, an old gentleman in the audience loudly proclaimed: "In the name of Chopin, I protest!" Others felt his Beethoven too loudly poetic and his rhythm too uncommunicative. He wrote, "I but for myself my ideal of Beethoven and my ideal of Beethoven is really no more than 'live'." One of his pragmatic dictums was: "Bach is the foundation of piano playing. Liszt is the summit, and the two embody perfection."

He loved Bach as a religion. His Bach transcriptions were so well-known that once, at a party, he was introduced as Mr. Bach-Busoni. At the age of eleven he had already produced an edition of Bach's Inventions, fascinating in its fingerings, tempo and details of interpretation. For the Well-Tempered Clavier he prepared the edition of the Well-Tempered Clavier. His Bach was a thinker, and he tried to bring Bach to terms with the sound of the modern piano. In his reverence for the piano he is a true spiritual son of Liszt. "Take it for granted," he said, "from the beginning that everything is possible on the piano, even what it seems impossible to you, or me, or so." "With the piano," he wrote, "a single person can command a complete whole." And it was "wholeness" that he sought to give to Busoni. Above all he was an idealist, believing "anyone who will master the language of art must have nurtured his life through the soul."

#### Ignacy Jan Paderewski

Ignacy Jan Paderewski (1860-1941) was the greatest box-office attraction of any instrumentalist of the late-nineteenth century and the first third of the twentieth century. A figure of supreme interest and, after Liszt, Anton Rubinstein, the most written-about pianist in the history of the instrument.

Delano not one of a long line of musicians such as colleagues as Friedheim, Carreño, Rosenthal, Sauer, Bauer, and others. Paderewski, however, had a magical glamour, an all-pervading glory and dignity - an "other-quality" which translates to any audience. In contrast to an appreciation of his often austere and beautiful music is seldom an immediate one. Much of it must be sought and pondered at. Many artists have loved their instrument, but none more than Paderewski, who laboured with a passion that defied normal fatigue. One jealous pianist observed, "He does nothing well but play the piano."

However, he did play brilliantly and poetically, and in his best years until around 1920, he was a virtuoso with an enormous sound flooding the spacious auditorium he was forced to play to accommodate the growing audiences. Paderewski's encore sessions alone could contain from ten to fifteen encores, often including a number of small lyrics of Schumann and Chopin, often auditors as breathless, lost in nostalgia, often with eyes wet. To compound his success his

Minuet in G attained great popularity. Arriving at his destination in a private railway car, the same brass band often welcomed him with a rendition of the innocuous Minuet, which he came to detest. It mattered little, and he never deprived his audience of the thrill of it, knowing well the sheet music was breaking all sales records. H. L. Mencken recalled as a boy when the Minuet was first available in Baltimore it caused "a sensation... People lined up for the music in the music store..."

From his first appearance in the United States in 1891 he was idolised, and it was the same everywhere he went, playing in Europe, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa. Paderewski's arrival was greeted with almost frenzied joy. Once in a very small town, he was driven to his hotel in a carriage. Asked why he didn't cancel, he bluntly answered: "I promised to give a recital. A promise is a promise. You can't get away this way again. I could not disappoint them."

#### The Reproducing Piano

The first Reproducing Piano went on sale in Germany in 1904. As its success grew the Aeolian Company - the largest piano maker in America - recognised in an opportunity to advance the piano as a result of this unique development, unveiled in 1913, was an advanced digital system for recording the playing of great pianists - called the Duo-Art. Between 1913-1930 the Reproducing Piano was very big business. In its peak year, 1923 more than 192,000 domestic instruments were manufactured by the Aeolian Company in the USA, with a total sales value in excess of $100,000. The Aeolian Company made every effort to perfect and enhance their invention, and throughout this period they kept the world's most famous pianists under contract, offering a balanced repertoire of smaller works, and most significantly for us of larger works which the 78 rpm disc could not manage.

There was never any problem about recording the activation of keys and pedals via electric contacts on or the recording piano. There was no recording expression, or 'touch', lies in the hammer speeds. Because the piano has an escapement action, once the key is struck, the hammer moves irresistibly to its destination. There were four methods in use to ensure dynamic accuracy:

- Electric contacts at two points of the travel of each hammer measured its speed
- A gramophone record was kept for reference
- The recording engineer, sitting beside the pianist, entered his own notation into a console. The notes in the perforated paper roll
- After two editions of the roll had been made by highly skilled craftworkers, the piano and the check all details before the third edition was published. Correspondence survives, which indicate the meticulous pains approached this task.

Reproducing Pianos were powered by an electric motor generating suction. This in turn applied to a tracker bar, pierced with one note for each piano key, which in turn operated tiny precise variations in air pressure to the tiny bellows operating each key. Playback, via a performance roll, automatically controlled the action of the keys, the synchronisation of the pedals, and the dynamic value of each note.

The domestic reproducing piano needed constant maintenance. So, when the axe effectively fell on the entire enterprise amid the American depression of the 1930s, instruments quickly lapsed into a caricature of their true capability. Accurate speed-control became impossible, malfunctioning pedals turned legato into staccato, and as many as peribarate mechanisms degenerated. 'Regal' installation became uneven, unreliable and the whole dynamic range for deterioration. The 'Robot' used for this new series of recordings is a free standing construction which is pushed up to the keyboard. It was completed in 1975, specifically to present undiluted reproduction on modern grand pianos. Its essential feature is a unique special construction of the late Gordon Jones, creator and chief theoretician of the Aeolian Company in England. Other working parts were made between 1925 and 1936 in England and America, but are now adapted to the new model. A simple but significant advantage of this robot is in its correct application of the side-shift 'una corda' pedal; even in the softest passages. It accurately recreated in the Duo-Art process

All the mechanisms substitute an inferior 'halibut' effect. This component of a very basic element of tonal control was made in the domestic piano as in all other respects the 'Robot' incorporates the ultimate Duo-Art technology in order to deliver historic performances exactly as heard and approved by recording artists in the Aeolian Hall in the first quarter of this century.
