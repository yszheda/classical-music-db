### Jotted Memories: the Forgotten Melodies of Nikolai Medtner

Born in Moscow in 1879 or 1880 (depending on the calendar adopted), Nikolai Karlovich Medtner (or Metner, which would be more correct) came from a family of German origin, although they had been living in Russia for at least two generations. The Medtners were great admirers of Goethe, and the young musician grew up in a cultural environment that was lively and stimulating, initially studying with his mother and later with his uncle Fyodor Godike, who taught at the Moscow Conservatoire. Given the boy's evident talent, at the age to twelve he was accepted at the Conservatoire, where he studied under Anatoliy Gelt, and in his turn had been a pupil of Nikolai Zverev, the pianist and teacher whose students had included Rachmaninov and Scriabin. Medtner also took lessons in theory with Kashkin and Arensky, who had previously taught Scriabin and Rachmaninov as well. Moreover, he continued his piano studies with Paul Pabst, who had been a pupil of Liszt; briefly with Sapeinikov, and lastly with Safonov, the renowned teacher whose earlier pupils included Josef Lhévinne. Unfortunately he was to fall ill with Bessie. Another influential teacher was Sergei Taneyev, from whom Medtner took private lessons. Taneyev had studied piano under Nikolai Rubinstein and composition under Tchaikovsky, and was considered one of the foremost teachers of the century. His erstwhile pupils included Scriabin and Rachmaninov as well as Ziloti, Gliere, Grechaninov and even Prokofiev when he was still very young.

This line-up of names provides us with an idea of Medtner's early musical universe. Arguably the person who played the most decisive role in his musical education was not the musician he spent most time with, but Taneyev, whose complex polyphonic constructs and far-reaching studies of the western musical repertoire were particularly influential. The contacts with this famous teacher probably helped Nikolai Karlovich to decide to follow up the gold medal he was awarded for his piano studies at the career as a composer-pianist, in the wake of the slightly older Sergei Rachmaninov, who had already made a considerable name for himself, and of course Anton Rubinstein. As a pianist Medtner managed to reconcile extensive concert tours in Russia and abroad with his teaching assignments, achieving significant fame during the last decade of his life. Like many other Russian composers after the Revolution, in 1921, largely in search of a situation that was financially more rewarding and without imagining that he would only once return to his native country, for a series of concerts in 1927. During the first he was based in Berlin and Paris, although he also performed in various tours of the United States. Then in 1935 he settled in England, where two fundamental encounters were to help shape future development: the first was with Edith a pupil of his to whom we owe much that we know about the composer today, and the second was with the Maharajah Jaya Chamraja Wadiyar, who in 1946 founded a Medtner Society whose aim was the financial support of the recording of his works, as well as a sensational interpretation of the Beethoven Appassionata. Although by then he suffered from poor health and was gradually retiring with his pupils' enrichment of the composer and his musical personality. Medtner died in 1951 at Golders Green, in London.

Taneyev declared that the young Medtner was «born armed with the classical sonata», and indeed he was always considered a champion of tradition, a role that he clearly enjoyed, to judge by his comments on «modernism» in the book *The Muse and the Fashion*. Given the long list of the Romantics was typical of the early 20th century, but the fact that Medtner felt the need to theorise his own creative vein tells us a great deal about him as a person. His music is emotionally learned, with fugal studies and counterpoint that can ramify to the point of abstraction. Yet unlike Taneyev in his somewhat Flemish compositional solemnity, Medtner never abandoned a certain lyricism, which may not have embraced the instinctive spontaneity of Rachmaninov or the ecstatic refinement of Scriabin, but was nevertheless heartfelt and constant, often with literary hints of ancient times, including errant knights, paladins, elfin creatures and country dances. In his oeuvre as a whole, his great theme is the co-existence of lyricism and intellectualism: on the one hand there are the fourteen piano sonatas, the three concertos, the two great sets of songs, the sonatas for violin and the imposing quintet for piano and strings, and on the other, the arabesques, the sketches, the dances and over thirty *Skazki* or fables, as well as other works for the most part of reduced form of exalted lyricism. The *Forgotten Melodies* that are the subject of this recording are in a middle ground between the two categories.

Let's start with the question: why «Forgotten Melodies'? Medtner was in the habit of jotting themes, motifs and ideas down in notebooks, calling these fragments *soggetti*, the Italian term normally associated with the fugue. The opus numbers 38, 39 and 40 collect these melodic subjects that he jotted down over the years and then forgotten until they returned to mind, ultimately taking on a shape of their own in the form of a series of pieces. Taneyev described the *Forgotten Melodies* as a «middle ground» between the classical framework and the Romantic sketch, and in these pieces Medtner effectively manages to reconcile the two principles. The thematic material in the *Melodies* is extremely cohesive, not only on account of the tonal relationships, but also because the compositions largely derive from the same *soggetto*, or theme.

The foremost example of this balancing act is Op.38, which originally had the subtitle 'Nature'. The opus opens with the placid development of a theme, *Sonata Reminiscenza*, provoking reflections on the unusual choice of entrusting reminiscence with the job of presenting the material that was to prevail throughout the work. There is a flowing quality to *Sonata Reminiscenza*, with movements similar to court dances that evolve and gain in hazy timbres and then suddenly launch into frantic outbursts before subsiding into the languor of seductive melodies. It goes without saying that the handling of polyphony is absolutely flawless. Next comes the *Danza graziosa*, whose changes are so subtle that it resembles the *leggiadro* passage that alternates with an engagingly worldly Allegretto section. Although the following *Danza festiva* was originally intended as the finale of Schubert's Étude-Tableau Op.33 No.7, the dance form also bears witness to Medtner's constant desire to develop and expand his chosen material. For the next piece, the *Canzona fluviale*, he adopts the same standards used for the opening of the *Danza festiva*, generating a sort of barcarola in which some of the *Sonata Reminiscenza* motifs return with greater clarity, appearing and disappearing like objects submerged in the water. Delight and beauty of folk dances, especially the habanera, the *Danza rustica* is essentially polite and refined, creating a sense of contrast that makes it one of the most buoyant and fascinating pieces in the whole collection. The *Canzona mattinata* begins with an almost literal citation of the opening section of the *Sonata Reminiscenza*, which is then developed into an intensely poignant melody. Very different in character is the following *Danza silvestra*, which seems to belong to the sphere inhabited by Debussy's minstrels and Liszt's gnomes. The most virtuosic piece in the collection, it evokes some of the main themes of the *Reminiscenza* in the passionate outbursts that give form to the musical underworld portrayed. The last piece in the collection is *Alla reminiscenza*, which follows the opening attacca as a rewriting of the main theme, in the major key, and is entrusted with the narration, gradually bringing the story told to a serene conclusion.

There are two alternative versions of the *Canzona serenata* and *Danza silvestra*. In the case of the former, this is a mere transcription a semitone higher, which is how Medtner played it on his first Moscow performance of a selection of pieces from the collection. By contrast, the earlier version of the *Danza silvestra* contains various differences, including an alternative coda, which was ultimately replaced in one of Medtner's many later reworkings of the pieces.

Although the *Forgotten Melodies* Op.39 are conceptually less sophisticated, they nevertheless comprise some of the most inspired moments in Medtner's entire collection. *Meditazione* in particular, in which there is an Introduction (quasi Cadenza) involving roving keyboard reflections on timbre and colour that ultimately lead to a *Mestamente* piece, the *Canzona fluviale*. The third great moment in Op.39 is the *Romanza*, begins in a pensive mood with the returning theme from the *Meditazione*, which is then transfigured in the *Espressivo* cantabile section, whereas the following *Primavera* seems to develop almost entirely within the framework of the introspective and melodious handling of the meditative theme. The flowering is hard, cold, almost icy, only to burst forth in the third piece, *Primavera*. Here the meditative theme reappears in a completely transformed state, with expanded intervals in a luminous B flat major, while the development section opens with the same motif that suggests the vernal sunshine of Schumann and early 20th century Russian music. There are also hints of Schumann in the central part, marked *Con moto*, which gives way to some extraordinarily complex polyphonic handling of the notes and elegant harmonies. The spring-like opening of the section is followed by a varied refrain - Medtner always modified in some way the passages he chose to reiterate. Next comes the *Canzona mattinata* which abandons the meditative theme and clearly has the completion of the cycle in view. It nevertheless maintains a three-note element as a generating

motif, achieving a relatively simple melodious development that is actually based on complex, layered polyphony. The outcome is one of the loveliest themes Medtner ever wrote. Although the modal hints of the *Canzona* may seem to hark back to more elaborate earlier movements, the finale also embodies a novel form of simplicity: like opening a window to let Spring (Primavera) flood into a dusty room full of books and thoughts. This opus should accordingly be enjoyed, yet at the same time be the subject of deliberate intent that is an intrinsic part of this second cycle, especially bearing in mind the last movement. The *Sonata tragica* is arguably Medtner's most famous composition of all. It is impressive, not only because it is dramatic from the outset, as it banishes the serenity so arduously achieved in the previous two sections. Here again the three-motive motif is used, but this time it is transformed into a funereal, surge theme, with imposing modulations in the left hand. Due space is at last devoted to the return of the central theme of the *Canzona mattinata* in a masterly balancing act between the light and dark strains which bring to mind the contrast between luxuries and poverty. This then leads to a development that combines the tragic music of the opening section, the tenacious rest becomes a kind of heroic theme. The work hurls itself into a grand refrain, with a heady coda that is ultimately truncated by the absolute despair of the tragic parenthesis.

Though it is unusual that the three *Forgotten Melodies* Op.40 should contain no sonata as such, the subdivision in six dances does effectively make it a suite, albeit a la Medtner. In this case he casts aside the thematic derivations and relations of the two earlier works to create an organic whole. This includes some of the most folk-oriented passages of his entire oeuvre. It all comes together in the conclusion with its two-chord finale, reminiscent of the opening of the Second Sonata by Rachmaninov. The first *Danza* col canto begins with an Allegretto poco capriccioso, a bouncing, almost martial section that gives rise to an intriguing Andantino cantando, combining serenity with touches of anxiety in a manner typical of Medtner. For the following dance the composer returns to his idiosyncratic Italian terminology: *mestamente*. It begins with a placid introduction, which is followed by developments that characterise the piece as a whole. Although Medtner did not write many orchestral works, the strict polyphony, ample chords, the octaves and the odd hint of the *Danse macabre* by Saint-Saëns give an unquestionably symphonic idea of the work. The *Danza fiorata* adopts for a symphonic suite. Here the final segment seems to dissolve in a flowing arpeggio, disappearing into the serene marcando of the final bars. Next comes a sensuous dance, the elegant *Danza fiorata*, a charmingly concise and effective piece with occasional echoes of medieval music, naturally within the overall framework of Romanticism. This is followed by the *Danza jubilosa* that strikes with the power of the ancient Russian church tradition, almost like a composer - never an easy undertaking for Russian composers. Once again Medtner would seem to have been inspired by the German school, especially Schumann, yet the pages are truly Russian, with a vein of melancholy, shares certain features with the *Canzona fluviale* from Op.38, above all in the flowing left-hand accompaniment, yet without succumbing to stereotypes such as the barcarola. Indeed, there is often a hint of agitation, as in the tenacious Allegro that concludes the *Danza*. Op.40 ends with a *Danza Ditirambica*, which refers to the ancient Greek dithyramb but also evokes Medtner's three *Ditirambi* Op.10. The mood remains celebrative, but in a lighter, simpler manner. Here the Hellenic model is musically more ideal than substantial, and refers to some extent to the Greek structure of the work, and sensation of verse form, where Medtner displays his skill in the art of variation. At this point the final parenthesis becomes part of the coda, and the work ends with a melodically ironic echo of Beethoven's *Fifth Symphony*.

## Page 10-11: About the Artist

### MATTIA OMETTO

A pupil of Aldo Ciccolini in Paris and Earl Wild in Palm Springs, Mattia Ometto graduated with full honors under Anna Barutti at the "B. Marcello" Conservatory in Venice. Since a young age he has performed in the concert seasons of the main Italian cities, including Venice (Teatro la Fenice), Milan (Società dei Concerti), Rome, Naples, Turin, Trieste, Udine, Bologna, Treviso, Padua (Amici della Musica), Vicenza (Società del Quartetto). In 2008 he made his debut at the Carnegie Hall in New York and at the Théâtre du Rond Point des Champs Elysées in Paris. Since then he has performed regularly in Europe and the United States (New York, Boston, Des Moines, Redlands, Berlin...) and has been featured a soloist with the Lyric Symphony Orchestra of Los Angeles, the Academic Baskent Orchestra of Ankara, the Vidin State Philharmonic Orchestra (Bulgaria) and the Orchestra di Padova e del Veneto to name a few.

Mattia Ometto has recorded extensively for Brilliant Classics, including Britten's and Poulenc's works for two pianos and orchestra with Leonora Armellini, Franz Liszt's music for two pianos with Leslie Howard and the complete *Melodies* by César Franck and Henri Duparc. Further recordings include Brahms' complete works for two pianos with Leonora Armellini (Da Vinci) and the complete music for piano duo by Reynaldo Hahn with Leslie Howard (Melba Recordings).

Mattia Ometto is a Professor at "B. Marcello" Conservatory in Venice (Italy).
