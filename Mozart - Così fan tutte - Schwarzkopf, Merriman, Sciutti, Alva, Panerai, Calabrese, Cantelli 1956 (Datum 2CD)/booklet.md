In the long standing "ltalian" Mozart versus "German" Mozart dispute, choosing sides is not easy. The most recent generation of orchestral conductors has, in its' entirety, followed the German fashion with the result that, little by little, Mozart's "italianism" - an italianism that is due not only to its' dominance as the operatic language, but also to the very structure of the arias themselves - has disappeared into limbo. Even Riccardo Muti, who is to a certain extent the only one who has attempted to reinject into Mozart a litle Mediterranean warmth and passion, has limited himself to the orchestral side; the recitatives, so crucial from the theatrical point of view, have been performed in a manner that, given the spirit and the intent in which and with which they were composed, is a contradiction in terms. The choice of singers, who, in the main, have been either German or Anglo saxon, has always broughi forth a chorus of doubts. In other words, Muti's is a somewhat half hearted revolution and the Italian public's disaffection with the Salzburg genius can probably be traced to the principal interpreters' incomprehension and less than perfect delivery and accent. Contrary to the assertions made by certain uninformed persons, Mozart's operas are not the voca transposition of the German instrumental experience, but accord perfectly with the Italian, or italianate, melodramatic tradition and they represent, from a certain point of view, the artistic high point of the entire 1 8th century. Moreover, a perusal of Mozart's correspondence will confirm to what extent he considered the human voice the purest and most perfect o instruments, and the passion aroused in him by the operatic genre in vogue at the time i.e. Italian. This recording of Cosi fan tutte takes us back to the era in which Mozart was surrounded by singers familiar with the Italian language; not only artists who were themselves Italian, but true international professionals who possessed the ability to render the Italian idiom in all its' many facets and subtleties. The praise must also go to conductors like Guido Cantelli - who died so tragically in an air crash at Orly on the 24th November of the same year, 1956, as this recording - who had the ability to urge and impel the singers into a search for the expressivity that is, in the end, the only way of creating true characters instead of stereotypes. Cantelli wastes no time and immediately whisks us off to Naples, where the action takes place, thanks to the deeply emotive content of the overture, a content that we may look for in vain in other celebrated interpretations. In particular, listen carefully to the brief oboe solo that follows the opening chords; it contains a dramatic expressiveness and a lyricism that brings to mind the musical romanticism that came after Mozart. caveat applies to the ensuing Presto that is not a mere display of directorial bravura but in fact dovetails perfectly with Cantelli's overall interpretative design. Even Don Alfonso's theme possesses an ironic beau monde eloquence that presages the action's future development. Some of the most memorable moments; the opera's opening trio, the quintet "Sento, o Dio, che questo piede", Fiordiligi's aria "Come scoglio", - which is truly sublime - , Ferrando's aria "Un'aura amorosa" and the opera's magnificent finale. Memorable amongst the performers - and how could it be otherwise? - is Elisabeth Schwarzkopf's Fiordiligi. The whole of the recitative running up to "Come scoglio", for example, is performed in the spirit of a "grande tragedienne" and her handling of the aria is both expansive and solemn as befits that which is, after all, a parody - and as such, should be interpreted extremely seriously - of the passionate, inflamed arias fashionable in the first half of the 18th century. Admittedly, at times the calibration of the low register is not perfect and the vocalisation is aspirated in a decidedly teutonic manner. But this goes unnoticed when listening to the imperious delivery, the thinly veiled sensuality and the magnificent accentuation. The B flat on the word "tempest" is a thunderbolt. The result is an example of the highest value vivified further by a direction that is at one and the same time sparkling and languorous. Also meritorious of inclusion in our anthology of operatic gems is the duet "Prendero quel brunettino" in which Schwarzkopf is both languishing and fatuous and which gives me the opportunity to mention the mezzosoprano Nan Merriman in the role of Dorabella. It is depressing to observe just how often voices of importance are forgotten, as is the case with this artist, born in Pittsburgh on the 28th April 1920. And yet Merriman was, in her day, a highly reputed singer, a favourite of Toscanini and perfectly at home in the world's most famous opera houses. A straightforward lady, free from the usual airs and graces of a diva that distinguished so many of her colleagues, she retired at only forty five years of age at the height of her powers. Her rendering of Dorabella - a role she interpreted at the Festival of Aix en-Provence from 1953 to 1959 - is provocative and flirtatious and in the aria "Smanie implacabili" presents a near perfect example of vocal bravura and expressiveness. If, on the other hand, you would like an idea of the marvellous art of Graziella Sciutti, then you should listen to all Despina's recitatives. Obviously, the arias and the ensembles are superlative, as one would logically expect from a Mozartian of her calibre and fame. But those finely crafted recitatives, that carefully measured accentuation, at times viperish and almost frenzied, form together something absolutely extraordinary, like getting -7
a glimpse of the dawn of melodrama - the recitar cantando of Monteverdi - such is its naturalness and truth. However, it is perhaps the male performers that render this Cosi fan tutte so utterly convincing; in the performance of Mozart several other female singers stand out and it is worth remembering - and I cite these names at random - Teresa Berganza, Margaret Price, Lella Cuberli or Mirella Freni. In contrast, on the men's side, it seems as if progress ground to a halt at the end of Fritz Wunderlich's or Erich Kunz's era. It is always difficult to generalise, but it is evident that, apart from the obvious exceptions, Alfredo Kraus for example, the various tenors, baritones and basses have not come up to the standard of their female partners. Here, on the other hand, we have Luis Alva at the start of his career who, although obliged to forego the aria "Ah, lo veggio quell'anima bella" and the cavatina "Tradito, schernito", demonstrates a phrasing that is extremely elegant and aristocratic and who in "Un'aura amorosa" - listen to the hushed repeat with no halts for breathing - presents a complete picture of what the Mozartian tenor should be. I do not think, moreover, that I have ever heard the baritone Rolando Panerai sing as well as he does here, with a voice of excellent timbre, measured delivery, and, as was his nature, broad and mellow sound. His swashbuckling and impetuous Guglielmo should be taken as a model for any singer about to tackle this role. Listen especially to the voluptuous and provocative "Non siate ritrosi" in which, aided by Cantelli who adopts a tempo markedly slower than usual, the Italian baritone appears both tender and mischievous at the same time. And finally we come to the Don Aifonso of Franco Calabrese, a respected if not first rate singer, notable for his piquant accentuation, his commendable refusal to hurl out the top notes, his fine legato and his portrayal of a character disillusioned and rendered crafty by experience. If you then proceed to compare this with all the successive Don Alfonsos on disc you will obtain the exact measure of the inexorable decline which, alas, the modern vocal art is undergoing.
