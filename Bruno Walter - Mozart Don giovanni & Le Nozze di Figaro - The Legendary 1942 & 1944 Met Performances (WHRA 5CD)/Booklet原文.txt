# BRUNO WALTER CONDUCTS MOZART AT THE MET

Bruno Walter, one of the twentieth century's great conductors, was born in Berlin in 1876. He had a distinguished career throughout Europe and the United States lasting more than sixty years. Although his extensive discography ranged from the acoustic era, through electrical recordings, to stereo long playing records in the 1950s, Walter's operatic legacy is limited to the justly famous Act 1 and segments from Act 2 of *Die Walküre*. These 78 rpm recordings were made in Vienna in 1935, and the first act, recorded complete with the finest exponents of the roles, Lauritz Melchior, Lotte Lehmann, and Emanuel List, has long been regarded as perhaps the best of all versions. Considering its artistic excellence and critical acclaim, it is difficult to explain why Walter never recorded opera in a studio again.

For this reason we must be very grateful for the survival of a number of Metropolitan Opera broadcasts that preserve his interpretations. *Die Zauberflöte, Fidelio, La forza del destino*, and *Un ballo in maschera* were broadcast more than once with Walter at the helm, however, the two operas that constitute the present release were broadcast only once each with Walter conducting. Walter also conducted *The Bartered Bride and Orfeo ed Euridice* at the Met but sadly these were not broadcast. Recorded fragments exist of Walter conducting both works in Salzburg in 1937, and there are also complete Salzburg performances of *Don Giovanni* and *Nozze*, but the surviving Met versions are superior in terms of sound quality and the uniform excellence of the casts.

Walter's career evolved from the provincial German houses to the prestigious Berlin Opera before 1900. He then worked at the Vienna Hofoper with Gustav Mahler before returning to Germany to conduct in Munich, Berlin, and later Leipzig.

As a Jew, Walter suffered increased discrimination from the rise of the Nazis during the 1930s and was eventually hounded out of Germany. He became director of the Vienna State Opera in Austria between 1936 and 1938. When the "Anschluss" annexed Austria to Germany in March 1938, Walter was conducting in Paris. The French government granted him citizenship, but in 1939 Walter sailed to the United States where he would eventually take up permanent residence. Europe's loss was America's gain; after joining Metropolitan in early 1941, he conducted more than a hundred performances between 1941 and 1959.

*Don Giovanni* was one of three operas Walter conducted during his first Met season, the others being *The Bartered Bride* and *Fidelio*. The New York critics, familiar with Walter on the concert platform, were overwhelmed by his ability as an opera conductor. Howard Taubman thought 'the inspiring leadership of Bruno Walter was a source of endless delight' while Olin Downes described Walter's first *Don Giovanni* performance as 'the finest interpretation of Mozart that the writer has witnessed in this lyric theatre.' *Le nozze di Figaro* did not feature in Walter's first two seasons at the Met, but he conducted the opera nine times between December 1942 and April 1944. The 1944 broadcast presented here earned Downes' admiration: 'Bruno Walter conducted with inspiration, with ... love for every note of the score, and also reverence.' Contemporaries clearly knew they had an exceptional musician leading these two concerts and writers and critics of our time concur. Paul Jackson in his monumental three-volume overview of all Metropolitan Opera broadcasts praises the merits of this 1942 *Don Giovanni*: 'a cast of exceptional musicality and nearly uniform strength aids Walter in his Beethovenesque reading of Mozart's bequest to nineteenth-century romanticism.' Discussing the 1944 *Le nozze di Figaro* broadcast, Jackson simply notes 'in Walter's hands Mozart's Figaro at last receives his due.' [*New York Times* 6 December 1941, 8 March 1941, and 30 January 1944. Paul Jackson, *Saturday Afternoons at the Old Met: The Metropolitan Opera Broadcasts, 1931-1950* (Portland, OR.: Amadeus Press, 1992), 302, 311]

Walter had a well-known affinity with Mozart's music. He seems to have been able to adopt just the right tempi at key moments, and his lightness of touch and swift reading of the score never allows the story to flag. Walter himself wrote that while working in Munich before World War I, 'I finally discovered behind a seemingly graceful playfulness the dramatist's inexorable seriousness and wealth of characterization. I recognised in Mozart the Shakespeare of opera. At the same time, I understood the unique creative miracle vouchsafed us Mozart's work. With him, everything was dramatically true: nobility as well as baseness, kindness as well as malice, wisdom as well as stupidity, and he turned all truth into beauty. My task in Mozart performances had become clear to me: every characteristic and truthful detail must be given vigorous dramatic expression without impairment of the vocal and orchestral beauty. This beauty permitted no exaggeration in dynamics and tempos, in gesturing and action, in forms and colors.' [Bruno Walter, *Theme and Variations: An Autobiography* (New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 1946), 217.] These broadcasts allow us to hear Walter's declared aims in action.

*(Bruno Walter, 1943)*

Italian bass Ezio Pinza (1892-1957) singing Don Giovanni and Figaro is one of three principals common to both casts. Pinza made his debut in Cremona in 1914, and after military service in the first World War, he resumed his career at La Scala under Arturo Toscanini before joining the Metropolitan Opera in 1926. He made no complete opera recordings in the studio, thus we are especially fortunate that many Met broadcasts between 1931 and 1947 preserve his artistry so richly. His surviving recordings include *Don Giovanni, Le nozze di Figaro, Boris Godunov, Faust, Carmen, Les contes d'Hoffmann, Pelléas et Mélisande, Mignon, Lucia di Lammermoor, Rigoletto, Norma, Louise, Lakmé, L'amore dei tre Re, Die Zauberflöte, La bohème, Il barbiere di Siviglia, Samson et Dalila, Aïda, La forza del destino, and Simon Boccanegra*. Following his retirement from the Met in 1948, Pinza famously created the role of Emile de Becque in *South Pacific* on Broadway. During the early 1950s he starred in his own television series, *The Ezio Pinza Show* and the short-lived comedy series *Bonino*. He also appeared in several Hollywood films. The only two with musical interest are *Carnegie Hall* (1947) where he was joined by a large number of celebrated artists, among them Bruno Walter, and *Tonight We Sing* (1953) in which he plays the legendary Russian bass Feodor Chaliapin.

Pinza's relationship with Walter dates back to 1934. Looking for the ideal Don Giovanni for the Salzburg Festival, Walter recalled that Pinza had recently sung the role in New York and that he was noted for his 'effect on the female sex.' After casting him, Walter helped Pinza develop his characterization of the Don beyond the common womaniser, making him a 'magnetic rogue ... doomed by his quest for the perfect woman.' The Austrian critics thought his performances were 'fascinating ... full of a passionate, effervescent love of life'. Pinza returned to the Salzburg Festival for the next few years and during 1935 began a romantic relationship with Walter's daughter Gretel. Gretel was separated from her husband and the relationship seems to have had Walter's blessing. In August 1939, while Pinza was singing in Salzburg and Walter was conducting in Luceme, Gretel was shot and killed in Zűrich by her estranged husband who then turned the gun on himself. Both Walter and Pinza were devastated by her death, but as one biographer notes, 'Walter never seems to have held Pinza responsible for his daughter's death. Rather it appears to have been a shared tragedy that kept them on close terms until Pinza's own death in 1957.' [Erik Ryding and Rebecca Pechefsky, *Bruno Walter: A World Elsewhere* (New Haven and London: Yale University Press, 2001), 238-9, 277].

Pinza was an ideal Don Giovanni, having both a remarkably beautiful voice and a striking stage presence. It had not always been the case: reviewing a 1929 performance Lawrence Gilman grumbled that Pinza 'lacks the elegance, the grace, the adroitness, the magnetic charm that the successful Don possesses, and his voice is not sufficiently flexible for the music.' Thirteen years later, reviewing this particular performance (coincidentally the 200th Metropolitan broadcast), Olin Downes thought 'Mr. Pinza in exceptional form. No wonder the applause crashed out after his champagne aria. But in scene after scene this role seems to have grown with significance, grown in significance and versatility of dramatic effect, supplementing the qualities of the superb voice.' A performance a few months previously, marking the 150th anniversary of Mozart's death, earned Pinza this plaudit from critic Howard Taubman: 'Ezio Pinza has improved his Don Giovanni steadily, so that it has become possibly his best role. His singing is exemplary - suave, incisive and gutsy. In addition, he plays the part with the assurance of a man who has mastered its every facet. With such a Don Giovanni the production achieves focus and a vitalizing drive.' [*New York Times* 29 November 1929, 8 March 1942 and 6 December 1941]

Although Pinza had sung Figaro for Walter in Salzburg during the 1930s, he did not appear at the Met in the role until the new production of 1940, the first time *Le nozze di Figaro* had been staged at the Met since 1918. It is clear that Pinza particularly enjoyed playing Figaro, delighting in, but not overdoing, the comic potential of the role. Irving Kolodin, reviewing a 1940 performance, observed that 'Ezio Pinza as Figaro dispensed with some of the more obvious comedy of his part, especially in his fourth act monologue addressed to the audience. This, incidentally, was a bit of virtuoso singing that properly climaxed an evening of exceptional effort by Mr. Pinza. The vocal richness and verve of his Figaro will be a durable memory.'

When Bruno Walter first conducted the opera at the Met in December 1942, the critics singled out certain members of the cast as 'outstanding. Nowhere was it more than the Figaro of Ezio Pinza, who brings a bouncing vitality to the role and who sings it with vocal splendour.' Reviewing the 1944 broadcast Olin Downes commented: 'Mr. Pinza's magnificent voice was accompanied with the humor, not exaggerated, and with fresh business of his Figaro.' [*New York Sun*, February 27, 1940; *New York Times* 17 December 1942 and 30 January 1944]

*(Pinza as Figaro)*

Bidú Sayão (1902-1999), the Zerlina and Susanna in these broadcasts, was the leading lyric coloratura soprano at the Met between 1937 and 1952. Born in Rio di Janeiro, Sayão trained in South America and Europe, and made her debut at the age of eighteen. After performing in Paris and Milan, Sayão made her American debut on 30 December 1935 in a Town Hall Recital in New York. Her performance of *La Demoiselle Élue* by Debussy with the New York Philharmonic Symphony under the baton of Arturo Toscanini in April 1936 raised her profile in musical circles considerably, and within a year she made her Met debut as Manon. For the next fifteen years Sayão dominated the roles of Violetta, Manon, Mimi, Juliette, Gilda, Norina, and Rosina at the Met and broadcast recordings have fortunately captured her in each of these parts. Some recordings, the 1947 broadcast of *Romeo et Juliete* opposite Jussi Björling in particular, have justifiably achieved legendary status.

Sayão was the Susanna opposite Pinza in the new production of *Le nozze di Figaro* in February 1940, and by the time of this performance Olin Downes was able to comment that her Susanna was 'finished and charming in detail and most eloquently sung.' Zerlina was a recent addition to Sayão's repertoire in 1941, but it should be counted as another of her signature roles. Downes almost ran out of superlatives: 'Bidú Sayão's Zerlina was a complete delight. That artist made the arias, as well as the recitatives, a miracle of seductiveness, for she sang, the recitatives with radiant charm, but acted with much of the vivacity which had made her work in "The Marriage of Figaro" so brilliant to see and hear.' [*New York Times* 30 January 1944 and 8 March 1942]. The recorded evidence substantiates this judgment.

If Sayão's virtuosity is something we might expect from someone so well known, the excellence of Jarmila Novotná, the Donna Elvira and Cherubino on these recordings, comes as more of a revelation. Czech-born Novotná (1907-1994) was even younger than Sayão when she made her debut in Prague in *The Bartered Bride*, aged seventeen. By the mid-1930s she was singing at the Vienna State Opera with Bruno Walter and Alexander Kipnis, and with Pinza at the Salzburg Festival. A testimony of her versatility is that in 1934 she was chosen by Lehár to create the title role in his *Giuditta* opposite Richard Tauber. Novotná first appeared at the Met in January 1940, and sang more than two hundred performances between 1940 and 1956. Her wide-ranging roles included among others Manon, Violetta, Mimi, Pamina, Melisande, Euridice, Marenka, and three highly successful 'trouser roles': Octavian, Orlofsky, and Cherubino. She also had an extensive cinematic career in Europe, appearing in eleven films before 1938, among them in the role of Marie (Marenka) in a German film version of *The Bartered Bride* (1934). Her brief American film career included a small part in *The Great Caruso* (1951).

Olin Downes thought her Cherubino 'a delightful and admirably composed interpretation' and described her first performance as a 'triumph.' Irving Kolodin concurred: 'A thoroughly credible figure in appearance and a graceful one in movement ... her "Voi che sapete" was sung with cultivated taste and a superior sense of line. She also handled the comic complexities of the part with good mobility and resourcefulness.' [*New York Times* 27 February 1940 and *New York Sun* 27 February 1940]

Her first appearance in the role of Elvira in *Don Giovanni* required a completely different approach and it is a testament to her abilities that she does this so successfully. On her first appearance in 1941 critics thought she 'was an ideal figure ... nobility in the person, yet weak to the beloved seducer ... she was the most distinguished guished artist on stage.' Reviewing this particular broadcast, Olin Downes thought Novotná's Elvira 'is beautifully done and the execution of the music with high intelligence and the skill in the employment of not unlimited resources.' [*New York Times* 8 March 1941, 8 March 1942].

*(Sayão and Pinza in Nozze)*

*(Novotna as Cherubino)*

In a somewhat unusual casting, Leporello was assigned to the renowned Ukrainian-born bass Alexander Kipnis (1891-1978). Kipnis trained in Warsaw and sang in various German cities during World War I before joining the Berlin Staatsoper in 1922. He performed at Bayreuth between 1927 and 1933 and his discography includes an abridged recording as Gurnemanz in *Parsifal*, made in 1927 under the baton of Siegfried Wagner. Kipnis sang regularly at the Chicago Civic Opera between 1923 and 1932, and became a US citizen in 1931. Like Bruno Walter, Kipnis was also *non grata* in Germany because of his Jewish origins. In fact, Walter Legge, preparing the first complete *Die Zauberflöte* recording in 1937, was unable to risk taking him to Nazi Germany for the sessions, thus in a compromise Sarastro fell into the lap of Wilhelm Furtwängler, an able singer yet still a substitute for the best. Kipnis joined Bruno Walter at the Vienna State Opera between 1936 and 1938, but he fled Austria after the Anschluss. He was nearly 50 years old when he made his Met debut in May 1940 as Gurnemanz in *Parsifal*. He sang more than a hundred performances there until his retirement in 1946, including Hermann, Hunding, Hagen, King Marke, Sarastro, Baron Ochs, Rocco, Arkel. Like Chaliapin before him, Kipnis also sang the title role in Boris Godunov in Russian while the rest of the cast sang in Italian. He can be heard in fifteen Met broadcasts, six of those Wagnerian roles, and as is the case with Pinza, the surviving recordings provide us with valuable evidence of his artistry. This broadcast of *Don Giovanni* is the sole recorded example of Kipnis in an Italian language opera. While it takes a bit of getting used to hearing his mostly correct yet heavily accented Italian, his performance was a challenge and a triumph. Olin Downes commented: 'It was a pleasure to perceive in Mr. Kipnis a singer who is an artist to his fingertips, who knows well the tradition of his role - and who knows so well the difference between humor and farce, to say nothing of his voice and his distinguished vocal style of the occasion.' Despite the opposition of the occasion, Kipnis sang Leporello only twice at the Met, in this broadcast and then in a second performance in February 1943. Kipnis recalled much later that his Leporello ruined his friendship with Pinza: "It is not easy for me to talk about Pinza because if you happened to sing the same clef that he did, and if you happened to sing well, you could not count on being a friend of his. We started out to be friends, even though we really didn't have much in common off stage. But it ended when we were cast together in *Don Giovanni* - I sang Leporello to his Don. As the score calls for, I did an impersonation of his Don. Now, Pinza had a rapid vibrato in his voice, and overall his voice was lighter in color than mine was. So I accented these differences in my impersonation of him, making my voice lighter and higher and adding a tremolo to it. He hated the laughter I got. It was his turn to impersonate my Leporello, he couldn't make his voice dark enough or his vibrato less rapid, so the audience didn't react so favourably. Therefore, he was never my friend, by his choice." [*New York Times* 8 March 1942. James Drake, "Kipnis Speaks: An interview with Alexander Kipnis" *Opera Quarterly*, (1991), 80-81]. One can hear in this broadcast exactly what he means. His impersonation of Pinza in Act 2 is spot on and the audience is audibly amused. The Met management may have decided not to pair them more often to avoid any artistic rivalry, however, another, more persuasive explanation for Kipnis singing Leporello only twice could be the presence of the great basso buffo Baccaloni, especially famous for his Leporello ever since the magnificent 1936 Glyndebourne recording. He was the Met's choice for the role in nearly sixty performances.

*(Alexander Kipnis, 1944)*

Alongside these international stars were a number of home-grown American singers who certainly lived up to the standards set by their colleagues. Soprano Rose Bampton (1907-2007) had started her career as a mezzo-soprano in regional opera houses in the USA before joining the Met in 1932. A move to soprano roles in 1937 was a notable success. Bampton sang Aida and Alceste as well as Donna Anna. In the 1940s she was heard in the lighter Wagnerian roles of Elsa, Elisabeth, Sieglinde, and in the more dramatic role of Kundry. She also had an active career in Europe and Latin America during the 1930s and 1940s and recordings exist of her singing Richard Strauss in Buenos Aires: the Marschallin in *Der Rosenkavalier* and the title role in the title role in Bampton played Daphne, roles she never sang at the Met. Bampton sang Donna Anna fourteen times at the Met between 1938 and 1948 but this was her only broadcast in the role. She brings the necessary weight to the role, and was compared favourably with Zinka Milanov, with whom she alternated in the part. Olin Downes described her delivery 'the truly passionate Donna Anna of Rose Bampton, sung with the most moving sincerity, and often, despite the fact that makes dangerous demands upon her voice, with a quality of dramatic expression in song more intense and more convincing than any other recent treatment of the part.' [*New York Times* 8 March 1942] One can only assume that the implication of this last statement must have irked Mme. Milanov to no end.

American-born Charles Kullman (1903-1983), cast here as Don Ottavio, first made his name in Europe, principally in Berlin, Salzburg and Vienna. In 1936, Kullman took part in the first live recording of Mahler's *Das Lied von der Erde* made by Columbia Records in Vienna, Bruno Walter conducting. Kullman made his Met debut in December 1935, and sang more than four hundred performances at the Met until his retirement in 1960. A versatile singer, he was able to sing lyric parts such as Rodolfo, the Duke of Mantua, Cavaradossi, and Pinkerton as well as far heavier roles such as *Lohengrin, Don José, Walter von Stolzing, and Parsifal*. More than forty Met broadcasts capture him in *Carmen, La traviata, Manon, Rigoletto, Madama Butterfly, Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg*, among others, as well as three Mozart operas, *Die Zauberflöte, Die Entführung aus dem Serail*, and *Don Giovanni*. Don Ottavio is a tricky part for a tenor, being neither heavy (as most tenors are) nor cad (such as Pinkerton or the Duke of Mantua) and often regarded as a weak character submissive to Donna Anna. Vocally the role is demanding, with two difficult arias in addition to duets, trios and ensemble pieces. Kullman does very well with the part, helped by a stronger voice than many other Don Ottavios, and earning plaudits for his 'ten-der manner and musical grace' and singing 'like an artist.' [Jackson, *Saturday Afternoons*, 303; *New York Times* 6 December 1941]

Baritone Mack Harrell (1909-1960), the Masetto, won the Metropolitan Opera Auditions of the Air in 1938, and performed at the Met between 1939 and 1958. He brings a fresh-voiced enthusiasm to the part, though some critics grumbled, quite erroneously in light of Lorenzo da Ponte and Mozart, that his acting was a bit too 'boyish.'

Bass Norman Cordon (1904-1964) gives us a powerful portrayal of the Commendatore. Cordon sang more than five hundred performances at the Met between 1936 and 1946, and was a noted Sparafucile, Ramfis and King Marke. The last word on this *Don Giovanni* broadcast should go to one of the critics who was there: 'The production of Mozart's *Don Giovanni* ... is now one of the brightest ornaments of the Metropolitan ... there was the conducting of Mr. Walter, the sensitive performance of the orchestra, the unity of the ensemble and of the whole interpretive scheme. Once more the indispensability of a unity of the ensemble and of the whole interpretive scheme was demonstrated.' [*New York Times* 8 March 1942]

*(Charles Kullman)*

*(Norman Cordon)*

Less than two years later Bruno Walter conducted his only Met broadcast of *Le nozze di Figaro*. Pinza, Sayão and Novotná were joined by John Brownlee as Count Almaviva, Eleanor Steber as the Countess, Salvatore Baccaloni as Dr. Bartolo, Irra Petina as Marcellina and Alessio De Paolis as Don Basilio. Baritone John Brownlee (1900-1969) made his way from rural Australia to New York via Paris and London. He was helped into the limelight, in a manner of speaking, by his mentor Nellie Melba, when he was asked to sing Marcello alongside Melba's Mimi in her Covent Garden farewell in 1926. A recording preserves the occasion. Brownlee made his Met debut in 1937 as *Don Alfonso* in the famous recording of *Cosi fan tutte* made with the Glyndebourne cast in 1935. Although his wide repertoire encompassed the music of Puccini, Rossini, Bizet, Gounod and Wagner, it was as a Mozart singer that Brownlee was especially renowned. He sang *Don Alfonso* in the famous HMV recording of *Cosi fan tutte* made with the Glyndebourne cast in 1935. Brownlee returned to Glyndebourne in 1936 to capture Brownlee's *Don Giovanni*. The American radio audience would hear him as Almaviva no less than eight times between 1940 and 1950, and with Paul Jackson in not complimentary about Brownlee in the role, critic Olin Downes thought that on this occasion he delivered a performance full of 'personality, and an uncommon salience of detail, and he was unusually authoritative.' [*New York Times* 30 January 1944].

The youngest member of the cast is American soprano Eleanor Steber (1914-1990), not quite thirty at the time of the broadcast. Steber made her Met debut as *Sophie* in *Der Rosenkavalier* in 1940 and would make more than four hundred appearances before bowing out at the Met in 1966. Despite the occasional forays into the operas of Wagner and Verdi, most of Steber's career was dominated by the operas of Richard Strauss and Mozart. This 1944 broadcast of *Le nozze di Figaro* was the second of six featuring Steber heard by radio audiences between 1943 and 1955. On her debut in the role Howard Taubman commented: 'Miss Steber made a handsome Countess as far as sheer looks and carriage went. She also sang the part with credit ... her handling of "Dove sono" was the work of a young artist who is distinctly up to an exacting part.' Olin Downes thought her 1944 performance 'charming' while Paul Jackson praised her 'silvery timbre' and the 'complete poise of her vocal ions'. [*New York Times* 17 December 1942 and 30 January 1944; Jackson, *Saturday Afternoons* 309].

*(Brownlee in Nozze)*

*(Salvatore Baccaloni)*

Singing Dr. Bartolo is the Italian bass Salvatore Baccaloni (1900-1969). Although his early repertoire included such roles as Raimondo, Sparafucile, the King in Aida, and Timar, in the second half of his career he specialised and set standards in comic parts in the operas of Mozart, Donizetti and Rossini. Baccaloni enjoyed an extensive career in Europe and the United States and he was appeared in more than four hundred Met performances between 1940 and 1962. At the same time he was seen in a number of films including *The Desert Song* (1955) alongside Nelson Eddy, *Full of Life* (1956) with Judy Holliday, *Merry Andrew* (1958) with Danny Kaye, *Rock-a-Bye Baby* (1958) with Jerry Lewis, Fanny (1961) with Maurice Chevalier, and *The Pigeon That Took Rome* (1962) with Charlton Heston. Reviewing an earlier broadcast of *Le nozze di Figaro* Olin Downes praised Baccaloni as 'an admirably schooled buffo of the old school. He sang with authority and relish, and he drove each one of his scenes home.' [*New York Times* 8 December 1940].

Russian contralto Irra Petina (1908-2000) sings Marcellina. She appeared at the Met four hundred and forty-five times between 1933 and 1950, graduating eventually to the title role in *Carmen*, notably in Leonard Bernstein's production. Petina also appeared in several Broadway productions, notably in Leonard Bernstein's *Candide*. Baccaloni and Petina's comic timing during the scene where Figaro's parentage is revealed was singled out for particular praise by the critics.

Character tenor Alessio De Paolis (1893-1964), the Don Basilio, appeared an astonishing 1555 times at the Met between 1938 and 1964. After singing at the Met between 1938 and 1964, he died just four days after his last stage appearance in Eugene Onegin. Once again, Olin Downes provides the best summary of this performance: 'This was the best summary of this performance: 'This was comedy and art at a height for which the Metropolitan deserves the thanks of the community, and congratulations upon the prowess of an organization which can hold up such a model, and so communicate beauty and genius to people. As always when such a rare interpretive achievement takes form, it revealed anew the secrets of a master's spirit.' [*New York Times* 30 January 1944]

These two historic broadcasts are valuable documents of Mozart operas in the 1940s as well as showing the artistic standards the Metropolitan Opera was able to attain with their best artists under the leadership of an exceptional musician and great conductor. At a time when the world was mired in war, these operas acted as an antidote. It was not so much their comedy, but rather their 'warmth, loveliness, and enduring humanity shining through to cheer a grim world.' [*New York Times* 17 December 1942].

*(Walter listening to playback)*
