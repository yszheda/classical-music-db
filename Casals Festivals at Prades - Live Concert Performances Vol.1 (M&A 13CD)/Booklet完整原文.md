### Pablo Casals [1876-1973]
#### A life in music

Pablo Casals, one of this century's most revered musical figures, was born in the Catalonian town of Vendrell where his father was parish organist and choirmaster. Even by child-prodigy standards the youngster's musical development was awesome; able to identify notes at four, chorister at five, fluent pianist at six, and composing and transposing as well as taking up the violin at seven. In 1885 Casals was given his first "cello," a narrow, hollowed gourd and, at last, permitted to learn the organ. It therefore comes as no surprise to learn that the boy was accepted into Barcelona's Municipal School of Music in 1888 with the primary intention of becoming a composer. He studied counterpoint and harmony with Joaquin Malats and Francisco Costa Llobera and cello with Jose Garcia. Garcia, a highly influential tutor, soon discovered the young boy rebelling against current cello technique and developing a more flexible, less cumbrous method of his own.

Casals made his first public appearance as a member of a Barcelona cafe trio in 1889. Even at this youthful age he raised the Cafe Tost's musical sights and became something of a local celebrity.

It was now time for wider horizons. In 1899 Casals made his London and Paris debuts and, in 1901, his first trip to America. Three years later he returned to America on his first solo tour of the continent, performing at the White House and giving the first American performance, under the composer's baton, of Richard Strauss's *Don Quixote*. Paris became his base, his tiny house forming an oasis for the vast number of artists he befriended. From their evenings of relaxed private music-making grew the illustrious Cortot-Thibaud-Casals Trio, a team of highly contrasted personalities unified by a common love of chamber music, which came into existence in 1904/5 and lasted until the growing pressures of European turmoil forced them irreconcilably apart during the late thirties. In 1905 Casals made his first trip to Russia, his first rehearsal being attended by Rimsky-Korsakov, Glazunov and Liadov. Vienna, then the musical centre of Europe, by which time Casals was the world's undisputed master of the cello. The instrument was now the equal of its stringed brothers, its repertoire unbelievably transformed.

As a result of the First World War and his marriage to the American singer Susan Metcalfe, Casals' performing career became centered upon the American continent though this was the time that he became increasingly aware that the cello was not adequate enough to express all the music within him. In 1920 he formed the Orquestra Pau Casals in Barcelona of which he was artistic director and permanent conductor for seventeen years. It was a tenure which inevitably required a considerable amount of time though his world travels continued unabated. In the 1930s, Spain's troubled political life began to make itself felt and Franco's heartless regime eventually proved too much for Casals: he played his last concert in Spain, a benefit for the children of the Civil War, in October 1938 and left for Prades in France where he remained throughout the Second World War. In those dangerous years he walked the treacherous path of opposition to the Nazis whilst assisting his fellow Catalans as best he could.

When hostilities ceased Casals, aged 68 and now a world symbol of moral courage and resolution, resumed his solitary pilgrimage playing first in London in 1945, forty-six years after his first appearance there. Naively perhaps, he expected the victorious forces to remove Franco. Realising that this was not to be he returned to Prades and took a vow of musical silence, devoting his musical energies to composition, save for a few charity performances. As each year passed Casals became more and more entrenched and isolated. The solution to this impasse – the Prades Festival which commenced in 1950 – surprised even his closest friends. The Festival marked the beginning of Casals' gradual re-emergence into the active musical world (and the restoration of standards long presumed dead).

Casals' marriage to a young cello student, Martita Montañez, resulted in a move to Puerto Rico in 1957 where he formed a Symphony Orchestra and became president of the local Conservatory of Music. The Prades Festival competed briefly with the Festival Casals de Puerto Rico, which was first given in 1957 but was never the same again. Casals commenced a series of trips to the United States with a 1958 United Nations Concert. Within three years he was traveling the globe on behalf of his campaign for world peace, his chief offering being his oratorio *El Pessebre*, much of which was written during his years at Prades. Later he wrote his *Hymn of the United Nations*, conducting its première on United Nations Day 1971. Casals died in 1973 just weeks short of his ninety-seventh birthday.

#### Casals and Prades

Casals' friends were determined to find an honourable way in which to break the cellist's silent protest against Franco and those who supported him, to bring once more the great man's art before the public. Between them, and the pianist Mieczysław Horszowski, whose friendship with Casals dated back to 1906, and the violinist Alexander Schneider devised a scheme which they hoped would bring the "Hermit of Prades" back to the concert platform, a series of all-Bach concerts at Prades marking the two hundredth anniversary of Bach's death. Would Casals attend? Would he play "in honour" of his revered Bach? Casals eventually capitulated to the persuasive arguments of his old colleagues and a thirty-piece orchestra with Schneider as concert master was assembled and rapidly fashioned by Casals into an homogeneous ensemble. Yehudi Menuhin first visited Casals in 1947 hoping to enlist Casals' support for Furtwängler, then under a cloud of accusation concerning Nazi-collaboration. Menuhin later participated in the Prades Festivals, most memorably in 1955 which Casals once declared to be the finest of all the Prades participants, Menuhin was well aware that he personally brought "fresh air into an atmosphere by then somewhat claustrophobic with mingled flattery and exploitation" (as he states in his autobiography *Unfinished Journey*); something quite possible as his relationship with Casals was "warm" but also "disinterested." In the following year the same artists enjoyed a comparable success of which Schumann's sadly neglected Second Piano Trio is a souvenir.

Eugene Istomin was so moved when he discovered the true extent of Casals' isolation during the first Prades Festival that he stayed behind in order to act as Casals' accompanist for as long as his commitments enabled him. Few will need reminding that, along with Isaac Stern and Leonard Rose, Istomin became a member of a trio which in recent times became the equivalent to the Cortot-Thibaud-Casals Trio of the pre-WWII era.

A European guest violinist who took part in the 1953 Festival was Arthur Grumiaux who had made a sensational American debut the previous year. Now recognized as the outstanding exponent of the great Belgian violin tradition, his collaboration with William Kapell in Beethoven's First Violin Sonata and Mozart's Second Piano Quartet was one of the discoveries and delights of that fourth festival. Kapell, one of the very finest native American pianists was in 1953 poised on the brink of an exceptional international career. He was killed in a plane crash that same year aged 31.

The career of Paul Tortelier, who contributes the bass line to the Mozart Piano Trio as well as the Oboe Quartet bears an uncanny resemblance to that of Casals. Tortelier first met Casals, after whom he named one of his daughters (Maria de la Pau), in 1946. Also actively composing and conducting, Tortelier, too, had taken up the cudgels on behalf of world peace. The oboist Marcel Tabuteau, long-time section leader in Philadelphia, remains one of the great unsung masters of his instrument.

In the 1953 performance of Beethoven's last Cello Sonata Casals is partnered by Horszowski who commercially recorded the work with Casals in 1939. Their friendship was to last sixty-seven years. In the 1954 performance of the Kakadu Trio we hear another close collaborator of Casals, Rudolf Serkin, a regular participant at Prades and the colleague whose own Marlboro Festival was to be later honoured by Casals as conductor. Szymon Goldberg, ex-leader of the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra in the pre-Hitler era, requires no introduction, being one of the foremost chamber music performers of the 20th century.
