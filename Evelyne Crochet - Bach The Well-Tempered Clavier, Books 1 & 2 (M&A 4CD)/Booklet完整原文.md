# JOHANN SEBASTIAN BACH (1685-1750)
## The Well-Tempered Clavier

Bach wrote many works for the keyboard—French and English Suites, Toccatas, Partitas, Inventions and Sinfonias, the Goldberg Variations, Fantasies etc., and, of course, numerous works for the organ. Yet in none of these magnificent works does one sense the intimate nature, the transcendent message of *The Well-Tempered Clavier*. It stands as a profound expression of spirituality, of humanity, and one cannot help but think that this huge work represents a very personal account of the composer's philosophy, perhaps a look back on his own life journey. With its absence of theatricality, with its depth and straightforward simplicity, the *The Well-Tempered Clavier* takes us beyond the instrumental brilliance of Bach's other keyboard pieces (not to diminish their own depth and greatness) as we sense its authentic meaning.

The *Well-Tempered Clavier* consists of two volumes, each of twenty four preludes & fugues. The first dates from 1722 when Bach was Kapellmeister in Cöthen, the second from 1744—six years before his death—as he was cantor and director of the St. Thomas Church in Leipzig.

They are written in the twelve major and minor keys and proceed chromatically; each key revealing highly contrasted and diversified moods. The prelude, of a free, soulful character, is not a mere opening to the fugue of the same key. Though together they form a pair, each is a self-contained, complete composition. While Bach's style is essentially contrapuntal, the preludes are also strongly harmonic, vertical in form, in contrast to the fugues which are solely linear, horizontal. Starting from Book One all the way through the entire second book, the form becomes more extended, with the use of binary movements.

Because of their very nature, fugues are structural and require a special approach to listening. They start with one voice, the theme, as the main subject. As soon as it has been heard, the theme is stated again by the next voice while the first one expands into another idea, countersubject, serving, in some sort, as a canvas. One by one, in that fashion, each voice will be introduced, until all of them join in the development. The fugues may have two or up to five voices, and countersubjects often constitute a secondary theme of quasi-equal importance (eg: C sharp minor fugue, Book One). The theme may also take different forms: inversion, where the notes come in reverse order; augmentation, in which the theme is stated in longer, rhythmical values; or diminution, where the theme occurs in shorter rhythmical values, all these elements allowing for great invention and variety. The E flat minor fugue of Book I, and the B flat minor fugue of Book II illustrate the practice of this particular compositional technique.

The whole displays a superb representation of musical democracy: no voice predominates, each one pursues its own individual course while remaining all the time in a supportive way, mindful of and in harmony with the others.

Listening to a fugue is particularly rewarding. Rather than favoring one line over another systematically, each reading of the same fugue brings a different meaning, according to the choice of polyphony one makes. This process of selection defines our own personal relationship to the music. One must emphasize the richness and perfection of Bach's harmony, a model and a guiding light in rules of musical composition. His infallible skill demonstrates an audacity which even legitimizes bold moves such as harmonic dissonances and false relations. His absolute ease in handling the aesthetics of harmony often makes his music sound daring to the point of modernism (as in the twelve-tone theme of the B minor fugue in Book One).

It is said that Bach wrote *The Well-Tempered Clavier* for the benefit of students eager to learn as well as for the highly skilled musician. He made almost no mention of tempo, dynamics, character or phrasing, thus leaving these choices to the performer's discretion.

The term "Clavier" indicates all keyboard instruments available in his time: organ, harpsichord, clavichord, and also the pianoforte, in its development since the beginning of the XVIIIth century, which Bach undoubtedly knew. It is the piano, in this performer's opinion, that best serves this music of a high order because of its wealth of color and expressive qualities. The "Well-Tempered" part refers to a rather complex acoustical issue, the end result being that a keyboard could from now on be tuned in any and all keys. More technically speaking, the new method of tuning was meant to be free of the limits imposed by the multiple aspects of the pitch, namely the discrepancy between enharmonics (for ex. E flat and D sharp) which caused intervals to be distorted and resulted in making distant keys and modulations sound impossibly out of tune.

The acoustic "beats" that characterize the pitch were therefore altered in order to eliminate the gaps between enharmonics. In taking away the acoustical difference and uniting enharmonics into one and the same note, it made all intervals, especially perfect fifths, so vital to the tonal structure, sound compatible through out the whole range, from low to high register. Consequently, composers, no longer limited by these distorted intervals, were free to use all keys, bringing all instruments together under the same umbrella.

This new method of tuning—the fixing of the pitch—was quite revolutionary and offered a solution of compromise which led to what we know as the equal temperament. It enabled all instruments to play in tune together in all keys. Without being asked to clearly grasp the acoustic nature of the question, one can only marvel at Bach's response to this major development which inspired him to write *The Well-Tempered Clavier*.

The *Well-Tempered Clavier* was only published in 1801. Though it was greatly admired by composers it is only by the end of the nineteenth century that it became universally recognized. It is commonly considered as the Bible in music. Encompassing all thoughts and feelings and not unlike *The Passion*, it offers a full spectrum of human emotion, from the dark side of despair to the bright one of joy and serenity, even humor (A major: Book One, B minor: Book Two).

It stands unequalled in all music literature.

# Evelyne Crochet

Evelyne Crochet's repertoire covers a wide scope, spanning over all periods in music. She performed Bach's whole *Well-Tempered Clavier*, Debussy's complete piano works, and music up to our present time. Her early recording in Russia, Mozart's Sonata K 310 and Beethoven's Sonata Opus 110 was followed by the first complete recording of Gabriel Fauré's piano works. This extensive recording was received with unanimous international acclaim (the *New York Times* described it as "a labor of love"). For Philips Records, she recorded the world premiere of newly-discovered works by Erik Satie, albums of Bach Transcriptions and Schubert's music and, with Alfred Brendel, she recorded Schubert's four-hand piano duets.

A native of France, she holds First Prize from the Paris Conservatory where her teachers were Yvonne Lefébure and Nadia Boulanger. She worked with Edwin Fischer in Switzerland. In Bern, she attended a master class by Rudolf Serkin, after which he invited her to come as a family guest to the United States and study with him.

Her first major US performance took place in Boston as the partner of Francis Poulenc in the premiere of his two-piano concerto, with the Boston Symphony under Charles Munch. It was followed by numerous appearances with the B.S.O. under Munch and successive conductors, and by many engagements with the world's major orchestras. She collaborated with such conductors as Steinberg, Leinsdorf, Kubelik, Kempe, Martinon, Foss, Foster, V. Neuman, Janowski, Commissiona and Schmidt-Isserstedt.

Crochet appeared in recitals and in chamber music participations in major concert Series and in venues such as New York City's Carnegie Hall and Lincoln Center, Chicago's Orchestra Hall, London's Royal Festival Hall, Amsterdam's Concertgebouw, Vienna's Konzerthaus, Mexico's Bellas Artes, Washington's Allied Arts, major cities in the U.S., Canada, Europe, and on the American continent.

Evelyne Crochet has held faculty and artist in residence positions at the universities of Brandeis, Boston, Georgia State, Rutgers, and the New England Conservatory. She also served as an assistant to Rudolf Serkin at the Curtis Institute in Philadelphia.

She resides and teaches privately in New York City.
