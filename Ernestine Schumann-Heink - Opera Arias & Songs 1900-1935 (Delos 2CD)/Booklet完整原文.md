### ERNESTINE SCHUMANN-HEINK
15 June, 1861 - 17 November, 1936

Ernestine Schumann-Heink and Nellie Melba were born within less than a month of one another, but but almost exactly on opposite sides of the globe. That world that separated them then would provide a contrast in the lives, personalities and careers of two of the greatest singers of their generation. Melba came from a well-to-do family. After seven years' study in Australia, she was able to make her debut in a principal role in Brussels after less than one year's study with one of the most famous singing teachers of the day. The first time she set her foot on an operatic stage was a triumph from which she never looked back as she conquered the world's great opera houses in Paris, London, and New York and found herself enmeshed in the social life of the elite of these world centers. She began in full bloom at the top of the ladder.

## RECORDINGS OF ERNESTINE SCHUMANN-HEINK

Schumann-Heink had two "unofficial" encounters with the phonograph before she made "commercial" recordings. The first was probably in the year 1900 when she and her husband stepped into the recording "laboratory" of the Universal Talking Machine Co. in New York and made three 7" personal discs. These were marked "A", "B", and "C": the first is Mme. Schumann-Heink singing *Wie ein Grüssen*, a song written for her by Adolf Mehrkens which she used in a 1900 concert; Record "B" is entitled *Botschaft aus Amerika an ihre Kinder*. It is a spoken message by Heink and Paul Schumann to their children who had been left at home in Dresden. The final recoded, "C" is spoken by Paul Schumann alone. These recordings were thought lost in Germany before the war, and were thought lost when a collector in upstate New York found another copy of the Mehrkens song in an antique store in 1953 and allowed William H. Seltsam to make a copy of it for our release in this presentation (Disc 1, Track 1). The voice of Schumann-Heink can also be faintly heard in excerpts caught on a cylinder machine during performances at the Metropolitan Opera in 1901-03. The singer's first commercial recordings were made in February (?) 1903 for Columbia, and we have included one of these (Track 2) in our release. Schumann-Heink became an exclusive Victor Red Seal recording artist in January, 1906; she participated in some 47 recording sessions for Victor, the last being in January, 1931. Our release includes 32 published Victor recordings; 7 unpublished Victors; 5 recordings made from radio broadcasts in 1929, 1934, and 1935 and one unpublished experimental recording from 1931.

Of the unpublished Victor, *Down in the Forest* (Disc 1, Track 20) comes from a test pressing given to us by one of the singer's grand-daughters. The balance between voice and piano leaves something to be desired; it was probably intended to remakes this, but this never took place. *Old Black Joe* (Disc 2, Track 5) is considered one of the real gems of this collection, both as a remarkably sympathetic interpretation of the song and for its charming spoken dedication to one of the singer's great-grandchildren. It was obviously never intended for public release, and was never entered in the Victor recording books or given a number.

*Shepherd's Love* (Disc 2, Track 7) is a late acoustic recording; it was probably held for release, but then cancelled because of the advent of electrical recording. Other unpublished Victor recordings are alternate takes for titles which were issued.

Schumann-Heink's spoken introduction and her reminiscences of Brahms (Disc 2, Track 1) were taken from a sponsored radio program which the singer presented on a weekly basis from Chicago in the spring and summer of 1934. The three radio broadcast selections from 12 January 1929 (Disc 2, Tracks 11-13) were from a coast-to-coast program celebrating the opening of the Northern Pacific Railroad's Cascade Tunnel. They pre-date the availability of instantaneous recording. They were recorded directly from the radio broadcast in our own homes, those of us who can still maintain the tradition.

This was her last Christmas, but thanks to recordings, those of us who care can still maintain the tradition in our own homes.

*William R. Moran*

### 18. Träume - Dreams

Sag', welch wunderbare Träume
halten meinen Sinn umgangen?
Daß sie nicht wie leere Schläume
in jedes Stunde blüh'n,
jeden Tag schöner blüh'n,
und mit jeder Stunde gehn,
und mit jeder Stunde sink ich in die Seele,
Dort im ewigen Frieden
Dort geht das in jedes blaut mir geht.
Träum' welche die wahre Strahlen
Dreams, which as glorious rays,
that paints the whole in my mind.
Träum', welche the spring sun,
that that may grows and blossoms,
softly that breathes gives out their scent,
and then sink into the grave.
