## [Introduction by Mike Ashman (English)]

### "I am a Greek - good for everything"

The Greek composer, teacher and conductor Dimitri Mitropoulos (1896-1960) had an adventurous career that sounds like fiction and is often written up as if it were. The son of a leather-goods shop owner, he was composing by the age of eleven and organizing musical gatherings at his house every Saturday. He studied at the Athens Conservatory, writing an opera based on Maeterlinck's *Sœur Béatrice* and staging it as a vehicle for his then lover Katina Paxinou (later to win an Oscar for her role in the wartime Hollywood movie of *Zorba the Greek*). He secured a study grant to the conservatory in Brussels, from where his composition teacher Paul Gilson ordered him to Berlin.

In the German capital Mitropoulos stood in at zero notice for an absent conductor for the whole first act of *Parsifal* at the Staatsoper, passing discreetly from his place of observer into the conductor's chair at the chail gave him immediate fame. After some years he was invited to give a Lisztian Greek Sonata from the concert platform to the virtuoso Egon Petri, he was recommended to Ferruccio Busoni at the Prussian Academy of the Arts. Busoni's reaction to the Greek's composition secured Mitropoulos an assistant-conductor post at the Berlin Staatsoper under Erich Kleiber, whose modern-opera repertory of Wozzeck and Milaud's Christophe Colomb Mitropoulos would repeat.

Fallout from these events secured Mitropoulos an assistant-conductor post at the Berlin Staatsoper under Erich Kleiber. From that time Mitropoulos got his career going, in starting a new path that would lead eventually to the United States. Making a patriotic return to Greece in 1924, Mitropoulos led (and founded) various local orchestras during the next six years. He started a lifelong friendship with Katina Katsayanis, and a (later published) correspondence, the artist/patroness intensity of which has been compared to Tchaikovsky's with Nadezhda von Meck. In 1930 another of Mitropoulos's patrons hired the Berlin Philharmonic for him and he created a sensation playing and conducting Prokofiev's Third Piano Concerto for piano - "He's taken it over completely. I'll just have to write another concerto for myself," was the composer's less than laudatory reaction. By 1936 Mitropoulos had gained such a reputation for himself in Europe that Koussevitzky invited him for concerts with the Boston Symphony. Trumping what was to become the staple program mixing Romantic showpieces with worthy but untried contemporary works by American composers, Mitropoulos opened the way to spending much of the rest of his career in the United States. He led the Minneapolis Symphony Orchestra from 1937 to 1949 - a decisive period in history (the venue changed name after World War II) to the New York Philharmonic-Symphony Society Orchestra (as it was called then) in 1949.

In New York's essentially conservative mainstream musical circles Mitropoulos's career faltered. His programming started to look more eccentric than adventurous, his selection of contemporary American composers was narrower than people expected after the publicity that had preceded it. That was because his career was already starting to fade. It's all very interesting to read: the story of a Greek conductor in the United States, and after what can only be described as the "dirty laundry" that came out when Leonard Bernstein, his protégé, replaced him after he left, it's been pretty much written out of the history books.

His concert operas were legendary, either as triumphs (Elektra, Wozzeck) or failures (Christophe Columb). The ranks of the famously difficult and impatient New York musicians ("murderers' row" one conductor called it) began to get tangled and were difficult for him as one got older, his health was already crucially damaged by all this, but at first almost as a sideline, his career was already relaunching itself in Europe, with regular invitations to Berlin and Vienna, to the Concertgebouw and radio stations gave regular serious invitations. In an age of sixty-four years, when he was already repeating Mahler's Third with the Orchestra of La Scala. He had a weak heart and died prematurely at a rehearsal.

Many of Mitropoulos's recordings (especially the New Yorker ones for Columbia) were made under difficult sweatshop conditions, it is largely on "live" material that his newly refurbished reputation has come to rest. Like many conductors returning to work in war-torn Europe, Mitropoulos found the radio orchestras in Germany and Italy offered well-organized artistic havens, open in terms of rehearsal time and repertoire, happy to program distinctive interpretations of mainstream classics alongside newer music. The two Mendelssohn Symphonies are Mitropoulos's specialty, perhaps underrated because of the low reputation and scarcity of performances, they enjoyed in America when he started operations there. This present disc is completed by a work typical of Mitropoulos's wide-ranging sympathies, some François Couperin.
