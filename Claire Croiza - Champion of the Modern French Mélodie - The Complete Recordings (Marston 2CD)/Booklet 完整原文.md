## Liner Notes

### Claire Croiza

even more remarkable was the number of premiers in which she participated: in 23 November 1907 she sang Penelope in the La Monnaie premiere of Massenet's Ariane; twenty-three performances; in the world premiere of Pierre de Bréville's *Pour l'inauguration* (eight performances); on 10 April 1910 Alays in the world premiere of Guy Ropartz's *Le Pays*; six performances); on 12 May 1910 Clytemnestra in a major revival of Gluck's *Iphigénie en Aulide* (five performances); on 30 May 1910 Charlotte in the La Monnaie premiere of Strauss's *Werther* (nine performances); and on 9 December 1913 the title role in the first La Monnaie production of Fauré's *Pénélope* (fourteen performances).

It was not until 1908 that she made her debut at the Paris Opéra in a limited engagement beginning 26 April as Dalila in *Samson et Dalila*. On 21 February 1911 at the Concerts Lamoureux she appeared in a concert performance of the *Pelléas et Mélisande* of Debussy, conducted by André Messager.

At the Théâtre des Arts in Paris on 3 February 1913 she sang in a concert performance of Mussorgsky's *L'Invasion* de Peppe, arranged and conducted by Vladimir Golschmann; and on 1 March at the Salle Gaveau she created Léonore in *La Favorite* by Donizetti, arranged by Jacques de Tessel.

At the Paris Conservatory on 1 March 1913 she sang the premiere of Lili Boulanger's *Faust et Hélène* with Henri Albers as Méphistophélès and David Devriès as Faust, and later reprised her role at the Concerts Colonne with Albers and with Méphistophélès sung by Georges Petit. At this same performance she also created "Cor me crêpant" and "Promenade" from Alfred Bruneau's orchestral song set *Chant de la vie*.

Her debut at the Opéra-Comique took place on 14 April 1914 as Charlotte in *Werther* after the title role as a favor to the artist. She remained there through 1915. On 6 January 1916 she again appeared at the Paris Opéra in the creation of *La Légende de Joseph*, by Strauss and Hofmannsthal.

On 12 March 1916 she returned to Monte Carlo and sang Massenet's *Thérèse* with Charles Fontaine and Alfred Maguenat. In 1918, particularly in an effort to avoid the drudgery of traveling under fire, she entered a convent, but left it after three months. She then took up residence at La Baule-les-Pins, where she gave concerts and taught singing.

### The War Years and After

During World War I she gave herself unselfishly to the war effort, appearing in countless benefits throughout France, Switzerland and in Genoa, Italy for the Red Cross, sharing a number of these occasions with Isadora Duncan. In 1918 she received from King Albert of Belgium the Order of Léopold and was the first French woman to be decorated with the cross.

From 1923-1924 she made several tours of England. At a concert at Queen's Hall in London on 14 February 1924 she sang in the British premiere of Ravel's *Don Quichotte* with the London Symphony Orchestra conducted by Ravel himself.

In March 1924 she sang the title role in the first Paris performance of Gluck's *Alceste* at the Salle Gaveau with the Orchestre des Concerts Straram conducted by Walter Straram.

On 17 April 1924 she appeared at the Salle Gaveau in a concert of French music under the direction of Désiré-Emile Inghelbrecht. On 14 May 1924 she sang the role of Geneviève in *Pelléas et Mélisande* at the Opéra-Comique.

Claire Croiza was born Claire Rosenberg in Paris on 14 September 1882, the youngest of three children of Colonel John Townsend O'Connell, an expatriate American, and his Italian wife. References cite various versions of Croiza's family name: Comolli, Cozollo and O'Connolly, the last given by Betty Bannerman (British Institute of Recorded Sound Bulletin, no. 1, Summer 1956). Bannerman was a student and intimate friend of Croiza during the last 20 years of the singer's life, and for that reason alone, I have accepted her version.

Croiza took private singing lessons in Paris with Mlles. Revilly and Mathon. When they felt Croiza was ready, they took her to Jean de Reszke. After a short period of study, he arranged an audition for her with the opera at Nancy; she was immediately engaged and made her debut in Nancy in *Mireille* in 1905. Her success at Nancy led to her engagement by Camille Chevillard for the Paris creation of The Angel in Elgar's *Dream of Gerontius* in a special performance of the Concerts Lamoureux at the Trocadéro in 1906.

Immediately following her successes in France, she was engaged by the Théâtre Royal de la Monnaie in Brussels, making her debut there on 11 September 1906 as Dalila in *Samson et Dalila* with Laurent Swolfs and Jules Lassing.

On 27 December she sang Dido in *Les Troyens à Carthage* with Léon Lafitte, Ramon Blanchart, Hippolyte Bellhomme and Armand Crabbé (fourteen performances). For the next eight years, she was one of La Monnaie's leading mezzos, appearing in Dalila, Orphée, Charlotte (*Werther*), Carmen, Erda (*Siegfried*), and Léonore (*La Favorita*).

But it was at Nancy led to her engagement by Camille Chevillard for the Paris creation of the Angel in Elgar's *Dream of Gerontius* in a special performance of the Concerts Lamoureux at the Trocadéro in 1906.

It was in Nancy in 1908 she made her debut at the Paris Opéra in a limited engagement beginning 26 April as Dalila in *Samson et Dalila*. On 21 February 1911 at the Concerts Lamoureux she appeared in a concert performance of the *Pelléas et Mélisande* of Debussy, conducted by André Messager.

There are numerous erroneous references stating that Croiza created Arthur Honegger's *Judith* at Monte Carlo in 1926. On 13 June 1925 at the Théâtre au Jorat in Mézières, Switzerland, Croiza had created Honegger's incidental music to René Morax's play (numbered H.57A in Harry Halbreich's *L'œuvre d'Arthur Honegger* and subtitled "drame biblique").

After the success of the play, Honegger decided to expand his incidental music into a full opera. The opera (H.57B, subtitled "opéra-oratorio") was staged at Monte Carlo under the direction of René Morax. It was in this version that Croiza appeared as Judith.

On Good Friday 1912 at the Concerts Lamoureux, she gave the French premiere of L'Enfant prodigue (the scene) by Debussy with the composer's direction.

### Teaching Career

In 1924 she became a professor at the Paris Conservatory and at approximately the same time at the Geneva Conservatory. In Paris she taught Janine Micheau, Suzanne Juyol, Camille Maurane, Gérard Souzay, Jacques Jansen and many others.

Betty Bannerman, who had first met Croiza in 1928 and had become a student and later a close friend, wrote that in 1940 at the start of the war: "We begged her to return with us, but she felt she must return to her own home and so lost her class at the Conservatoire. She was terrified of the war—lost over four stone in weight—and the mental anguish and fear undoubtedly caused the fatal illness which overtook her ten years ago." Claire Croiza died in Paris on 15 May 1946.

Croiza made her first recordings in 1927 after she had been singing twenty-two years. The voice in 1927 is in fine condition and there was very little change in it by the time she made her final recordings in 1936. Croiza's disc recordings not only provide some very beautiful singing but also contain a virtually unique documentation of historical art.

Croiza's records, by the nature of the times, were 78s. Three of them (Columbia D13032, D13033 and D13034) are probably among the most elusive recordings in the world, vying with many of those of the fabled accetical tenors.

## A Note on These Records

About fifteen years ago I became passionately interested in Claire Croiza and began to collect her recordings. Her catalog was undertaken in preparation.

Most of Claire Croiza's nineteen solo recordings were issued on English 78s and many French singers and it seems that no one collection has a complete set of her discs.

The three rarest of her records are, understandably, D13042, D13043, and D13044 which can be heard on Disc One, Tracks 2-7. After scouring the world of record collectors, I located only one copy of each in three different collections. Sadly the copy of D13043 was in extremely poor condition and D13044, while slightly better, I have had to use these transfers rather severely in order to reduce the distortion on these discs. I hope that the results will be acceptable considering the circumstances.

Mme. Croiza in addition to her solo discs, participated in three ensemble recordings, all made for Columbia: Debussy's *Pelléas et Mélisande*, Honegger's *Judith* and Milhaud's *L'Orestie*. I have only included sides in which Croiza participates; due to the time limitation of a two disc set.

## A Note From Ward Marston

This two disc set was inspired by Victor Girard and without his help, I would never have undertaken its preparation.

First of all, no one else I have known would have spent the immense amount of time necessary to locate and digitize eighteen of Claire Croiza's four recordings. Additionally, his research on the various incarnations of Honegger's *Judith* has finally explained which version it was that Croiza actually recorded.

On 28 November 1998, Vic passed away suddenly at the age of seventy-one. His death was a shock to all of us who knew him, and I personally will miss him as a friend and mentor. Over the past three years I was enormously fortunate in many ways, and I will always relish the memory of the past three years we spent together in considering future Marston releases.

He was one of the most brilliant and knowledgeable individuals I have ever known. I am proud of those people that I somehow seemed to know everybody. He was a skilled and intricate researcher with a prodigious memory, especially for details. Although his scholarly contributions in the field of Anthropology/Linguistics are well known to his academic colleagues, he will be remembered most by record collectors for his many published discographies and his pioneering efforts to correct the records. *Cylinder and Disc*, co-authored with Harold Barnes in 1964. Over the past thirty-six years, he had devoted thousands of pages of discographical research on music and musicians, labels, and the attendant performers. At the time of his death, he was working on a book documenting the first fifty years of the American theater. His co-author, William Shuman, will complete the project.

Vic's real love was music and its performers. He was particularly interested in opera, with a special predilection for the French. His knowledge of French singers was such a special joy. When speaking of them, he gave the impression of having known them personally, as he surely did in the cases of Mary Garden, Ninon Vallin, and many others.

I am proud to have known Vic Girard and dedicate this Claire Croiza album to his memory. He will be greatly missed and fondly remembered.

/ At the thought of the spirit of my sin / And the weight of the yoke on my heart. / At the thought of my sin I conceived of an idea to appease their God; / I believed that this would soften him / And calm his punishment. / That this God, whoever he would become sought my soul, the one who would give / - Power! of God, forger my weakness. / I sought: people flee. the sacrifice ends. / The high priest approaches me with a frown: / While speaking to me, a surprise! a terror! / I see the same child who threatened me. / As if that terrifying dream had been printed on my brain. / A cry escapes from his same menacing lips. / I then left him, my back, his eyes, and all his features: / It was I walked beside the high priest: What fright I was made to suffer! / I fear beyond my eyes. / Such is the trouble that fills my poor heart; / And it is for this I wish to consult you.

## Technical Notes on the Recordings

[D13032 and D13034 appear to exist in single copies even in private collections, while two copies of D13033 are known to survive, one in a private collection, the other in an institutional archive. There is also a known unpublished index, copies of which have been difficult]. A set parsed from "La bonne Chanson" (Fauré); *Le jardin mouillé* (Roussel); and *Judith* (Boulnois).

In order to avoid confusion, it must be noted that two of Croiza's recordings were issued in different recordings. D15082 was already published in 1929, coupled with Honegger's *Automne* with the Swiss singer; the same unaltered recording set it apart, published in about 1933, the Swiss songs coupled with Rousseau's *Les amours des amants*, D152031, containing selections from Honegger's *Judith*, was issued in two different couplings, the first bearing matrices [W]5203[?], *Cantique de sainte*, and W18XX9, *Introdite et antique de reynes*; the second, matrices 055203-2 and [W]5217, *Rêve de Judith* et *Cantique de la beauté*. The U.S. Columbia issue used the first coupling; hence, all of [W]18XX9 was never published in America.

Croiza's recordings from Honegger's *Judith* and Milhaud's *L'Orestie* are also problematical. Some were made in Paris (1928), others in Brussels in at least two different recording sessions (1929 and 1936), despite the fact that all were labeled as accompanied by the Antwerp Cecylia Chorus and Nocturnes Consorts. Honegger, under both labels, was one of the experts that the recording from *Judith* took place across these lengthy sessions.

"Columbia" 3 "ten-inch" and "IX" (twelve-inch) matrix series were first used in London in 1924 for French-language recordings, then exclusively in France and Belgium from 1924 to 1933. The "W" matrix series was as a licensing marker to indicate an electrical recording made by the Columbia company to distinguish it from purely acoustic recorded pressings and labels, as the Croiza recordings attest. A bracketed "W" distinguishes this omission in the listings.

London, either officially or unofficially, contracted with the French Catholic Church, began recording operations in 1932 and issued a vast number of religious, quasi-religious, and liturgical music, as well as hundreds of recordings of folk ballads, which are indeed "for captive audience" nature, related to "les Scouts" and other Catholic children's organizations.
