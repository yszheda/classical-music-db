## [内页 1 - 左侧]

**ROSA PONSELLE**

Under the headline "THERE WAS NOTHING LIKE THE PONSELLE SOUND," Anthony Tommasini of the *New York Times* (January 23, 1972) wrote: "Rosa Ponselle was one of the most imposing figures in opera history. Her voice, according to those who heard her sing, was a big, pure, colorful golden voice that rose effortlessly, through the upper register, in the face, rolling over the stage in wide, billowing waves like shock-absorbing blast waves, wriggling with sheer physiological pleasure."

"There are voices and voices," said Met soprano Opera in the 1930s had nothing good or bad. But there was only one voice like the Ponselle sound, ever. To many of us it was the most single operatic experience in our lifetime."

"She never had formal education, no schooling, not even a kindergarten; her vocal technique went from bottom to top in the finished, most seamless of tones, with no shifting from chest to head. The good fairy was very kind to Rosa Ponselle," Maria Callas told her friend, the English critic and author John Ardoin. "I knew that Rosa Ponselle was the greatest singer of us all. I adored her voice and wore out countless records. As soon as she came on, I would replace it."

The contralto Gloria Swanson, who had longed to be an opera singer, wrote in her autobiography that Rosa Ponselle offered, to her great surprise, an already prepared contract to sign.

Gaetano Donizetti had the role of Leonora in LUISA MILLER premiere of Vespri Siciliani, and the Met was scheduled for the opening night of that season, 18 November 1918, as well as an occasion in 1918 for Ponselle to sing the title role in AIDA and the Verdi REQUIEM—all to be learned and prepared for in five months' time! She also changed her name, inventing the then fashionable sounding "Ponselle" to disguise her from the Rosa Ponzillo that appeared on her first record.

She was advised by conductor Romano Romani, who became her life-long coach, accompanist and great friend, to sign a contract with Columbia.
