## French lessons
**Pianist Stephen Hough tells Jeremy Pound about the highs and lows of learning from the great Vlado Perlemuter in person.**

**You experienced Vlado Perlemuter's expertise at first hand when he coached you during your masterclasses at the Royal Northern College of Music (RNCM) in the late 1970s. For you, what stood out about him as a player?**

He had a natural elegance to his playing. It was always a beautiful sound, and it was very fluid, graceful and tasteful. He never banged the piano and never went for cheap effects. I suppose you could say he was very French in that way, and the repertoire gained enormously from that approach. That's probably one of the reasons why Ravel himself was so keen on Perlemuter playing his music: he was very keen for people not to interpret his music – he wanted them to play what was on the page – and I don't think he liked, for instance, Alfred Cortot's playing because Cortot had a huge personality and had a very distinctive kind of rubato. Perlemuter, in contrast, had a much less vivid personality.

**His repertoire, though, was not particularly extensive, was it?**

He only played and taught a very limited number of pieces. There was a list of them which you put up on the notice board at the RNCM, and if you wanted to come in and play, as a particular Beethoven sonata wasn't on the list, he wouldn't teach it. He had very specific repertoires: all of Chopin, all of Ravel, but no Rachmaninov and very little Liszt. He was a fascinating throwback to a particular age.

**Can you explain a bit about what he was like as a teacher?**

He was lovely to us and encouraging to me when I played for him. By the time I was studying with him he was very frail and gouty but he could still play very beautifully when he was demonstrating and was most lively to hear. Plus there were so many wise things and experiences that he'd acquired directly from the likes of Ravel and Marguerite Long, and I found that fascinating. I played for him about three times and I was hoping that he might teach me permanently, but I think for me that would have been wrong – he had a very specific way of thinking of everything. And it wasn't flexible, I have to say!

**Do you think Perlemuter's decision to limit his repertoire enhanced or hindered his reputation?**

That is hard to say really. I think people like to think of someone as a specialist – it makes it easier if you can categorise someone, and so in that sense he was always seen as a French music specialist, with Chopin added on.

**Can you explain a bit about what he was like as a teacher?**

He was very kind and encouraging to me when I played to him. By the time I was studying with him he was very frail and gouty but he could still play very beautifully when he was demonstrating and was most lively to hear. Plus there were so many wise things and experiences that he'd acquired directly from the likes of Ravel and Marguerite Long, and I found that fascinating. I played for him about three times and I was hoping that he might teach me permanently, but I think for me that would have been wrong – he had a very specific way of thinking of everything. And it wasn't flexible, I have to say!

**In what way, exactly...?**

His approach was always this is it and he would even write fingering in your score – you would just have to play it for them, even if they didn't suit your hands. In one sense it's invaluable hearing him say 'The composer says this, that and the other, but that there isn't the one way to play music – in some ways the very definition of great music is that it can be interpreted in different ways. When I studied with Gordon Green and Derrick Wyndham, both of them had very broad minds musically, and Gordon in particular would never demonstrate anything because everything was about the student's own personality being developed – what he dreaded most of all was that a student imitated him! Perlemuter, in contrast, spent all the time demonstrating.

**How familiar are you with his recordings?**

There are some on record and, I think, he is generally they're not so easily available. There's no big best set of them, partly because he recorded on several different labels. Maybe that is something that is overdue – I would love to hear some of the earlier recordings.

*ANDREW CULLEN*

## The art of refinement
How much of Fauré at the Paris Conservatoire, Ravel shared his teacher's meticulous approach and sophisticated style, as **Ailian Haylock** explains

**Maurice Ravel** (1875-1937)
Miroirs

**Gabriel Fauré** (1845-1924)
Nocturnes Nos 1, 7 and 13
Barcarolles Nos 5, 6, 7 and 8

Ravel was just 14 when he began attending preparatory classes at the Paris Conservatory. By the mid-1890s he had his first work in print – the *Menuet antique* – and was making exceptional progress thanks largely to Fauré (then aged 54), who had been appointed professor of composition. In 1900 Ravel entered the Prix de Rome, with its promise of a year's study in the 'eternal city'. Three times in succession he failed to win it, despite the fact that he was already a prominent figure in Parisian musical circles with several trailblazing new pieces behind him including *Jeux d'eau* ('Fountains' or 'Playing-water', 1901) for piano, the String Quartet (1902-3) and the song collection *Shéhérazade* (1903).

The main stumbling block was the Conservatoire's director Théodore Dubois, a staunch traditionalist who viewed Ravel as a renegade who refused to play by the normal rules. 1905 proved to be the turning point in both men's careers. In this momentous year Ravel achieved new levels of creative mastery with his shimmering *Miroirs* and nonchalantly neoclassical *Sonatine* (both for solo piano), alongside his striking harp-led chamber work, the *Introduction et Allegro*. Yet still Dubois refused to acknowledge Ravel's burgeoning artistic genius and left himself wide open to derision from the young firebrand (at the fifth attempt!) when it allowed us to progress beyond the preliminary round of the Prix de Rome. The resulting outcry made headline news and such was the storm of protest unleashed (it became known as the 'Ravel Affair') that Dubois had to resign. Poetic justice was served when he was replaced by Ravel's former teacher, Fauré!

By now the main stylistic features of Ravel's music were firmly in place, each one a natural extension of his own multifaceted talents. His distaste for appearance – he had particular passions for glories, tiles and a dapper pair of black patent shoes – was reflected in a serious love of music which occupies the central core of his pieces. At least this unique ancestry on his lineage meant he had no opportunity to imbue his music with the sights and sounds of the Hispanic peninsula, as in the *Alborada del gracioso* from *Miroirs*. He loved keeping in touch with the latest fads and fancies – his later music embraced jazz and then – yet he possessed a child-like delight in traditional forms and genres, reflecting his love of toys, especially mechanical devices. Small wonder, then, that his most devoted admirers, astutely summed him up as 'the most perfect of Swiss clockmakers'.

The very title *Miroirs* suggests reflections of images in music, in this case a set of five ravishingly individual pieces, and is one dedicated to a member of Les Apaches, an ad hoc group of self-styled modernist composers (the word means literally 'hoodlums') that had helped form around the dawn of the 20th century.

It opens with *Noctuelles* ('Nocturnal Moths', dedicated to Léon-Paul Fargue), whose chromatically enhanced flutterings dart and dive elusively around a brief central episode evoking the eerie shadows of a Gothic castle. In *Oiseaux tristes* ('Sad Birds'), dedicated to Ricardo Viñes, who gave the first public performance in 1906, Ravel flouts his flouredishes of microtonically detailed washes of sound to produce a dazzling sonic exemplar for Messiaen's avian masterworks.

The following pair of movements inspired two of Ravel's most bewitchingly evocative orchestrations. 'Une barque sur l'océan' (A Boat on the Ocean, dedicated to Paul Sordes) is a tone poem in miniature, whose tidal washes of sound sweep incessantly mutates in the water's rolling power under the radiant calm of the midday sun. *Alborada del gracioso* ('The Jester's Morning Song', dedicated to musicologist Michel-Dimitri Calvocoressi), with its famously tricky, castanet-evoking repeated notes, while *La vallée des cloches* ('The Valley of Bells'), dedicated to Maurice Delage) evokes the magical interplay of bell-like sonorities with delicate subtlety.

While in his piano writing Ravel tended to take his lead from the choreographic precision and textural multi-layering of Liszt, Fauré's piano models were Chopin and Schumann. If Ravel viewed each piece as an individual sonic challenge, Fauré found musical fulfillment within a series of generic forms. Ravel's signature pieces are all minutely titled *Minuet, Gaspard de la nuit* and *Le tombeau de Couperin*, whereas Fauré's his 13 Nocturnes, 13 Barcarolles, five Impromptus, four Valses-caprices and nine Preludes.

A musician of refined culture and elegance – and irresistibly attractive to women – Fauré epitomised what the US composer Copland is the sum of all the remarks made by one of his temperament: sensibility, impeccable taste, classic restraint'. Swiss composer Honegger had his finger firmly on the pulse when he remarked: 'I know of no other music that is more purely and uniquely music, except perhaps that of Mozart or Schumann.' Fauré also possessed an extraordinary inner ear that enabled him to compose away from the piano and once memorably referred to the art of composing as being 'like a sticking door I have to open'.

It was the Irish émigré composer John Field (1782-1837) who first encapsulated the Romantic era's obsession with the magic of the night in a new type of piano miniature called the 'nocturne'. Chopin inherited Field's delight in decorative filigree and poetic fantasy, taking the genre to a whole new level. He also established its archetypal A-B-A structure, the outer sections consisting of a hauntingly expressive melody in the right hand, accompanied by a hypotonic calm, repeated rhythmic pattern in the left.

Fauré imparted a restrained nobility and poise to the nocturne that is evident from his first published example in 1883. Of the remainder of the nocturnes the most emotional world as Chopin's famous F minor Prelude, No. 7 is C-sharp minor typifies Fauré's tautened, almost tragic despair with sublime grace, while No. 13 in B minor (the last of the series, composed in 1921) distils a lifetime's experiences into melting phrases of heartfelt simplicity.

Chopin composed one barcarolle (a late work likened by celebrated virtuoso Karl Tausig as 'a love scene in a gondola'), whereas Fauré set the genre a bass for some of his most enchanting inspirations. The sublime controlledness and rippling rise of No. 6 in E flat conceals its technical intricacies behind waves of poetic inspiration. Composed nine years later, in 1905, the ebullient No. 8 is no less refined, exquisite emotional contours of No. 7 in D minor anticipates Fauré's ultra-refined late style, as does No. 8 in D flat of the following year, which dissolves music of mounting passion into lapidary phrases of pulsating restraint. All four once for it. Too apt, it and especially music, exist to elevate us as far as possible above everyday existence.

## Artist biographies

**VLADO PERLEMUTER** (piano)
Vlado Perlemuter (1904-2002) was born in Lithuania into an intellectual and musical Polish-Jewish family. The family moved to Paris when he was four and he began piano lessons at the age of eight. Perlemuter studied with Alfred Cortot at the Paris Conservatoire, won the top prize and eventually became a legendary teacher there. He also had strong ties with England – he first visited in the early 1930s, and made his debut at Wigmore Hall later that decade. At various times he taught at the Yehudi Menuhin and Dartington music schools, and gave masterclasses around the UK. His escape to Switzerland with his wife, during World War II, after being on a Gestapo 'hit list', is worthy of the storyline of the musical *The Sound of Music*.

Never one for self-promotion, his reputation was overshadowed for much of his career. His exquisite playing of works by Chopin, and particularly of Fauré and Ravel, both of whom he knew personally (studying for a while under Ravel in 1927), brought him a wide circle of admirers. He recorded for Nimbus records and made many BBC recordings, particularly of Chopin's works but also of Fauré and Ravel. Perlemuter continued to record and perform until just before his 90th birthday, and taught until he was 96.
