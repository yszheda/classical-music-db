## SYNOPSIS

**ORFEO ED EURIDICE**

**Act I: The tomb of Euridice in a secluded grove.**

[2] Nymphs and shepherds have gathered around the tomb and join in a somber lament, into which Orfeo's grief-stricken voice calls out "Euridice!", the name of his young wife who has suddenly died from the bite of an adder. [3] Inconsolable and made unhappy by their sad dirge, Orfeo asks his friends to render their last respects by [4] (Pantomime) strewing their garlands of flowers, with which they encircle the grave. [5] As Orfeo remains behind as the Nymphs and shepherds sing one last appeal to Euridice to hear the sounds of mourning that surround her urn, and then depart [6] (Ritornello) to the sorrowful strains of the orchestra.

[7] Despite the futility of sighing, mourning and weeps, to invoke a response from that "beloved shade", [8] Orfeo calls out yet again to Euridice, telling her that though he beseeches the gods to restore her, his tears are scattered by the winds, and [9] to his grief, the goddesses are so stony hearted that [10] even as he shows her to know her name, he and the valleys resound with it. [11] only the murmuring brook is touched by his tears, and answers.

[12] Orfeo cries out in repudiation of the cruel gods of the underworld, whose relentless hush has never been swayed by youth or beauty, and declares he will enter their dark realm and reclaim his wife. [13] the god of Love, Amore, appears and tells Orfeo that love has taken pity on him. [14] the magically sweet and placating melodies that emerge from Orfeo playing his lyre will tame the furies who guard the entrance to the netherworld. However, it is forbidden that he cast even one look upon his wife, nor can he explain his seeming indifference so that, until they have re-crossed the Styx back into the land of the living. [15] Amore gently encourages Orfeo, reminding him of the conditions set upon him, and the relative shortness of his ordeal.

Amore vanishes and [16] Orfeo muses on what he has heard, his heart trembling at the suffering he must inflict on Euridice with his forbearance, which she is certain to misunderstand. Nonetheless, he resolves to go forth, and hurries off [17] amidst thunder and lightning.

[18] Broadcast commentary.

**Act II, Scene 1: Entrance to underworld, spewing smoke and flames.**

[19] (Orchestral passage) Dance of the Furies and Spirits.

[20] The sound of Orfeo's lyre interrupts the dance, and the Furies demand to know who dares set foot where only ancient heroes have entered. [21] (Orchestral passage) [22] The Furies add terrifying threats as their dance redoubles in dark intensity; [23] Orfeo plays his enchanting lyre, while seeking their compassion for his desperate grief. The Furies resist throughout with bans of "No!", but they begin to soften, increasingly bewildered by this sad youth. [25] Orfeo sings that he carries his own hell within him, not unlike theirs, and [26] that the Furies are more cruel than he is. [27] "Tyrants!" he says, if they only knew for a moment what it was to die of love. [28] Completely overcome, the furies chant while the black-hinged gates open letting Orfeo pass. [29] and they resume their wild, sinister dance.

[30] Broadcast commentary.

**Act II, Scene 2: The Elysian Fields.**

[31] (Ballet) Dance of the Blessed Spirits. [32] (Orchestral passage)

[33] Another group of Blessed Spirits enter with Euridice, who sings of the tranquility of spirit she enjoys in this land of repose. [34] (Postlude) Euridice and the Blessed Spirits slowly leave. [35] Orchestral passage describing the natural sounds of the Elysian Fields, into which Orfeo enters from the underworld. [36] Orfeo is wonderstruck with the purity of the sky, the singing of birds and the whispering breezes, all inviting a rest and happiness he cannot feel without Euridice. [37] The Blessed Spirits return and welcome him with the promise that Euridice will love him once more. [38] Ballet of the Blessed Spirits. [39] Orfeo is enrapt with anticipation. The Spirits lead Euridice to him. [40] foreseeing another Elissium to come when she is reunited with her husband, Orfeo takes her hand, carefully averting his eyes, and leads her away.

**Act III, Scene 1: A winding path out of the infernal regions.**

[C.D.2] [1] Orfeo hurries Euridice through dark passages, telling her of his love with tender words and of the merciful intercession of the gods. Overjoyed, Euridice is reassured by his endearments only briefly before she becomes doubtful. She begins to question him. Why will he not look at or embrace her? Orfeo's excruciating test of obedience quickly comes to pass: torn desparately, Orfeo pleads for her trust, while remaining steadfast to his secret promise. Euridice reproaches him bitterly for what seems to be his cruel indifference, for his refusal to explain himself, and she will go no further. They separate some distance from each other, and Euridice feels a foreboding faintness that fills her with terror. [2] Euridice laments her fate to modern from death to such overpowering grief. [3] She sings a poignant duet. [4] Euridice repeats her plaint, [5] and feels that she is about to die. All recrimination departs her tone as she sings a brief and loving farewell to Orfeo, who is irresistibly drawn into one last look - she falls lifeless. Horrified by what he has done, and plunged into new depths of anguish, [6] he cannot imagine living without her. [7] There is no longer help to be had from man nor can heaven: [8] he resolves to kill himself as the only means to reunite with her.

**Scene 2: Temple of Love.**

[9] Amore suddenly appears and restrains him, saying that his constancy has been proven. Amore brings Euridice back to life. [10] and wishes them both joy. [11] The trio sings an ode to the power of love.

**Scene 3: Temple of Love.**

[12] Orchestral Introduction. [13] (Baller) Orfeo, Euridice, Amore and a multitude of shepherds and shepherdesses celebrate Euridice's return with a dance. [14] All join in a chorus calling for the triumph of Love.

## RECORDING NOTES

This 20 January 1940 broadcast of Orfeo ed Euridice derives from the NBC transcription discs, which were transferred to tape in the 1950s, then were varispeed in pitch, which they endeavored to correct. The revival of Orfeo at the Met occurred on 20 November 1938 with Bodanzky conducting. Repetitions of this production occurred in 1938 and 1939; Leinsdorf conducted in 1940. Thereafter there were two performances 23 March 1941 and 26 November 1941 conducted by Bruno Walter who restored the Overture and a different finale.

Our producer, Jonathan Woons, went to some considerable trouble trying to find a libretto and musical score that matched this performance. He enlisted the expertise of Professor Hugh MacDonald, who gave us a detailed analysis of the three standard versions Leinsdorf used together for this 1940 broadcast. We would like to extend our gratitude to Professor MacDonald for his interview work, from which we list below the three sources used:

1. Italian version done in Vienna in 1762
2. French version done in Paris in 1774
3. Bellini's version done in Paris in 1859

By studying numerous librettos from commercially released record albums, we have managed to identify the Italian words at each access point, with the exception (see CD-1, track 33). For this line we have provided a track identification from a libretto that appears to represent the sentiment expressed by Euridice in her opening words of the opera (Act II, Scene 2).

The interview with Thorborg was found on a tape received from the Aldrich Memorial Sound Library but neither its date nor the name of the interviewer was noted.

## KERSTIN THORBORG BIOGRAPHY

**KERSTIN THORBORG**, one of the greatest Wagnerian contraltos, was born in Vienn. North Sweden in 1896. Thorborg made her debut at the Stockholm Opera in 1924 as Ortrud (Lohengrin). For the next few years she remained there, singing important contralto roles. After four years of opera and concert engagements in Sweden, she sang at the Prague National Theater, and later at the Berlin State Opera. Following a season at the Teatro Colón in Buenos Aires, she was engaged by the Vienna State Opera. There she achieved her greatest success up to that time, and was numbered by many Viennese critics among the great contraltos in Europe.

In the spring of 1936, she made her first appearance at Covent Garden in the Ring dramas. She was so successful that she was called to the Metropolitan Opera House. As early as 1932 she had been invited to join the Metropolitan, but her numerous engagements throughout Europe made a visit to New York impossible at the time. Her first appearance took place on 21 December 1936, in the role of Fricka in Die Walküre. "Miss Thorborg," wrote one New York critic, "is a woman of regal and distinguished beauty, stately in bearing, slender, tall and straight. She knows the significance of what she is called to do. She is clearly an actress of intelligence and skill and power. She fills and holds the eye. The voice is not, or did not seem last night, of even beauty and expressiveness throughout its range. But of effectiveness in the embodiment of character there was little question."

At the Metropolitan, Thorborg followed her successful debut with memorable performances in other Wagnerian contralto roles and in Orfeo of Euridice and Elektra. In all, she sang at the Met for 13 seasons in 20 roles, giving 350 performances. Her vocal art is preserved in 10 broadcast recordings. These include her complete performances of Erda (Siegfried) and in Guild's Dream Rheingold.

## JARMILA NOVOTNA BIOGRAPHY

**JARMILA NOVOTNA** was charming, beautiful, and talented - reminiscent in her elegance and loveliness to Lisa Della Casa. She was born 23 September 1907 in Prague, Czechoslovakia. Her memories of childhood were happy ones, especially those connected with singing, which she loved above all other. Eventually, as her voice developed, she was accepted as a student by Emmy Destinn. She made an early debut at 16 years of age as Rusalka in Barbe in Lida in Traviata, at the Prague National Theater. By 1926 she was a regular member of the opera company and sang a variety of roles including Mimì and Nunù in Madama Butterfly and sang a Lady (Rigoletto with Lauri-Volpi). In 1928 she appeared at the Vienna State Opera and then was back in New York for Adina in L'elisir d'amore with Tito Schipa. By the time she was 22 she had sung opposite such important bass-baritones as Feodor Chaliapin, appearing at the Berlin Staatsoper.

During the early 30's Novotna was cherished for her singing of Cherubino in Nozze di Figaro and in 1933 for Butterfly (with Richard Tauber). Her career waxed luminous when she sang in the world premiere of Lehar's Giuditta with Richard Tauber. The composer conducted the gala event on 20 January 1934. In the later part of the 1930s Novotna excelled in the roles of Chemirino and the Countess in Nozze di Figaro; she was especially happy to take part in a performance in which Elisabeth Schumann sang Susanna alternating with Adele Kern.

Later she came to sing Euridice in Orfeo (with Thorborg) under the direction of Bruno Walter, a role she repeated with Thorborg and Walter at the Met in 1941. Two events ahead of her career change took place in 1936; a change in her personal life was singing under Toscanini's direction in The Magic Flute at Salzburg 1936. She stated that this experience was the center of what musicianship she was able to cultivate and the most unforgettable experience of her life in music. It was Toscanini who helped her move to America as the war closed in on Austria and the rest of Europe, and it was he who introduced her to Edward Johnson, the manager of the Metropolitan Opera.

Novotna arrived in the US a month after the war began in Europe and was immediately engaged to sing Butterfly at the San Francisco Opera. Then, she made her Met debut on 5 January 1940 as Mimì opposite Jussi Björling. Novotna's Met career spanned 16 seasons during which she sang 14 roles giving 205 performances. She left the Met 15 January 1956. Performances at the Met included Count Almaviva, Mimì, Orlofsky in Fledermaus, her Mélisande or her Maenka in The Bartered Bride but, to atone for these losses, broadcast transcriptions do exist of her singing many roles closely associated with her fame. These include her Beethoven Ninth (Toscanini), La Damoiselle Elue (Toscanini), Magic Flute (Toscanini - in German, 1937 and two broadcasts with Bruno Walter in English in 1942), Antonia in Tales of Hoffmann (Beckham 1934 / Pelletier 1938), Donna Anna in Don Giovanni (Bruno Walter 1942 / Breisach 1943), Cherubino in Nozze di Figaro (two broadcasts in 1940, another in 1943, and again in 1944, 1947, 1948 and 1950), Euridice in Orfeo 1940, Rosalinde in Die Fledermaus (in English) in 1949 and 1951, Violetta in Traviata in 1941 (debut of Jan Peerce) and her US debut in Butterfly with the San Francisco Opera in 1939.

## ERICH LEINSDORF BIOGRAPHY

**ERICH LEINSDORF** was born in Vienna in 1912. Drawn to music at a very early age, his studies led him to become a conductor at the age of 22. Bruno Walter recommended him to Toscanini as an assistant for the Salzburg Festival performances. He also assisted Bruno Walter there, and at the May Festival in Florence. Greatly impressed, Toscanini recommended him to the Met. Leinsdorf acceded to Bodanzky's position following the old man's death in 1939. Leinsdorf at 28 became principal conductor of all the Wagner works, in which he distinguished himself. Lawrence Gilman, music critic of the New York World Telegram, thought Leinsdorf to be one of the best ever, possessed of a youthful vivacity coupled with a superb sense of the dramatic and musical arc of the works. He conducted for 22 seasons (in eight other works) directing 28 works with 481 performances, 299 of which were at the Old Met. Guild Historical has released a number of the broadcasts Leinsdorf conducted between 1938-1943. These include the 1938 Parsifal, Act II (GHCD 2201), 1940 Die Walküre dream Ring (GHCD 2215/17), 1940 Nieza al Fígaro, Act II (GHCD 2203/05), 1940 Tristan und Isolde (GHCD2226/6/68), with others to come.

## ADDITIONAL NOTES

The praise heaped upon her performances during the 1940s. She was much loved as an artist yet, whatever the plaudits, she maintained a quiet grace; her innately aristocratic tone was completely internal. Novotna had no airs, and was never the "prima donna". She made few records, thus was not widely known outside the opera houses in which she sang. Nevertheless, she made her mark in operatic history, as her singing in her Met broadcasts amply demonstrates.

**MARITA FARELL**, soprano, made her Met debut 3 December 1937 as the Forest Bird in Siegfried in 1938, then sang Sophie in Der Rosenkavalier the following week. She sang for 10 seasons, leaving the company 19 May 1947. She sang 23 roles and made 230 appearances with the company. Her performances as Barbarina (Nozze di Figaro), Amore (Orfeo ed Euridice), Marzelline (Fidelio), and Nedda (Pagliacci) have been preserved on broadcast recordings.
