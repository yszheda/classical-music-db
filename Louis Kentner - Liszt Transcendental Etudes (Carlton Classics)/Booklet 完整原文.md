### Liner Notes

**Franz Liszt (1811-1886)**
*Études d'exécution transcendants*

Ferencz (Franz) Liszt was born at Raiding in Hungary , and died at Bayreuth. Arguably the most famous pianist/composer/arranger ever, his piano compositions alone guarantee him a place in musical history.

His career was one of unqualified success from his first piano recital at the age of 9. A year later he travelled to Vienna for lessons with Salieri and Czerny, making his Paris debut in 1823 and playing in London in 1824. For many years he settled in Paris where he was welcomed into society, meeting glitterati like Berlioz and Chopin, and living openly with the beautiful Countess Marie d'Agoult, who bore him three children. In the 1840s he returned to Vienna and London for more recitals and embarked on long concert tours, including one to Russia. Liszt later had a passionate liaison with Princess Carolyne Sayn-Wittgenstein and went on to enjoy a second career at Weimar, conducting the "moderns" Berlioz and Wagner. From 1860 onwards he lived in Rome at the Villa d'Este, taking holy orders and composing a quantity of religious music. He had many piano students in his last years and made a final jubilee tour to mark his 75th birthday.

The 12 *Transcendental Studies* originally date from 1826 when Liszt was only 15. Modelled on Czerny they were announced as *Study in 48 exercises in all the major and minor keys* - young Franz taking a leaf out of Bach's book of the masterly "48". The following year a further 24 *Grandes Études* were promised, though in the event only twelve actually materialised. Berlioz however was impressed: *"Unfortunately one cannot hope to hear music of this kind often - Liszt created it for himself and no one else in the world should flatter himself that he could even begin to perform it."*

The *Transcendental Studies* were indeed phenomenally difficult, but Liszt was not satisfied. After fourteen years a revised version appeared which solved a number of the problems inherent in the earlier one, while adding a more mature perspective. Besides, Liszt had heard the violin playing of Paganini, widely accepted as being possessed of the devil, and was now intent on making the same brilliant splash on the pianoforte.

The opening study *Preludio*, marked *Presto*, is in the nature of a "warming-up" exercise, or "running the hands over the keys".

The Liszt scholar Humphrey Searle gives his impressions of the remaining numbers:

**No.2:** *Study in A minor, Molto vivace*, subtitled "a capriccio". Possibly a reflection of Paganini's technique in terms of the piano - audacious, violent and brilliant in turn.

**No.3:** *Paysage*, *Poco adagio*. A calm evocation of landscape expressed with great originality and warmth. The syncopations of the middle section seem to echo Brahms, although he was only 4 years old when this piece was written!

**No.4:** *Mazeppa*, *Allegro*, was published separately in 1847 with a slightly altered ending. It is based on the story of the Polish nobleman Ivan Mazeppa, 1644-1709, made famous by Byron and Victor Hugo, also by Pushkin in his drama *Pultava*. Mazeppa was a page in the court of King John Casimir of Poland and became involved with the wife of a nobleman. For this he was punished by being tied naked to a wild horse which was then set loose, running to the Ukraine where it fell exhausted. Mazeppa was rescued by the Cossacks and became their Hetman. At the end of the piece Liszt quotes Hugo's words "He falls at last...and arises King!" The piece starts with the wild ride and climaxes with the fall, where there is a brief recitative followed by triumphant closing chords.

**No.5:** *Feux follets* (Will o' the Wisps), *Allegretto*, is delicate and charming. It rarely rises above piano and contains pages of intricate fingerwork demanding subtle gradations of tone.

**No.6:** *Vision*, *Lento*. This is laid out on a broad scale, with pesante chords surrounded by arpeggios. Powerful and dramatic, it rises to an impressive climax.

**No.7:** *Eroica*, *Allegro*. The introduction is derived from an early Liszt Impromptu on themes by Rossini and Spontini. Deeply patriotic, it and can be likened to the Heroic March in the Hungarian Style *Funérailles* and the symphonic poem *Héroïde funèbre*.

**No.8:** *Wilde Jagd* (Wild Hunt), *Presto furioso*. A typical nocturnal hunt, a feature of German Romantic music from Weber onwards. The triumphant ending is in the major key.

**No.9:** *Ricordanza (Remembrance)*. *Andantino, improvisato*. A nostalgic and romantic piece. "Like a packet of yellowed love letters" as Busoni said. The main theme is a typical cantilena of the Italian operatic type which Chopin, Field, Bellini and others all adored.

**No.10:** *Study in F minor, Allegro agitato molto*. This is a violent, savage piece.

**No.11:** *Harmonies du Soir* (Evening Harmonies), *Andantino*. Another calm, lovely piece conjuring up the atmosphere of a peaceful evening with distant echoes of bells. It contains an agitated middle section.

**No.12:** *Chasse-neige* (Snow-whirls), *Andante con moto*. An impressively monolithic piece that suggests a landscape being slowly and relentlessly covered with snow. Perhaps the most impressive of the entire set.

The nocturne, *En rêve*, a late piano piece, was composed in 1885, a year or so before the composer's death. Quietly contemplative, it is just two minutes long, without any of the atonal tendencies apparent in the music of Liszt's last years. It has two stanzas, and ends in a whisper.
