# Frances Alda — A Personal Remembrance

It was at a morning musicale, in the early twenties, that I first heard Frances Alda. When my voice teacher gave me the invitation card, she said: "Listen to Alda carefully. There is a lot to learn from her exceptional singing." My curiosity aroused, I could hardly wait for this highly talented soprano to arrive. Alda finally came and was greeted by a burst of applause. She walked to the platform, motioned to the accompanist, and began to sing a group of concert songs she had chosen for the occasion. As the recital progressed, I became more and more aware that there was indeed a lot to be learned from the soprano's vocal technique. I also realized that hearing her sing just once would not contribute much to my vocal education. It took me a while, but I did start listening to Alda often, with the help of her wonderful recordings, which I began to acquire upon receiving a Victrola as a gift.

Gradually I completed my collection of Alda's Victor discs, and it was while listening to some of them, way back in 1951, that the idea struck me to get in touch with the diva. I offered to write an article about her, together with a discography of her recordings. Meaning to have Madame (that's the way she wanted to be addressed) examine the discography before the interview, I prepared a layout based on the listing of her records kindly supplied to me by the Victor Company, then went on to phone her at "Casa Mia," in Great Neck, Long Island. Alda herself answered and, after learning the purpose of my call, she promptly invited me to come on a day we agreed upon, and even added that her car would be there to pick me up at the station. Needless to say, I was overjoyed at the thought of meeting the great exponent of vocal art whom I had boundlessly admired since the fateful musicale held some thirty years back.

The first thing Alda did after I came was to look through the discography of her records, which interested her immensely. While she was thus occupied, I looked out of the large picture window at her side. How beautiful: the veranda with wrought-iron furniture, the private beach, the dock with boats, and Manhasset Bay as far as the eye could reach. I forgot the interview for a moment and sighed: "What a way to live."

Satisfied with the discography, Alda took me to the music room to play her two unpublished recordings, which I had never heard before, including one with Caruso. As she tried to start the record player, something went wrong. "It won't work," she muttered, visibly annoyed. "Get a repairman," I suggested. "You are famous; he will come right away." She complied and called up a number. "This is Madame Frances Alda," she said in her operatic chest tones, and proceeded to explain the problem. Apparently the man did not know of her celebrity status and refused to come on such short notice. Alda turned to me saying: "That's how famous I am," not without a tinge of resentment in her voice. "No matter, we'll listen to those records next time you come," she concluded with a smile. "Alda wants me to come again," I thought. "That sounds promising."

Here I must say that in spite of her volcanic disposition, Alda was all kindness when we were together. She lost her temper only once, after telling me about Elizabeth Parkina, whom she had known, and of her fine singing. Obviously under the impression that I knew nothing concerning the obscure soprano, she blew up when I expressed my opinion with regard to her friend's vocal excellence: "How can you speak of Parkina if you have never heard her sing!" she thundered. "Not in person," I retorted, "but on records she sounds good to me." Madame calmed down, and I never incurred her ire again.

After our fiasco in the records department, I was hoping we could go on with the interview. But no, our next stop was the grand salon, where a baseball game was shown on television. Alda was a baseball fan! She sat down on a settee in front of the TV set and motioned that I sit next to her. Suddenly one of the players hit a homer and she began to applaud enthusiastically. Seeing my indifference, she started explaining what was happening at that moment on the screen. As I still did not appear interested, she gave up on me in disgust. "You and your singing! You think that's art?" she asked. "This is art! Look, look at what he is doing!" I looked, and all I saw was a fellow running around the bases, while a representative of a most beautiful art, sitting next to me, was beside herself because he made a home run!

At this time, I gently reminded Alda that it was getting late. "Of course, the interview," she said; "come with me," and we went to Alda's queenly boudoir, where photos of her family were displayed at the entrance to the room. There, in front of these mementos, Madame began to reminisce.

Frances Alda was born Frances Jeanne Davies on 31 May 1883, at Christ Church, New Zealand, of a British father and Uruguayan mother. Upon the death of her mother when the child was four, the maternal grandparents, who lived in St. Kilda, Australia, took care of her until she became twelve. Then Frances was sent to a boarding school, which she left when sixteen because her grandmother died. A point worth mentioning is that the girl studied the piano and the violin while growing up, which explains Alda's proficiency in music when she became a singer.

When eighteen, with five thousand pounds inherited from her mother in her purse, Frances traveled to Great Britain. As it had been discovered that she had a voice, the budding soprano was determined to seek a singing career. When in London, she visited the famous actress Cora Brown Potter, whom she had met in Australia. The lady welcomed her warmly and took her to the Derby, where she presented her young friend to Edward VII and Queen Alexandra. The King requested that Frances sing at a regimental benefit; she obeyed and received a beautiful silver box for her performance. At a later date, Edward VII decided to teach Frances golf. She hit the ball together with a large piece of turf, and broke the niblick. "Damn it to hell!" she blurted out, forgetting the amused King's presence.

When the time came for the aspiring soprano to learn vocal technique, she went to Paris to audition for Mathilde Marchesi, a voice teacher whose fame reached far and wide. Maman Marchesi listened to her sing and liked what she heard. For the next ten months, the young student remained under the complete charge of the capable pedagogue, who promptly gave her a new name—Alda. Aside from singing, Alda also studied French and Italian, and took lessons in mise en scène from Victor Capoul. On 15 April 1904, she made her initial debut in Paris as Manon, at the Opéra-Comique. Massenet himself had taught her the role.

During the next four years, Alda's life was filled with travel and appearances, some of major importance. From the Opéra-Comique she went on to La Monnaie in Brussels. Then it was London's Covent Garden, where she sang in *Rigoletto* with Caruso and Battistini, and in *Faust*, also with Caruso. After that she visited Parma to do *Rigoletto* with Bonci and, again, Battistini. These were the only performances from those years that she cared to mention.

In Milan, in 1907, Alda first met Gatti-Casazza and Toscanini. The two gentlemen came to pay their respects to Mademoiselle Alda and, incidentally, offer her the role of *Louise* in the La Scala première of Charpentier's opera, and also the role of Margherita in Boito's *Mefistofele*, opposite Chaliapin. Alda went back to Paris to study *Louise* with Marchesi, and early in 1908 began the rehearsals with Toscanini. After the première, which was Alda's debut at La Scala, the immediate result was the offer, and acceptance, of a contract with the Metropolitan Opera for the 1908-09 season.

Alda made her Metropolitan debut on 7 December 1908, in *Rigoletto*, with Homer, Caruso, Amato, and Didur as her partners. From then on she participated in many performances of various operas until 28 December 1929, when she bade adieu to the Metropolitan in *Manon Lescaut*, with Gigli and de Luca at her side. In the spring of 1910, she married the General Director of the Metropolitan, Giulio Gatti-Casazza, thus becoming the Met's First Lady in addition to being one of its brightest stars. The marriage ended in divorce in 1929, when Alda's career also came to its end. With this I'll close my thumbnail sketch of Alda's 21-year stay at the Metropolitan because in this CD collection our interest lies mostly in the diva's voice and her recordings, as well as in herself—a most fascinating woman and artist!

About her voice, I can only repeat what I wrote over forty years ago:

> Alda's voice was a pure lyric soprano of enchanting loveliness, flawlessly clear, and full like an open bloom. Among the many attributes that made her singing the sheer joy that it was, were the firm suppleness of her tone, its chaste legato and silky melodic line, and one of the most entrancing trills ever to flow from the human throat. And although impeccably correct, Alda's tone was devoid of any cold, mechanical perfection—it was warm and caressing, and it tugged at your heart strings.

A few days after I left her, Alda phoned: "Come, the record player is working. I want you to hear those unpublished recordings of mine." I went in a hurry, and Alda played them for me, in addition to some published ones that she particularly enjoyed. Hearing the discs was quite interesting, but our conversation while we were listening was probably even more so.

First she put on her Suzel's lament from *L'Amico Fritz*—a lovely recording that should definitely have been released. Alda's singing in it is on par with her best records, which made poor Suzel's rejection by the Victor engineers even more incomprehensible.

Then came the pièce de résistance—the *Carmen* Act I Micaela-Don José duet, sung by Alda and Caruso! Although I did not particularly care for their singing, nor for the accelerated tempi and the cuts made to fit the piece on a single side of a 12-inch 78 rpm disc, I knew very well that from an opera lover's standpoint the record was a real treasure. "Was it you or Caruso who prevented the release of the *Carmen* duet?" I asked. "Oh no, that was not my idea," Alda replied, "it was Caruso who complained: 'Ho cantato come un cane' [I sang like a dog]." Not waiting to find out what effect Caruso's poor appraisal of his own singing had upon me, Alda added: "Gatti called Caruso came, and Toscanini called him porco."

Yes, Alda was frank to a fault, and to think that she knew innumerable stories and anecdotes of a similar nature concerning operatic personalities, which she would tell when the occasion arose, or when the spirit moved her. A book containing those items would certainly be enlightening. She did not spare herself either. Among her favorite recordings were two Maori songs, melodies of the native people of New Zealand. As she was playing one of them, I winced at some flatting in her singing. "I had a hard time the night before," she said, winking meaningfully. We both laughed.

Alda considered the *Loreley* recording her best, with *Mefistofele* as a second choice. When I commented on the difficulties encountered in *Loreley* and her glorious rendition of the aria, she credited Arturo Toscanini for all her artistic achievements: "He worked with me by the hour, sat by the hour teaching me." She was in the habit of sending her chauffeur to drive the Maestro over to "Casa Mia" once a week. There, after a dinner for two, consisting of the dishes he liked best (absolutely no fish!), they would retire to the music room, where Toscanini, at the antique spinet, and Alda would "make music" as only they could.

Regarding my taste in Alda's recordings, I love them all—as long as I can hear her sing, I am happy. I may not care much for the pop songs she was persuaded to record, some at the first reading, but with me it's the sound of her voice that counts more than the piece she is singing.

While still on the subject of records: The *Carmen* duet with Caruso was ultimately released by RCA (now BMG) on LP and on CD, as well as by Pearl on CD. And the selection from *L'Amico Fritz*, included in this present CD collection, was first issued on LP by EJS, a small record publishing firm, no longer in existence.

After the enjoyable time we had together, Alda and I parted the best of friends. She and her second husband, Ray Vir Den, an advertising executive, were quite impressed with my article and discography, which appeared in *The Record Collector* of England. I was planning on writing more about Madame after her return from her trip to Italy, this time in *Hobbies*, an American publication, when the sad news came: Alda died of a cerebral hemorrhage in Venice, on 18 September 1952. It was, no doubt, her ebullient nature that killed this vital, over-emotional woman.

Frances Alda was a great prima donna in her time, and we are privileged indeed to have her sing for us now through her priceless recordings.

**AIDA FAVIA-ARTSAY**
