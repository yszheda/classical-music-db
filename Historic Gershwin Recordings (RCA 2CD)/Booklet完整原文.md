## Liner Notes

### H I S T O R I C
### G E R S H W I N
### R E C O R D I N G S

George Gershwin did not suffer from a dearth of recordings-piano rolls, single-sided, thick Edison discs and conventional 78rpm records sold wildly, even at the start of his career. As early as 1920, the star entertainer Al Jolson recorded "Swanee," which made the twenty-one-year-old Gershwin and his collaborator Irving Caesar rich young men and led to Gershwin's meteoric Broadway ascent.

But then one snowy afternoon in February 1924, another George Gershwin strode upon the American musical scene and made history. To the surprise of his friends and admirers, and to the dismay of his academic critics, the composer of such tunes as "Swanee," "I'll Build a Stairway to Paradise" and "Do It Again" wrote a piece for jazz orchestra and piano with the title of *Rhapsody in Blue*.

What was this Tin Pan Alley upstart doing in Aeolian (and, soon after, Carnegie) Hall? Making history, that's what. And no one was more surprised than Gershwin himself. On January 3, 1924, a newspaper article announced that the popular bandleader Paul Whiteman was preparing a concert he called "An Experiment in Modern Music," scheduled for February 12, which he claimed would answer the question, "What is American music?" At the time Gershwin was reading this article, he was busy working on *Sweet Little Devil*, a Broadway musical due to open at the Astor Theater on January 21. He read on, appalled: "George Gershwin is at work on a jazz concerto..."

News, indeed.

Gershwin called Whiteman that very day, explaining the impossibility of it all with the impending show, and that composing a formally structured concerto was unthinkable. But Whiteman, who had been deeply impressed by Gershwin's one-act opera *Blue Monday* two years before, was certain he could compose something for the concert. Gershwin was intrigued, of course. He might come up with something freer, like a rhapsody–*An American Rhapsody*? Whiteman liked that idea and before the conversation ended, they decided that because of limited time, and the unorthodox instrumentation of Whiteman's band, Whiteman's arranger Ferde Grofé would do the orchestration under Gershwin's guidance.

Ira Gershwin suggested the work's final title, inspired by an exhibition of the paintings of James McNeill Whistler. The paintings had such titles as "Arrangement in Black and Gray" (better known as "Whistler's Mother"), "Nocturne in Blue and Silver" and "Symphony in White and Blue." Why not, Ira asked, a "Rhapsody in Blue?"

Why not? On January 7, 1924, Gershwin began writing the rhapsody in two-piano form with several instrumental indications for Grofé. The startling opening glissando is marked "Slowly" with cl. (for clarinet) at the beginning of the manuscript. Grofé recalled that he haunted the Gershwin apartment on 110th Street daily, getting a page or a few pages at a time. He finished his part of the job on February 2, in time for a few rehearsals. On the twelfth, a new chapter in American music began.

Whiteman's "experiment" failed to define American music–but it did introduce a very youthful, original voice in American music. And a popular one, alas; you can't, grumped many of the stuffy critics at the time, be popular and serious at the same time. They were quite wrong, as Gershwin went on to prove.

Thanks to the technology of the time, *Rhapsody in Blue* was recorded only four months after its sensational premiere, on June 10, 1924. The recording was made before the development of the microphone; Gershwin and the Whiteman band were picked up acoustically by a giant horn. The band included the members who performed at the February concert, among them clarinetist Ross Gorman. The recording itself was a milestone, since at the time recordings of "serious" American music were rare. The work was abridged to enable it to fit on both sides of a single twelve-inch record.

Three years later, with the introduction of the electric microphone, Gershwin and the Whiteman band gathered in the New York studio of the Victor Talking Machine Company to record the rhapsody again, spurred by the best-selling popularity of the acoustic disc.

There are three conspicuous absentees from the second recording. Ross Gorman had left the Whiteman band; so had Ferde Grofé, to form his own band and to spend more time on his own compositions. And Paul Whiteman? Yes, there is no Paul Whiteman. During the prerecording rehearsal Whiteman and Gershwin disagreed on Whiteman's tendency to speed up the work (his philosophy may have been the faster the jazzier). Their discussion reached a high pitch and Whiteman stalked out of the studio. After he had composed himself and returned, he was startled to find the session under way with Nathaniel Shilkret, Victor's Music Director and also Gershwin's old friend, leading the band. Whiteman left again and the recording continued without him, although with his band.

That was the beginning. Today, *Rhapsody in Blue* is probably the most widely performed American work for piano and orchestra, and may be the most frequently recorded. One of the outstanding complete recorded performances, which dates from the early long-playing era, was by Gershwin's younger friend and admirer Morton Gould. In 1955, Gould assembled his own orchestra to record a set called "The Serious Gershwin," which he conducted from the keyboard. Gould had begun in music as a prodigy. His first professional encounter with Gershwin occurred in the summer of 1935, when he was the pianist for a rehearsal of *Porgy and Bess*, with Gershwin conducting. The composer and some of the cast were testing Gershwin's orchestrations a month or so before the opera was complete. Gould was twenty-one at the time.

Gould's admiration for Gershwin was mutual. When Gershwin's good friend Kay Swift asked him to orchestrate a ballet she had written for George Balanchine, he suggested Gould instead. And when soprano Eva Gauthier hoped to repeat a sensational recital she and Gershwin had performed in 1923, with Gauthier singing works by composers ranging from Purcell to Bartók to Gershwin, Kern and Berlin, Gershwin again suggested Morton Gould as his musical stand-in.

Gould had complete mastery of the Gershwin idiom and, in "The Serious Gershwin," he included another side of the composer with the *Preludes for Piano*, dating from 1927. The preludes are among Gershwin's most polished compositions, a suite for piano with a nocturne enclosed by two dances, "glowing little vignettes of New York life," in the opinion of one critic. Gershwin referred to the *Prelude No. 2* as "a sort of blue lullaby."

Gershwin triumphantly returned to Carnegie Hall in December 1928, with his tone poem for orchestra, "really a rhapsodic ballet," which he called *An American in Paris*. Almost immediately after the premiere, Nat Shilkret conducted its first radio performance and, on February 4, 1929, recorded it for Victor. Gershwin did not provide himself with a piano part, but Shilkret remedied that lack by drafting him to play the solo celesta part in the score. Another highlight: the recording includes the taxi horns that Gershwin had brought home from Paris to add to the Parisian atmosphere of the work, in which he portrays "the impressions of an American visitor in Paris as he strolls about the city [and] listens to the street noises..." Shilkret and company capture Gershwin's intent in one of the most vital and brilliant recordings of this composition.

The same may be said for the later recording of *An American in Paris* led by Leonard Bernstein in 1949. Then at the beginning of his trail-blazing career as a conductor, Bernstein was already an adventurous maestro who conducted pioneering recordings of such American composers as Gershwin, Copland, Blitzstein, and himself, as well as of Stravinsky, Milhaud and Ravel. Many of these were first recordings, and all are treasures of Bernstein's early years.

*Porgy and Bess* is Gershwin's towering achievement. In what he called a "folk opera," he drew upon his finely honed creative resources writing for orchestra, piano, voice and groups of voices. The opera was based on DuBose Heyward's novel *Porgy*, drawn from the author's experiences as a "cotton checker" on the docks in Charleston, South Carolina, before he began writing poetry and studying local folklore. His "Porgy" was based on an actual person, "Goat Sammy," a beggar who had lost the use of his legs and made his rounds in a cart drawn by a goat.

After a half dozen years of postponements–in which Heyward's wife Dorothy had secretly converted the novel into a play, Gershwin was involved in his radio show and a couple of musicals, Heyward went to Hollywood to write a screenplay–Gershwin and Heyward finally set about their collaboration in earnest.

In late December 1933, Gershwin began at the beginning and wrote "Summertime," the first song in the opera. He spent eleven months composing, working closely with Heyward and, later, with his brother Ira (who came to the project after the first act was finished). An additional nine months were devoted to the opera's orchestration; on the final page Gershwin wrote, "Finished, August 23, 1935." Three days later rehearsals began.

The premiere was held in Boston on September 30 (four days after Gershwin's 37th birthday), and was greeted with great acclaim. But there was a hitch. The producers felt that the production was overlong, and insisted that about fifteen minutes must be cut before the opera opened in New York. Good showman that he was, Gershwin reluctantly agreed.

When *Porgy and Bess* premiered at the Alvin Theater on October 10, 1935, reviews were mixed, with some critics intimating that it was neither a musical nor an opera. It ran for a money-losing 124 performances, not bad for an opera but financially deadly for a musical. After a brief tour *Porgy and Bess* closed and was virtually forgotten for years. But the songs never languished.

Only four days after the premiere, recordings were made of several of the songs, including "The Buzzard Song", which had been eliminated in Boston. Curiously, while the records employed conductor Alexander Smallens and the Eva Jessye Choir, both from the stage production, the opera's two leads, Todd Duncan and Anne Brown, were replaced by the better known Metropolitan Opera stars, Lawrence Tibbett and Helen Jepson. It should be mentioned that an excited George Gershwin was present in the studio during the recording session, assuring an authentic presentation of his work. Though not recognized in its time, *Porgy and Bess* is now regarded as the finest American opera.

A note on the division of labor: Ira Gershwin did not write the words to "Summertime;" in fact, he contributed nothing to Act I because he was working with Harold Arlen and E.Y. Harburg on completing *Life Begins at 8:40*, a musical revue. When Ira joined the *Porgy* team, his brother and Heyward had moved on to Act II, in which "Bess, You Is My Woman Now" is heard. It is Ira's lyric, but in the published music it is attributed to him and DuBose Heyward. Ira insisted on this because he had taken the idea and the title from a line in the libretto. When he suggested they do the same with "It Ain't Necessarily So," Heyward demurred saying, "Ira, no one would believe it."

The bulk of the music for *Porgy and Bess* was written to the sections of the libretto and lyrics that Heyward mailed to Gershwin in Manhattan. "I Got Plenty o' Nuttin'" was the only lyric written to a completed melody. One day, during a rare Heyward visit to New York, all three decided a lively song was needed early in the first scene of Act II. Gershwin tried out a tune on the piano and someone said, "I got plenty o' nothin'," to which Ira added, "And nothin's plenty for me." The words fit perfectly. Happily, Heyward left the apartment with a completed melody and the first two lines. He insisted that Ira share credit for this lyric.

Theirs was a remarkable, creative collaboration–no ego friction, only respect and appreciation for one another's work. Of course, George Gershwin, the youngest of the trio, led the way with full approval of his librettist and lyricists. From the beginning he had shaped what he called "a labor of love," producing a masterpiece.

Arthur Fiedler, a long-time Gershwin champion, and the Boston Pops contribute a musical lollipop to this collection: "Strike Up the Band," the title song of two Gershwin musicals. The first failed in 1927; the second was a great success when it was revised and produced in 1930. The tune is a lively satire on military marches.

Selections from Morton Gould's 1955 long-playing records of Gershwin's music close this set, including Gould's own suite from *Porgy and Bess*, and "Jazzbo Brown," the exciting solo piano music that originally opened the opera, leading into "Summertime." This piece had been cut in the Boston premiere. It is actually Gershwin's final composition for piano, and Morton Gould recorded it for the first time in his musical tribute to Gershwin.

—**EDWARD JABLONSKI**
