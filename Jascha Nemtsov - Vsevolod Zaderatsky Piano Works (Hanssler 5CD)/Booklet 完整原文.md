## Programme Notes (German Version)

### "Ich bin längst tot..."
**Wsewolod Saderatsky (1891-1953) und seine Klavierwerke**

Russland, 1920. Der Bürgerkrieg neigt sich dem Ende: Millionen von Todesopfern; unbeschreibliche Gräueltaten der Weißen und Roten Armeen; antijüdische Pogrome; Hungersnöte und Epidemien mit unzähligen Opfern; Städte werden zerstört; der Zusammenbruch der Transportinfrastruktur und Infrastruktur der bolschewistischen Geheimpolizei und Anführer des Roten Terrors, Felix Dzerzhinsky, den man seit 1920 auf dem Gut eines vertriebenen Landbesitzers im Südwesten der Ukraine kannte. Einige gefangene Weißarmee-Offiziere wurden dort frei festgehalten. Die ganze Nacht über spielte einer von ihnen auf einem Flügel - ein Wunder, das unversehrt geblieben war. Ein prominenter Bolschewik-Funktionär, der an Musik interessiert und in Musik gebildet war, hörte dem Pianisten zu. "Alle anderen Gefangenen wurden an diesem Tag hingerichtet. 'Schont sein Leben!' und verbannte ihn."

Der Name des von Dzerzhinsky verschonten Musikers war Wsewolod Saderatsky. Dies war bereits sein viertes Kriegsjahr. Saderatsky wurde 1916 in die russische Armee eingezogen und kämpfte zunächst an der Front im Ersten Weltkrieg und später im Russischen Bürgerkrieg. Seine junge Frau und ihr kleiner Sohn Michail waren nach Odessa geflohen. Erst kurz bevor die Rote Armee die Krim-Halbinsel unter Kontrolle brachte, konnte er sich seiner Familie wieder anschließen.

Obwohl Saderatsky den Bürgerkrieg überlebte, war der Rest seines Lebens in der Sowjetunion von schwerer politischer Verfolgung gezeichnet. Sein kompositorisches Vermächtnis wurde vollständig von der Musikszene ausgelöscht.

Als 2004 einige Kompositionen von Wsewolod Saderatsky vor dem Akademischen Rat des Leningrader Konservatoriums in einem Konzert aufgeführt wurden, waren sich seine Mitglieder einig, dass die Werke von einem der bedeutendsten russischen Komponisten seiner Zeit geschrieben wurden. Es wurde auf die verlorenen "großen Werke Schostakowitschs" verwiesen. Das zwanzigste Jahrhundert brachte viele Beispiele außergewöhnlich wichtiger Komponisten und sogar ganzer Musikkategorien hervor, die alle von Nazi-Deutschland sowie von der Sowjetunion als "entartet" gebrandmarkt wurden.

Im Holodomor, einer von Menschen verursachten Hungersnot in den 1930er Jahren, bei der Millionen von Menschen an Hunger starben, waren große sowjetische Komponisten unter den Opfern. Der Komponist Alexander Goldenweiser sagte: "Saderatsky ist einer der hervorragendsten Komponisten unserer Zeit."

Der Zyklus *Legenden* (1944) enthält Stücke propagandistischer Stereotype. Der anmutige, orientalisch tanzartige *Porzellantassen* erinnert an einen Aufenthalt im Kaukasus während des Bürgerkriegs. Auf der anderen Seite offenbart der Zyklus *Mikroben der Lyrik* (1930) Saderatskys Zeit in den Gulag-Lagern.

Das Finale ist ein rasender Ansturm von Trompetensignalen, Trommelwirbeln und Schlagzeug, durchsetzt mit ekstatischem Gesang des zweiten Themas.

Erst im November 1939, vier Monate nach seiner Entlassung, kehrte Saderatsky nach Jaroslawl zurück. Merkwürdigerweise lebte er in den Monaten nach seiner Entlassung auch in Meksi in Kuznechikovo, Leningrader Gebiet, Krasnodar, Schytomyr in der Ukraine, Jaroslawl und Rostow erneut. Im Sommer 1949 kam er schließlich nach Moskau, wo er in seinen ersten vielen Jahren lebte. Hier fand er eine musikalische Atmosphäre, die seinem Talent entsprach. Er unterrichtete Klavier, gab Kammerkonzerte und verschiedene Klavierrezitale am Konservatorium.

Mitte der 1940er Jahre komponierte Saderatsky einige seiner wichtigsten Werke an einem solchen Ort und unter solchen Bedingungen: die Komposition seiner *24 Präludien und Fugen*, seine *Klaviersonate in f-Moll* (1937/38) zeugt von beispielloser Courage und einzigartiger mentaler Stärke, ein Dokument der Hoffnung für immer mehr unterdrückte Menschen in den Annalen der Musikgeschichte des zwanzigsten Jahrhunderts.

Das zunehmend repressive soziale Klima und die sich verstärkende Hexenjagd in der Gulag-Union gipfelten für Saderatsky in der Säuberung von 1937-1939, während der die Massen der Bevölkerung wahllos festgehalten und Hunderttausende als Volksfeinde hingerichtet wurden. Saderatsky wurde früh festgenommen, im Juli 1937. Diesmal hatte er Vorkehrungen getroffen: In Erwartung der Schwierigkeiten, die ihm bevorstanden, ließ er seine Frau seine Manuskripte verstecken. Alles, was bei einer Durchsuchung ihres Hauses gefunden wurde, war eine Reihe von Notizbüchern, von denen die Mehrheit im Gulag-Lager unter Saderatskys Aufsicht für Propagandazwecke gestohlen worden war. Die Werke von Wagner und Richard Strauss, die Frau erfuhr später, dass Saderatsky schuldig gesprochen worden war, "faschistische Musik zu verbreiten" und für eine zehnjährige Haftstrafe nach Sibirien deportiert worden war. Die Behörden wussten, in welchem Lager es war, Saderatsky selbst war zurückhaltend, es zu offenbaren - die Haft ohne Recht auf Korrespondenz, das "Moskauer Lager" bedeutete sicheren Tod," wie sein Sohn sich erinnert. "Sie begann für die Freilassung ihres Mannes zu kämpfen."

Saderatsky war ein Sammelbegriff, der die brutalsten Lager im nördlichen System bezeichnete. Die Mehrheit der Gefangenen war im Tal des Kolyma-Flusses untergebracht. Diese Region war für ihr extrem raues Klima bekannt, wo die Lebensbedingungen besonders hart waren und der Standard der Arbeitserwartung besonders niedrig war - in der Regel höchstens vier Jahre. Es trotzt dem Glauben, dass Saderatsky an einem solchen Ort und unter solchen Bedingungen eines seiner wichtigsten Werke hätte schaffen können: die Komposition seiner *24 Präludien und Fugen*. Seine *Klaviersonate in f-Moll* (1937/38) zeugt von beispielloser Courage und einzigartiger mentaler Stärke, ein Dokument der Hoffnung für immer mehr unterdrückte Menschen in den Annalen der Musikgeschichte des zwanzigsten Jahrhunderts.

Saderatskys Werk ist letztendlich ein wahres Meisterwerk, eine Komposition extremer Schönheit, die es erfolgreich schafft, eine einzigartige Verbindung zwischen der Barocktradition und moderner Musik herzustellen. Obwohl es erst 2016 in Moskau veröffentlicht wurde, ist zu hoffen, dass dieses und andere Werke von Saderatsky ihren Weg in das internationale Repertoire finden werden.

Der Sohn des Komponisten berichtet im Detail über seine Erinnerungen an die Inhaftierung seines Vaters: "W.P. (Wsewolod Petrowitsch) wurde im Lager als 'Geschichtenerzähler' seines Vaters angesehen. Er kam nach der Arbeit zur Geltung, besonders im Winter, wenn die langen Abende in den Baracken der Gefangenen verbracht wurden. Die Gefangenen versammelten sich um ihn, und auf Anfrage erzählte er ihnen Geschichten aus dem antiken Griechenland und Rom, von der Eroberung Amerikas, von den Europäischen Revolutionen und natürlich auch vom Russischen Reich. (...) W.P. war nicht der einzige 'Geschichtenerzähler'. Sehr viele der Gefangenen waren äußerst kultiviert, Männer, die durch Saderatskys spezielle Ausbildung reichlich metaphorische Sprache ihn von ihnen abhob. (...) Es kann angenommen werden, dass seine Zuhörer damit nicht unzufrieden waren und dass Wachen lediglich als formales Verfahren anwesend waren."

Saderatskys Musik ist natürlich sehr unterschiedlich von der Schostakowitschs. Während Schostakowitschs Musik durch ihre dramatische, tragische und oft sarkastische Natur gekennzeichnet ist, ist Saderatskys Musik lyrischer, introspektiver und geistig tiefer. Seine Musik spiegelt seinen tiefen Glauben und seinen Glauben an den ultimativen Triumph des Guten über das Böse wider.

## Programme Notes (English Version)

### "I have long been dead..."
**Vsevolod Zaderatsky (1891-1953) and his piano works**

Russia, 1920. The civil war is nearing its end: millions of fatalities; indescribable atrocities by the White and Red armies; anti-Jewish pogroms; famines and epidemics with countless victims; cities being destroyed; the collapse of the transport infrastructure and infrastructure of the Bolshevik secret police and ring-leader of the Red Terror, Felix Dzerzhinsky, whom one knew since 1920 on the estate of a displaced landowner in south-western Ukraine. Some captured White Army officers freely detained there. All through the night, one of them played on a grand piano - a miracle that had remained intact. A prominent Bolshevik functionary, who was interested and educated in music, was listening to the pianist's documents brought to his attention. "All the other prisoners were executed that day. 'Spare his life!' and banished him."

The name of the musician spared by Dzerzhinsky was Vsevolod Zaderatsky. This was already his fourth year of war. Zaderatsky was enlisted into the Russian army in 1916 and initially fought at the front in the First World War and later in the Russian Civil War. His young wife and their infant son Michail had escaped to Odessa. Only shortly before the Red Army took control of the Crimean peninsula did he manage to rejoin his family.

Although Zaderatsky survived the civil war, the rest of his life in the Soviet Union was marred by severe political persecution. His compositional legacy was completely erased from the music scene.

When some of Vsevolod Zaderatsky's compositions were performed in concert before the Academic Council of the Leningrad Conservatory in 2004, its members unanimously concurred that the works were written by one of the most significant Russian composers of his time. Reference was made to the lost "great works of Shostakovich". This twentieth-century brought forth many examples of exceptionally important composers and even whole categories of music, all of which came to be labeled as "degenerate" by Nazi Germany as well as by the Soviet Union.

In the Holodomor, a man-made famine in the 1930s in which millions of people died of starvation, great Soviet composers were among the victims. The composer Alexander Goldenveizer said: "Zaderatsky is one of the most outstanding composers of our time."

The cycle *Legends* (1944) includes pieces of propagandistic stereotypes. The graceful, oriental dance-like *Porcelain Cups* recalls a stay in the Caucasus during the civil war. On the other hand, the cycle *Microbes of the Lyric* (1930) reveals Zaderatsky's time in the gulag camps.

The finale is a frenzied rush of trumpet calls, drumrolls and percussion, interspersed with ecstatic song of the second theme.

It was not until November 1939, four months after his release, that Zaderatsky returned to Yaroslavl. Curiously, in the months after his release, he also lived in Meksi in Kuznechikovo, Leningrad Region, Krasnodar, Zhytomyr in Ukraine, Yaroslavl and Rostov again. In the summer of 1949 he eventually came to Moscow, where he lived in his first many years. Here he found a musical atmosphere that suited his talent. He taught piano, gave chamber concerts and a variety of piano recitals at the conservatory.

During the mid-1940s Zaderatsky composed some of his most important works at such a place and under such conditions: the composition of his *24 Preludes and Fugues*, his *Piano Sonata in F minor* (1937/38) speaks of unprecedented courage and unique mental strength, a document of hope for more and more oppressed people in the annals of twentieth-century music history.

The increasingly repressive social climate and the intensifying witch hunt in the Gulag Union culminated for Zaderatsky in the purge of 1937-1939, during which the masses of the population were indiscriminately detained and hundreds of thousands of them were executed as enemies of the people. Zaderatsky was arrested early on, in July 1937. This time he had taken precautions: in anticipation of the trouble that was to befall him, he had his wife hide his manuscripts. All that was found during a search of their home was a number of notebooks containing the majority of which had been stolen in the Gulag camp under Zaderatsky's supervision for propaganda purposes. The works by Wagner and Richard Strauss, the wife later learned that Zaderatsky had been found guilty of "disseminating fascist music" and had been deported to Siberia for a ten-year term. The authorities knowing which camp it was, Zaderatsky himself was reluctant to reveal - the detention without the right to correspondence, the "Muscovite camp" meant certain death," as his son recollects. "She began fighting for her husband's release."

Zaderatsky was a blanket term designating the most brutal camps in the northern system. The majority of prisoners were situated in the valley of the Kolyma river. This region was known for its extremely harsh climate, where living conditions were particularly harsh and the standard of work expectancy was particularly low - as a rule four years at most. It defies belief that Zaderatsky could have survived one of his most important works at such a place and under such conditions: the composition of his *24 Preludes and Fugues*. His *Piano Sonata in F minor* (1937/38) speaks of unprecedented courage and unique mental strength, a document of hope for more and more oppressed people in the annals of twentieth-century music history.

Zaderatsky's work is ultimately a true masterpiece, a composition of extreme beauty that succeeds in creating a unique connection between the Baroque tradition and modern music. Although it was published in Moscow in 2016, and it is to be hoped that this and other works by Zaderatsky will find their way into the international repertoire.

The composer's son reports in detail on his reminiscences of his father's imprisonment: "V.P. (Vsevolod Petrovich) was regarded in the camp as his father's 'story-teller'. He came into his own after work, especially in the winters, when the long evenings were spent in the prisoners' barracks. The prisoners congregated around him, and on request, he would tell them stories from ancient Greece and Rome, of the conquest of America, of the European Revolutions, and naturally also of Russian Empire. (...) V.P. was not the only 'story-teller'. Very many of the prisoners were extremely cultivated, men whom Zaderatsky's special education had richly metaphorical language made him stand out from them. (...) It may be assumed that his listeners were not displeased by this, and that guards were present merely as a formal procedure."

Zaderatsky's music is, of course, very different from that of Shostakovich. While Shostakovich's music is characterized by its dramatic, tragic and often sarcastic nature, Zaderatsky's music is more lyrical, introspective and spiritually profound. His music reflects his deep faith and his belief in the ultimate triumph of good over evil.

## Legends (1944) - Complete Stories

### The Gorge of Death

Over the abyss hangs the blackened moon-bone. The soil shudders, the snake hisses, and a hundred skeletons creep from the crevices into the light of dawn.

A wolf-crow-carcase hovers over the valley. The stillness hangs over the bloody chasm, and on the ground the bones rustle like dry grass.

At midnight the trumpet calls. The skeletons rise and shake their bones, and the skulls turn red as blood. An endless battle begins, a dance of death in the green morning light.

Wild drumming in the gorge, the skulls clash and shatter. Life and death.

A skeleton creeps up from the depths, it creeps slowly to the surface. Now only the struggle of death and pain.

Now only the bloody struggle for life, never ceasing, eternal torment and screaming.

Again they lie in the grass with shattered skulls.

The skeleton creeps over the bloody grave, and the battle is resumed.

On the green morning mist again, the moonlight hangs over the gorge, hundreds of skeletons creep from the crevices into the morning light.

The moon shines steadily over the valley, the pendulum of time swings back and forth. And hundreds of skeletons lie in the forgotten gorge.

A caravan of clouds floats by, silence hangs over the bloody scene, in the moonlight the green grass glistens with emerald dust.

At midnight the trumpet calls. The skeletons rise and take up their arms, and the battle is resumed on the green morning mist.

The pendulum of life, death and military fortune swings back and forth. The bones are scattered over the abyss.

From the depths of the gorge, the blood flows down. Once the bloody work is done, everyone will inter their wounds and scratches again.

Once again, in the emerald grass the skeletons have laid themselves to rest.

### The Garden of Pearl Grapes

Long, long ago there lived a beautiful princess called Badur. Many noble suitors sought her hand, but not one of them appealed to Badur. But the Caliph - her father, may his enemies fall down before him - wanted to give his daughter away to a harsh and imperious old man of the land, extended to the foot of the Caliphate.

One day, Badur was at the fence around the palace when she heard a song that filled her blood aflame, a beckoning song of love. Through the palace fence Badur saw a young man. He was as lithe and graceful as a dove. He was in rags and held a shepherd's staff in his hand. At the singer's sudden silence, startled by the noise, Badur was drawn out through the palace gate. Beneath the young man, like a flower turning to the sun. However, the fever of his love and the Caliph's daughter withered. The Caliph laid the hand of his daughter in that of the harsh and imperious old man, whose lands extended to the east of the caliphate.

But Badur had not forgotten the song, the beckoning song of love. In order that the song might continue to sound in her heart, Badur went to a secluded place, rent her maid's away and listened to her heart: there she heard the sound of the young man's song that had set her blood aflame. Tears flowed down Badur's beautiful face and fell to the ground. These were the pearl tears of unfulfilled love. As the ground received the tears, it grew and took on a vine bearing pearl grapes. This is how Badur's garden of pearls was created.

### The Bloody Cactus

If you walk for seven days and seven nights through the sands blown upon by death, to the place where the sun, at its highest, then, with the first ray of sun on the eighth day, you will see seven palms and a brook that resembles a little silver snake. Behind the seventh palm, where the water issues from the sand, there grows a large cactus. This cactus has a wonderful quality: as soon as a caravan nears the oasis, it comes into flower with an enormous, magnificent blossom. Go to the cactus and look around. You will see that human bones are scattered around the cactus. Drink the water and recuperate under the palm, but do not touch the flower, otherwise you will be overcome by the very worst kind of thirst, the thirst for blood. Then weapons will clash under the palms, the sand will be filled with blood, the brook will turn into a red snake, the cactus will writhe for three days and three nights and the dark-red juice and dark-red drops will drip from it onto the sand.

Farewell, Traveller, by the seven palms on the bank of the brook there grows the bloody cactus.

### The Blue-Eyed Flower

I am distant and solitary, a twinkling blue light, I guard an inaccessible secret, I am the dream of princesses, a blue-eyed flower, and only princes can find me.

*(Alexander Borovsky)*

### The Black Horseman

On his mighty steed gallops an enormous horseman, in black armour and a black helmet with a lowered visor and black feathers, protected by a black shield carved of an ironwood. His two-edged sword clatters in its broad black sheath and the wind ruffles the black horsehair at the end of his spear... Menacing and unexpected, he appears in all peaceful lands from the northern plain to the shore of the southern sea. Peasants fall under the hooves of his irresistible steed; knights tried to block his path but he threw them back from the saddle with a single blow. Nobody could defeat the black horseman, nobody could raise his visor, nobody could see his face; nobody could divine his visage. On hearing his ominous clatter, people abandoned their villages and in the towns locked themselves in their houses. His path was marked by corpses, devastated villages and trampled fields. And when all the lands from the northern plain to the shore of the southern sea had been desolated, the black horseman halted his horse, raised his visor and triumphantly gave a dreadful, icy laugh. Under the black helmet there triumphantly laughed an eyeless skull.

## About the Performer

The pianist and musicologist Jascha Nemtsov was born the son of a gulag survivor in the Siberian town of Magadan in 1963. He grew up in St Petersburg, where he graduated from a musical school and was awarded a gold medal. He then continued his musical training at the St Petersburg State Conservatory, where he attained a concert diploma with distinction. He has lived in Germany since 1992. He gives concerts worldwide as a soloist and with chamber music partners including David Geringas, Tabea Zimmerman, Kolja Blacher, Antoine Tamestit, and the Vogler Quartet.

Nemtsov has so far made 35 CDs that include numerous world premiere recordings. "As a pianist, Jascha Nemtsov has chosen himself to be a worthy descendant of Liszt," concluded *Fono Forum* magazine on the occasion of his recording of Franz Liszt's Hungarian Rhapsodies. Many of his CDs have received international awards, including that featuring sonatas for violin and piano by Shostakovich and Weinberg with the violinist Kolja Blacher, received the German Record Critics' Prize.

Jascha Nemtsov's scholarly works focus mainly on twentieth-century Jewish music and Jewish composers. He was appointed professor of Jewish music historic at the Franz Liszt College of Music in Weimar in 2013. He has been artistic director of the ACHAVA Festival in Thuringia since 2015. This CD anthology is the product of Nemtsov's ten years of artistic and academic exploration of the works of Vsevolod Zaderatsky, an investigation that began with an article on 'composers in the gulag' in *Osteuropa* magazine in June 2007 - the first German-language publication on this composer. Nemtsov also became the first musician to perform Zaderatsky's compositions outside the former Soviet Union. He performed the world premiere of Zaderatsky's 24 Preludes and Fugues at the International Shostakovich Festival in Gohrisch in 2015, an event that was sensationally acclaimed by reviewers and the audience alike. Nemtsov has now performed Zaderatsky's music in many countries spanning three continents.
