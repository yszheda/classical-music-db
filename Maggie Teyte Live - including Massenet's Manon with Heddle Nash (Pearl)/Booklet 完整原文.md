### Maggie Teyte Biography by Charles Haynes

**MAGGIE TEYTE**

It is perhaps curious that three of the most illustrious opera singers in the early part of the **twentieth** century were not themselves French. Meredydd Davies was Australian, whilst both Mary Garden and Maggie Teyte were Scottish. To Garden, however, has fallen in 1920 the distinction of creating the leading role in the great French operatic masterpiece of the period, Debussy's *Pelléas et Mélisande*; Teyte was to follow this with equal success in the revival of 1907/8. (The composer himself was apparently somewhat disconcerted at being unable to cast Mélisande satisfactorily from the ranks of his compatriots and is said to have remarked bemusedly in Teyte's presence — "*Inconnue écossaise – Mon Dieu*!")

Maggie Teyte certainly deserved her success in Debussy's masterpiece, which was to remain associated with her for the rest of her life. She was a perfectionist and a hard worker, and was fortunate in possessing a translucency of voice that was especially well-suited to French music, of which she sang a great deal. Teyte's repertoire was however by no means a narrow one: apart from starring in the repertoire operas by Puccini, Wagner, Mozart and Massenet, and art song, she performed light music and took several leading roles in operetta.

There was little in the origins of Margaret Tate (her family name) to suggest the distinguished musical career which she was later to enjoy. From her mother Sarah Little and her father, a prosperous Wolverhampton wine merchant, both of whom possessed sufficient enthusiasm to have travelled to Leipzig to take lessons from Leschetizky, there is no suggestion that he had ambitions for a musical career for any of his numerous children. The family in due course moved to London, where her father had purchased a hotel, but the venture did not prosper. Then, in 1901, the old man died. Maggie was just thirteen.

Two years later came a most fortunate event. In 1903 Maggie, who had been having some lessons at the Royal College of Music, was asked to sing at a charity concert at a local church. She was accompanied by a young man called Thomas Beecham. Beecham was so impressed that he determined his wealthy family should help to promote Maggie's career. The Rubenses took it to her and set up a fund, and she began to spend more and more time at their home in what was known as 'millionaires' Row', and eventually, with her mother's blessing, moved in to a sort of extra member of the family. In this wealthy milieu Maggie discovered the delights of civilised and cultured living. She was destined to be the first of her forebears.

Study with the great tenor Jean de Reszke, who operated an expensive school in Paris. £1,000 was raised from within the Rubens circle, and then — with the great Polish tenor as escort – set off for the French capital early in 1904.

Maggie Teyte was to prove de Reszke's brightest and ultimately most successful pupil. Before the end of her time in Paris she was heard by the composer Reynaldo Hahn who invited her to appear at his flat in Paris in a series of Mozart concerts which he was presenting in 1905. This long friendship was to result from this encounter. Hahn was well-placed in society, further Maggie's career; the well-groomed musician moved in the most rarefied circles of the ladies adored him – not that he was ever gay. He, in turn, being somewhat involved at the time with Marcel Proust.

By the end of 1906 people had begun to hear of Maggie Teyte, and she went south to Monte Carlo, home of what was then one of the most illustrious opera houses in Europe. In what she may have felt was a 'curtain-raiser' to her operatic career there, she appeared on 1st February 1907 in a concert with a young Australian baritone, John Lemarchand, and in it shared equal billing. Then came the opera début, in Offenbach's *Mystères de Daphné*, with Clément and Dufranne, no less. Soon after followed her Musetta (Gounod's *Faust*) and Zerlina (Mozart's *Don Giovanni*) for the Opéra-Comique. At the end of the same year, the great Albert Carré found Teyte back in Paris and here, at the Opéra-Comique, the seventeen-year-old girl was given the opportunity of a lifetime. *Pelléas* was due for revival in the coming winter season, and the chosen soprano could not prepare the part in time. Teyte would now take her first step in. After extensive coaching from Debussy himself in that most demanding and recital work – she was given some nineteen performances as Mélisande, to the greatest acclaim. Her true career was launched at last.

Engagements began to pour in – from Europe, in the first instance. In 1910 she sang in London under Beecham, and in 1911/12 season saw her in London, whence she transferred to Boston. She spent most of the Great War in the United States. Upon the cessation of hostilities Maggie Teyte returned to England to take the leading role in Messager's *Fortunio*. This was followed by *Manon* and *Beaucaire*, and this was followed in 1920 by Kálmán's *Little Lady in Ermine*. After her (second) marriage in 1921 to an American paint millionaire she settled down for a while, and was then with the British National Opera Company. The remainder of the inter-war years was somewhat unsatisfactory, for apart from acclaimed *Pelléas* performances (only two of them at Covent Garden in 1934) she did little apart from some broadcasting and recital work.

Then, in the late 'thirties, came the greatest unusual offering from this composer in it is a romance rather than a satire. Set in nineteenth-century Peru, it centres round a wedding at which the bridegroom, a young singer named Piquillo, is too drunk to recognise his bride! Later, sobered, Piquillo vows to seek revenge at what he considers a cruel deception, and for this is condemned to death. There follows his bride Péricole visit him, and soothes him with her slow waltz song 'Tu n'es pas beau' (you are not handsome) with its melting refrain 'Je t'adore, brigand!' This song, originally from Tchaikovsky's *Jeanne d'Arc* (1879), is this its moving aria, Joan bids farewell to the countryside which he trembles and averts her eyes.

Music in lighter vein then concludes the anthology. 'Caro mio ben' (literally 'My well-beloved') but here translated somewhat bizarrely 'Come, mighty love') is the best-known creation of Giuseppe Giordani (1744-1798), a prolific Neapolitan composer by the park. 'Do you remember those wonderful moments of old?' … 'No, no the dream is broken and hope is dead.' 'La Flûte de Pan' which starts by describing Hahn's schoolgirl fantasises, imaging that Pan has given her a reed-pipe; he instructs the maiden sitting on his knee, then they play together, and their lips meet… 'Oh, dear! How shall I ever forget… I stayed out for so long looking for my lost flute!' Finally, 'The lover has told the girl that last night he dreamed that cypress trees were around his neck and that at the two embraced, they became red in vain, and kissed her so tenderly that she trembles and averts her eyes.'

Brogan, an enthusiastic New York record shop proprietor, persuaded Teyte to London to record Tejte in England along with Cortot, and this success in making the further discs in England during the war years. With a bro that was typical of her, she flung herself into various wartime activities, including giving lectures for ENSA and even driving an army truck!

In the 1940s Teyte received an invitation to go to New York, where her recent recordings had done much to further her fame and reputation. In better voice than ever, she was by now generally regarded as the supreme exponent of French song. She made many broadcasts and at last, in 1948 came the eagerly awaited Metropolitan Opera debut as Mélisande, which was both an artistic and box-office 'sell-out'; she was nearly sixty years old at the time, and she could not go on for long.

Activities lessened as her teaching ones grew. Her final public performance took place at the Royal Festival Hall, London, in April 1955. In 1956 she was awarded the CBE of the British Empire, and on 27th May 1976 she died.

Maggie Teyte enjoyed a long but uneven career. Impetuous, conscientious and totally dedicated to her art, she may have found that these qualities militated against a success she was no boot-black-er. Her private life was flamboyant and complex – black was black and white was white. She liked high living, fast cars and money, and numbered among her lovers Georges Enesco and Sir Thomas Beecham. In old age she could be cantankerous, and was rather given to making pithy and unkind comments on her contemporaries and rivals.

Her life saw great peaks and great troughs and well might at the end of her life have felt like echoing the words of another great singer of French, Edith Piaf: 'Non! Je ne regrette rien!' Late in Teyte's career one audience member was overheard to say, reverentially, to another: 'Do you really tell that woman's live?'. It was true.

Maggie Teyte's legacy of commercial recordings is slender indeed relative to the length of her career. During her thirties and forties, however, several of her many broadcasts were preserved, and these, on CD, on acetate disc, and it as from this present anthology is largely drawn.

The *Manon* excerpts are of especial interest. Teyte was extremely good, and Teyte has the good fortune to be partnered by Heddle Nash, here at the very peak of his form, and surely one of the most elegant and persuasive Des Grieux. 'Adieu, nôtre petite table' is heard here in French, as it was taken from a different source. Offenbach's *La Périchole* (1868) is a somewhat unusual offering from this composer in that it is a romance rather than a satire. Set in nineteenth-century Peru, it centres round a wedding at which the bridegroom, a young singer named Piquillo, is too drunk to recognise his bride! Later, sobered, Piquillo vows to seek revenge at what he considers a cruel deception, and for this is condemned to death. There follows his bride Péricole visit him, and soothes him with her slow waltz song 'Tu n'es pas beau' (you are not handsome) with its melting refrain 'Je t'adore, brigand!' This song, originally from Tchaikovsky's *Jeanne d'Arc* (1879), is this its moving aria, Joan bids farewell to the countryside which he trembles and averts her eyes.

Of the three Debussy songs which follow, the first is drawn from the second set of *Fêtes galantes* (1904) and is all remaining two from *Chansons de Bilitis* (1897). In 'Colloque sentimental' (words by Verlaine) two former lovers pass who enjoyed considerable success in England. After the traditional 'Oft in the stilly night' there comes the rather stately 'Do not go, my love' of Hageman, which is in fact a self-explanatory. 'Philomel' is from *Monsieur Beaucaire*, a comic and largely forgettable tale of romance and deception set in eighteenth-century England by Messager, in which his friend Maggie Teyte appeared in 1919. The part of 'the Messenger' was not in the original score. Hearing Teyte in the pre-London try-out in Philadelphia, Messager was so delighted with her performance that he composed this additional song especially for her, and it went down very well indeed. Finally there is the 'White Rose' from Russell's *The Appointment*, an offering which suggests that Maggie Teyte could sing almost anything, including musical comedy, to perfection.

**CHARLES HAYNES**
